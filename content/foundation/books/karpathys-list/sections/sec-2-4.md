**Andrej Karpathy · 2022 · [karpathy.github.io/lecun1989](https://karpathy.github.io/2022/03/14/lecun1989/)**

LeCun et al.'s 1989 zip-code paper is plausibly the first neural network trained end to end with backpropagation and then actually deployed on a real task. Karpathy reimplements it in PyTorch, matches it, then applies thirty-three years of progress **one change at a time**, measuring each. The result is the cleanest available answer to a question the field asks constantly and almost never tests: **which of our improvements actually did the work?**

The original is small in a way worth stating precisely. `9,760` parameters, `64K` multiply-accumulates per forward pass, `7,291` training digits, `23` epochs, **three days** on a SUN-4/260. Test error `5.00%`. A modern laptop does that forward pass a hundred million times a second.

The reproduction lands at `4.09%` test error — close enough that the reimplementation is faithful, with the small gap attributable to details the paper never fully specifies. Then the modernisations, each measured on the same data:

- **Cross-entropy instead of squared error** as the loss, and dropping the output squashing function: test error `4.38%` — *worse* — while training error goes to `0.00%`. Read that pair together. The model now memorises the training set perfectly and does slightly worse on new digits, which is progress of a kind: it means capacity has stopped being the thing holding it back, and the bottleneck has moved.
- **AdamW instead of plain gradient descent**, with a tuned learning rate: `3.59%`.
- **Data augmentation** — shifting each image by a pixel to manufacture new examples, sixty epochs: `2.19%`. This is the single largest jump in the sequence.
- **Dropout at `0.25` and ReLU** in place of the old squashing function: `1.59%`.
- **The dataset scaled to `50K` examples**, everything else held fixed: `1.25%`.

So thirty-three years of algorithmic progress takes `4.09%` to `1.59%` — and then simply having more of the same data takes it to `1.25%` with no algorithmic change whatsoever. Karpathy's conclusion is blunt: "Simply scaling up the dataset in 1989 would have been an effective way to drive up the performance of the system, at no cost to inference latency." The 1989 team could not have known that, because neither the compute to exploit a larger dataset nor the dataset itself existed. But the *ordering of the levers* is the finding, and it is the same ordering [A Recipe for Training Neural Networks](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-2-2) gives as advice and the [scaling papers](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-3) later give as a power law.

The extrapolation half of the post is the part people quote. Run the argument forward: the networks of 2055 are the same networks, roughly a million times larger, trained on far more data, and the 2055 reader will find our models as quaint as we find a `9,760`-parameter convolutional net. It is a deliberately unexciting prediction, and it has held for the four years since better than most exciting ones.

> Thirty-three years of algorithmic improvement cut the error by roughly sixty per cent; more of the same data, with no algorithmic change at all, cut a further fifth off that.

**What to be careful with:** the experiment measures one small convolutional network on one small dataset, and generalising from it to "algorithms barely matter" is a misreading the post does not commit but is often used to support. Two of the five modernisations — the augmentation and the dropout — are data-and-regularisation moves rather than architectural ones, which is precisely why they win on a `7,291`-example problem, and there is no reason to expect that ranking to hold at $10^{12}$ tokens. The genuinely architectural change of the intervening period, attention, is absent from the comparison entirely, because a digit classifier has no use for it. The honest summary is narrower than the popular one: **at small scale, on a task where the model already fits, data and regularisation dominate.** What the post establishes beyond that is a method — reproduce the old result exactly, then change one thing at a time and report every number, including the change that made things worse.
