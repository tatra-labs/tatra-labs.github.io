**Alexander Wei, Nika Haghtalab & Jacob Steinhardt · 2023 · [arXiv:2307.02483](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02483)**

Jailbreaks were, until this paper, a folk practice. People traded prompts on forums — the grandmother story, the fictional-scenario wrapper, DAN — and each one was patched individually, which produced a treadmill and no understanding. This paper's contribution is to explain *why* the class exists, and it does so with two failure modes that between them account for nearly everything.

**Competing objectives.** A deployed model has been trained on at least three things that do not agree: language modelling (continue the text plausibly), instruction following (do what the user asked), and safety (refuse certain requests). Ordinarily these coincide. An attack works by constructing a prompt where they cannot, so that satisfying one requires violating another. **Prefix injection** asks the model to begin its reply with "Absolutely! Here's" — after which the language-modelling objective, which has an overwhelming prior that a sentence beginning that way continues helpfully, is pulling against the refusal. **Refusal suppression** instructs the model never to use the words "cannot", "unable" or "sorry", removing the vocabulary a refusal is built from. Neither disguises the request. Both simply make refusing locally improbable.

**Mismatched generalization.** Pretraining covers an enormous domain; safety training covers a much smaller one. Wherever capability generalises and safety does not, there is an opening. The model learned Base64 from pretraining, and nobody wrote refusal demonstrations in Base64, so a Base64-encoded request is decoded by a capability that safety training never reached. The same holds for ROT13, leetspeak, low-resource languages, and any encoding the model happens to have picked up. The request is not hidden — the model understands it perfectly. The refusal behaviour simply was not trained in that region.

Composing the two produced attacks that worked broadly against GPT-4 and Claude v1.3, including a combination attack that stacked several mechanisms at once.

**The conclusion is the part that has held up, and it is uncomfortable: scaling makes the second failure mode worse.** A larger model is fluent in more encodings, more languages and more formats, so the surface where capability outruns safety coverage grows with capability. The authors argue for **safety-capability parity** — that safety mechanisms must be as sophisticated as the model they constrain — and observe that a safety approach based on enumerating and training away specific behaviours is structurally behind a system whose capabilities are increasing.

> A jailbreak is not a trick that fools the model — it is a region where the model's abilities extend past where its refusals were trained, or a construction where obeying the user and obeying the safety policy cannot both happen.

**What did not survive:** every specific attack in the paper, which is what one would expect and does not touch the argument. Base64 and ROT13 requests are handled by current frontier models, low-resource-language attacks are much weaker, and prefix injection is directly trained against. What survived is the taxonomy, which remains the right way to reason about any new jailbreak, and the safety-capability parity thesis, which the intervening years support. The field's response has been layered rather than fundamental — classifier guardrails on input and output, constitutional methods, adversarial training against generated attacks, deliberative approaches where the model reasons about the policy before answering — and each layer raises cost without closing the gap the paper identifies. Notably, none of them provides the kind of guarantee that would make the [optimisation-based attacks](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-7-2) in the next entry stop working.
