**Silver, Huang, Maddison, Guez, Sifre, van den Driessche, Schrittwieser, Antonoglou, Panneershelvam, Lanctot et al. · 2016 · [Nature 529, 484-489](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961)**

Karpathy uses this paper to name something a language model does not have. Kahneman's split is the frame: System 1 is fast, automatic, associative; System 2 is slow, effortful, deliberate.

**A transformer's forward pass is System 1 and only System 1.** It spends *exactly the same computation on every token*, whether the next word is obvious or requires a proof. There is no dial marked "think harder about this one". AlphaGo has the dial, and this paper is the clearest demonstration of what the dial buys.

The system has four networks, and the division of labour is the point. A **supervised policy network** trained on `30M` expert positions predicts the move a strong human would play, reaching `57%` accuracy. A **fast rollout policy** does the same job far worse — `24.2%` — but in about two microseconds rather than three milliseconds, and that ratio is what makes it useful for a completely different purpose. A **reinforcement-learning policy network** improves the supervised one by playing against itself, winning around `80%` against its own teacher. A **value network**, trained on self-play positions, estimates who is winning straight from a board position, using only one position per game so that it cannot memorise closely related states.

Then search binds them together. Monte Carlo tree search walks down the tree choosing the move that maximises `Q(s,a) + u(s,a)` — where `Q` is the value accumulated for that move so far, and `u` is a bonus that starts high for moves the policy likes and shrinks each time a move is tried. **So the policy network says where to look first, and the search decides whether it was right.** A leaf position is evaluated two ways and the results mixed: the value network's estimate, and the outcome of actually playing the game out quickly with the cheap policy. Results are carried back up the tree, and the loop repeats until the clock runs out.

**That last clause is the entire lesson.** The policy network alone plays strong amateur Go. The same network, given thinking time, beat Fan Hui `5-0` and Lee Sedol `4-1`, and won `99.8%` of games against other Go programs. Nothing was added to the network. Time was converted into accuracy by an explicit search over possible futures, using the network as a suggestion rather than as an answer.

For a language model in 2023 there was no equivalent. The model answers in one pass, at one cost, and if the first token is wrong the rest of the response is a well-written elaboration of a mistake. Every entry that follows in this chapter is an attempt to build the dial.

> A policy gives you an intuition and a search converts wall-clock time into accuracy; a transformer's forward pass has the first and no mechanism at all for the second.

**What did not survive:** the transplant. The obvious move — bolt tree search onto a language model — was tried extensively and mostly failed, for reasons that are structural rather than incidental. Go has about `250` legal moves per turn and text has as many as there are tokens in the vocabulary. Go has a terminal state and a definite winner; an essay has neither. And the value network works because board positions are cheap to evaluate and self-play generates unlimited labelled outcomes, none of which holds for open-ended text.

What actually worked, arriving in 2024 and 2025, was to **internalise the search into the policy**: train with reinforcement learning on long chains of reasoning against rewards that can be checked automatically, until the model learns to backtrack, verify and revise within a single linear generation. The dial exists now, and it is measured in output tokens rather than tree nodes. The insight this paper contributed — that search over a learned prior beats the prior alone, and that the exchange rate is time for quality — survived completely. The algorithm did not.
