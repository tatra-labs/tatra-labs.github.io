**Andrej Karpathy · 2022 · [github.com/karpathy/nn-zero-to-hero](https://github.com/karpathy/nn-zero-to-hero)**

Every deep-learning course begins by importing something. **The import is the problem.** `loss.backward()` is one call, it works, and it leaves you with no model of what just happened — which is fine right up until the gradients come out zero and there is nowhere to look. Karpathy's course begins one level below the import, and the whole of lecture one is spent building the thing that call belongs to.

That thing is **micrograd**: an autograd engine in about a hundred lines of Python, with a fifty-line neural-network library on top. An *autograd engine* is the machinery that works out, automatically, how much each number in your model contributed to the final error — the step every framework hides.

The design decision that makes it teachable is that it is **scalar-valued**. Not tensors — plain numbers, one at a time. A `Value` wraps one float and records four things: its `data`, a `grad` starting at zero, the tuple of `Value`s it came from, and a small function `_backward` that knows the derivative of the single operation that produced it.

Concretely: adding two `Value`s returns a third whose `_backward` adds `1.0 * out.grad` into each input, because if you nudge either addend by one, the sum moves by one. Multiplying returns one whose `_backward` sends `other.data * out.grad` the other way, because nudging one factor moves the product by the size of the other. **That is the chain rule, written out one operation at a time, with nothing vectorised on top of it.**

Calling `.backward()` on the final number then does exactly two things: sort the graph of operations so that nothing is visited before the things that depend on it, then walk that ordering in reverse calling each `_backward` in turn. Seed the output gradient at `1.0`, and every derivative in the network falls out.

It is worth being precise about how little machinery this is, because the framework version looks like infrastructure and this looks like a homework exercise. **They compute the same quantity.** PyTorch's advantage is that it batches the operation into a fast kernel. It is not that it knows something micrograd does not.

The pedagogical payoff arrives when the `+=` shows up. A `Value` used twice — the same activation feeding two different downstream units — has to *accumulate* gradient from both paths rather than overwrite one with the other. Get that wrong and the network still trains, just worse. That is the exact failure mode nobody ever finds by reading a stack trace, and building the engine puts the rule somewhere you cannot skip past it.

On top sits `nn.py`: `Neuron`, `Layer`, `MLP`, given a PyTorch-shaped API on purpose so the transfer is free. The demo trains a small network to separate two interleaved crescents of points. Eight lectures follow, and the arc is deliberate — micrograd, then character-level language modelling in [makemore](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-3), then [a GPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-4), then [a tokenizer](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-5). By the end you have written every component of a language model at least once.

> Backpropagation is not a library feature. It is a topological sort and a hundred lines of local derivatives, and writing it once makes every subsequent gradient bug legible.

**What the format costs.** Twenty hours of video is a real price, and video is a bad medium for reference — there is no way to grep a lecture. The notebooks in the repository are the durable artifact and the videos are the tutorial; treat them that way. The scalar design also has a hard ceiling the course never quite names: micrograd cannot train anything real, because a Python object per number is several orders of magnitude too slow, and the leap to tensors is handled by simply starting to use PyTorch in lecture two. That leap — from one closure per number to batched kernels — is where a great deal of practical confusion actually lives, and it is the one step in the whole sequence that is asserted rather than built. [microgpt](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-6) later closes the loop by carrying the scalar engine all the way to a working GPT, at a speed that makes the point about kernels better than any explanation would.
