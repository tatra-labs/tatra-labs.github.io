**Andrej Karpathy · 2022 · [github.com/karpathy/nn-zero-to-hero](https://github.com/karpathy/nn-zero-to-hero)**

Every deep-learning course begins by importing something. The import is the problem. `loss.backward()` is one call, it works, and it leaves the student with no model of what happened — which is fine until the gradients are zero and there is nowhere to look. Karpathy's course begins one level below the import, and the whole of lecture one is spent building the thing that call belongs to.

That thing is **micrograd**: an autograd engine in about a hundred lines of Python, with a fifty-line neural-network library on top of it. The design decision that makes it teachable is that it is **scalar-valued**. Not tensors — numbers. A `Value` wraps one float and records four things: `data`, a `grad` initialised to zero, the tuple of `Value`s it came from, and a closure `_backward` that knows the local derivative of the operation that produced it. Adding two `Value`s returns a third whose `_backward` adds `1.0 * out.grad` into each input; multiplying returns one whose `_backward` routes `other.data * out.grad` the other way. That is the chain rule, written out per operation, with nothing vectorised over it.

Calling `.backward()` on the final node does exactly two things: build a topological ordering of the graph by depth-first search, then walk it in reverse calling each `_backward` in turn. Seed the output gradient at `1.0` and the derivatives fall out. It is worth being precise about how little machinery this is, because the framework version looks like infrastructure and this looks like a homework exercise. They compute the same quantity. PyTorch's advantage is that it batches the operation into a kernel; it is not that it knows something micrograd does not.

The pedagogical payoff arrives when the `+=` shows up. A `Value` used twice — the same activation feeding two downstream units — must accumulate gradient from both paths, not overwrite. Get that wrong and the network still trains, just worse, which is the exact failure mode nobody ever finds by reading a stack trace. Building the engine puts the multivariate chain rule somewhere you cannot skip it.

On top sits `nn.py`: `Neuron`, `Layer`, `MLP`, with a PyTorch-shaped API on purpose so that the transfer is free. The demo trains a small MLP to separate the two-moons dataset. Eight lectures follow, and the arc is deliberate — micrograd, then character-level language modelling in [makemore](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-3), then [a GPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-4), then [a tokenizer](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-5). By the end you have written every component of a language model at least once.

> Backpropagation is not a library feature. It is a topological sort and a hundred lines of local derivatives, and writing it once makes every subsequent gradient bug legible.

**What the format costs.** Twenty hours of video is a real price, and video is a bad medium for reference — there is no way to grep a lecture. The notebooks in the repository are the durable artifact and the videos are the tutorial; treat them that way. The scalar design also has a hard ceiling that the course never quite names: micrograd cannot train anything, because a Python object per scalar is several orders of magnitude too slow, and the leap to tensors is handled by simply starting to use PyTorch in lecture two. That leap — from per-scalar closures to batched kernels — is where a lot of practical confusion actually lives, and it is the one step in the whole sequence that is asserted rather than built. [microgpt](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-6) later closes the loop by carrying the scalar engine all the way to a working GPT, at a speed that makes the point about kernels better than any explanation would.
