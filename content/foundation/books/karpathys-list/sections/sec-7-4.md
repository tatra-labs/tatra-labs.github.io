**Carlini, Jagielski, Choquette-Choo, Paleka, Pearce, Anderson, Terzis, Thomas & Tramer · 2023 · [arXiv:2302.10149](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.10149)**

Data poisoning had a reputation as a theoretical attack — real in a laboratory, requiring an implausible degree of control over someone else's training set. This paper demonstrates two attacks against **datasets actually in use**, prices them, and finds the number is about sixty dollars.

**Split-view poisoning** exploits how web-scale datasets are distributed. LAION, Conceptual Captions and their relatives cannot ship images for copyright reasons, so they ship **lists of URLs**. The curator downloaded and validated the content at curation time; the consumer downloads it months or years later. Nothing binds the two. If the content at a URL changes in between, the consumer trains on something the curator never saw and nobody ever reviewed. The cheapest way to change it is to buy the domain: a fraction of the domains in any large index have lapsed and are purchasable at registrar prices. The authors found that for around `$60` they could have controlled at least `0.01%` of several popular datasets — and prior work establishes that poison rates well below that suffice to install a backdoor.

**Frontrunning poisoning** targets periodic snapshots. Wikipedia is dumped on a schedule that is public and predictable, and moderation of a given edit takes longer than the window between the edit and the snapshot. An attacker who edits at the right moment gets their version into the dump even though the edit is reverted shortly afterwards — the snapshot is a permanent record of a transient vandalism. Their estimate was that a meaningful fraction of Wikipedia articles could be poisoned this way.

Both attacks were verified for feasibility and responsibly disclosed rather than executed, and both have mitigations that are almost embarrassingly simple. Distribute cryptographic hashes of the content alongside the URLs, so a changed image is detectable. Randomise snapshot timing, so the window cannot be predicted. Several dataset maintainers adopted these.

> A dataset distributed as a list of URLs is not a dataset — it is a promise about what those URLs contained on a day that has passed.

**What has changed since, and it is worse rather than better.** The paper's framing assumes the attacker needs a *fraction* of the training set, which is what made `0.01%` the relevant number and what made scale feel protective — a bigger corpus should dilute a fixed amount of poison. A 2025 study by Anthropic with the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute reported that this intuition is wrong: for the backdoor they tested, the number of poisoned documents needed stayed roughly **constant at around `250`** across models from `600M` to `13B` parameters, even as the clean data grew by orders of magnitude around them. At the largest scale that was on the order of `0.00016%` of the corpus. If the required quantity is absolute rather than proportional, then growing the training set provides no protection at all, and two hundred and fifty documents is a quantity anyone can produce.

The caveat matters and the authors state it themselves: the backdoor tested was narrow and low-stakes — a trigger phrase that makes the model emit gibberish — and it does not follow that more dangerous behaviours are equally cheap to install. But the scaling result is the alarming part regardless of the payload, and it lands on an ecosystem that still, in the main, cannot say what is in its training data. That is the same provenance gap the [Sparks](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-5) entry runs into from the evaluation side: you cannot verify a model is uncontaminated, or unpoisoned, without knowing what it read.
