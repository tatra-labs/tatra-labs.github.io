**Touvron, Martin, Stone, Albert, Almahairi, Babaei, Bashlykov, Batra, Bhargava, Bhosale et al. · 2023 · [arXiv:2307.09288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)**

[InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) described the recipe. Llama 2 is the first paper that describes running it at production scale and reports what actually goes wrong, across seventy-seven pages that read more like an engineering log than a research paper. That is precisely its value, and it is why Karpathy points at it rather than at a better-scoring model: everything else at this level of capability was described in a blog post.

**Pretraining** is unremarkable and stated plainly: `7B`, `13B` and `70B` parameters, `2T` tokens, a `4096`-token context, and grouped-query attention on the largest models — several attention heads sharing one set of keys and values, which keeps the memory cost of generation affordable. The interesting half is everything after.

**The supervised stage found that quality dominates quantity, decisively.** Meta collected millions of third-party instruction-tuning examples, then set them aside in favour of `27,540` annotations they commissioned and vetted themselves. Tens of thousands of good demonstrations beat millions of mediocre ones, and the paper is direct that annotation quality was where the returns were. Anyone budgeting a fine-tuning effort should read that paragraph twice.

**Two reward models, not one.** Helpfulness and safety pull against each other — the most helpful answer to a dangerous question is dangerous — and a single reward model trained on both objectives turned out to be worse at each than a specialist would be. So they trained two and combined them at optimisation time. That is a small architectural decision with a large admission inside it: the field's standard framing of "a reward model" as one number standing for human preference does not survive contact with objectives that genuinely conflict.

**Iterative RLHF, five rounds.** Not one pass but `RLHF-V1` through `V5`, each collecting fresh preference data on the *current* model's outputs — because a reward model trained on last month's policy is measuring a distribution that no longer exists. They also combine two optimisers rather than picking one: **rejection sampling**, which means drawing several samples, keeping whichever scores best, and fine-tuning on it, for the `70B`, with PPO layered on top; the smaller models are then trained on the large model's best samples. **Ghost Attention** is a training-time trick for a specific, mundane failure: models forget the system prompt after a few turns, so during training the instruction is synthetically pasted into every turn and then dropped at inference.

> A reward model trained on your previous policy's outputs is already out of date, so preference optimisation has to be a loop with fresh human data in it, not a stage.

**What did not survive:** the word "open", which the paper uses and the licence does not support. The weights are downloadable; the training data is not described in enough detail to reproduce, the preference data is not released, and the Llama 2 Community Licence is not an open-source licence by the OSI definition — it carries use restrictions and a clause requiring a separate agreement above `700M` monthly active users. Llama 2 is the paper that created "open weights" as a distinct and useful category, and it is worth being precise that this is a third thing, not a synonym for open source. The safety tuning also aged poorly in a specific and well-documented way: Llama 2-Chat refused a great many entirely benign requests, and the paper's own examples of over-refusal became a standing joke. That failure was informative — it showed that optimising safety against a reward model produces a model that has learned to avoid the *shape* of a risky request rather than the risk itself, which is the same proxy problem [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1) describes, arriving from the other direction.
