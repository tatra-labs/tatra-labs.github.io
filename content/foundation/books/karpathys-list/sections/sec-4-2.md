**Touvron, Martin, Stone, Albert, Almahairi, Babaei, Bashlykov, Batra, Bhargava, Bhosale et al. · 2023 · [arXiv:2307.09288](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)**

[InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) described the recipe. Llama 2 is the first paper that describes running it at production scale and reports what actually goes wrong, across seventy-seven pages that read more like an engineering log than a research paper. That is precisely its value, and it is why Karpathy points at it rather than at a better-scoring model: everything else at this level of capability was described in a blog post.

**Pretraining** is unremarkable and stated plainly: `7B`, `13B` and `70B` parameters, `2T` tokens, a `4096` context, grouped-query attention on the largest models to keep the KV cache affordable at inference. The interesting half is everything after.

**The supervised stage found that quality dominates quantity, decisively.** Meta collected millions of third-party instruction-tuning examples, then set them aside in favour of `27,540` annotations they commissioned and vetted themselves. Tens of thousands of good demonstrations beat millions of mediocre ones, and the paper is direct that annotation quality was where the returns were. Anyone budgeting a fine-tuning effort should read that paragraph twice.

**Two reward models, not one.** Helpfulness and safety pull against each other — the most helpful answer to a dangerous question is dangerous — and a single reward model trained on both objectives was worse at each than a specialist. So they trained two and combined them at optimisation time. This is a small architectural decision with a large honest admission inside it: the field's standard framing of "a reward model" as one scalar standing for human preference does not survive contact with objectives that genuinely conflict.

**Iterative RLHF, five rounds.** Not one pass but `RLHF-V1` through `V5`, each collecting fresh preference data on the current model's outputs, because a reward model trained on last month's policy is measuring a distribution that no longer exists. They also combine two optimisers rather than picking one: **rejection sampling** — draw `K` samples, keep the best by reward, fine-tune on it — for the `70B`, with PPO layered on, and the smaller models distilled from the large model's rejection-sampled outputs. **Ghost Attention** is a training-time trick for a specific, mundane failure: models forget the system prompt after a few turns, so the instruction is synthetically pasted into every turn during training and dropped at inference.

> A reward model trained on your previous policy's outputs is already out of date, so preference optimisation has to be a loop with fresh human data in it, not a stage.

**What did not survive:** the word "open", which the paper uses and the licence does not support. The weights are downloadable; the training data is not described in enough detail to reproduce, the preference data is not released, and the Llama 2 Community Licence is not an open-source licence by the OSI definition — it carries use restrictions and a clause requiring a separate agreement above `700M` monthly active users. Llama 2 is the paper that created "open weights" as a distinct and useful category, and it is worth being precise that this is a third thing, not a synonym for open source. The safety tuning also aged poorly in a specific and well-documented way: Llama 2-Chat refused a great many benign requests, and the paper's own examples of over-refusal became a standing joke. That failure was informative — it showed that safety optimisation against a reward model produces a model that has learned to avoid the *shape* of a risky request rather than the risk, which is the same proxy problem [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1) describes, arriving from the other direction.
