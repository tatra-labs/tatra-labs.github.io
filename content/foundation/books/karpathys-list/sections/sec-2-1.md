**Andrej Karpathy · 2015 · [karpathy.github.io/rnn-effectiveness](https://karpathy.github.io/2015/05/21/rnn-effectiveness/)**

The mechanism is covered in [Sutskever's List](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-2-1), where it belongs: a plain recurrent network reading one character at a time, trained only to guess the next one, producing samples from Shakespeare, LaTeX and the Linux kernel that were startling in 2015. Read that entry for what the model does. This one is about what the *post* did, because on a Karpathy list it is not primarily an RNN paper — it is the origin of a method that every other entry in chapters 1 and 2 follows.

**The method is: build the smallest thing that works, run it on something absurd, and show the raw output.** Not a benchmark table. Not an ablation. Four hundred lines of samples, with the good ones and the broken ones both left in.

`min-char-rnn.py` — the accompanying gist, a hundred lines of NumPy with the gradients worked out by hand — is the direct ancestor of [micrograd](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-1), of makemore, and of [microgpt](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-6). The template was set here: a hundred-line reference implementation with no dependencies, published alongside the thing that made the point, so a reader can run it the same evening.

**That template mattered because of the audience it reached.** In 2015 the mainstream understanding of sequence modelling was that structure had to be supplied by hand — a grammar, a parser, a transition matrix. The field's own literature was never going to change that quickly, because a paper reports a perplexity score and a perplexity score persuades nobody who does not already care. Generated Linux kernel source with plausible indentation and mostly-balanced braces persuades everybody. The post is the reason a generation of engineers arrived at language modelling already holding the intuition that **next-symbol prediction is a general-purpose objective and not a stand-in for one** — several years before there was a system that made the case commercially.

It is also where Karpathy's characteristic honesty about failure starts. The LaTeX "almost compiles" and he says so, and shows the patches. The C declares variables it does not use and uses variables it never declared, and he prints those samples too. Every subsequent artifact in this list carries the same habit — the makemore lecture that leaves the shape bugs in, the nanochat README that tells you the model is roughly a kindergartener. It is a small thing that is unusually rare, and it is why the material is trustworthy at all.

> The post's lasting contribution was not the architecture but the demonstration format: smallest working implementation, absurd corpus, raw samples including the broken ones.

**What did not survive:** almost every technical claim, which the Sutskever notes go through in detail — character-level modelling, the plain RNN, and the tidy result that about five per cent of hidden units are individually interpretable, since superseded by the finding that a network packs far more features than it has units and smears each one across many of them.

There is a second thing worth flagging that has aged less well than the code. The title's word is "unreasonable", and the post frames the results as surprising in a way that reads oddly now: the samples are exactly as good as a model of that size trained on that data should be, and the surprise was a fact about the field's expectations rather than about recurrent networks. That framing became a small genre — every capability jump since has been announced as unreasonable, magic or emergent — and the field has spent considerable effort walking those descriptions back into scaling curves. Karpathy's own later writing does the walking back, which is to his credit and worth reading in that order.
