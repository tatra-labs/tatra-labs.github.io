**Andrej Karpathy · 2016 · [Medium](https://karpathy.medium.com/yes-you-should-understand-backprop-e2f06eab496b)**

The essay exists because students complained. CS231n made them implement forward and backward passes by hand in raw NumPy, and the objection was reasonable: TensorFlow computes gradients automatically, so why spend a week on the derivative of a matrix multiply? Karpathy's answer is that **backpropagation is a leaky abstraction**, and the leaks are not exotic. They are the ordinary failure modes of training, and they are invisible unless you know what the backward pass does.

The examples are the essay. **Saturated sigmoids:** the local gradient of $\sigma(z)$ is $\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, which peaks at `0.25` and goes to zero in both tails. Initialise weights too large and every preactivation lands in a tail, so the entire network multiplies incoming gradient by approximately zero and nothing moves. The forward pass looks fine. The loss is simply flat, and no error is raised anywhere.

**Dead ReLUs:** the gradient through $\max(0, z)$ is a gate — it passes gradient unchanged when the unit is active and passes nothing when it is not. A single large update can push a unit's bias far enough negative that it never activates on any training example again, at which point it receives no gradient forever and cannot recover. Karpathy's figure is that with a learning rate set too high you can find `40%` of a network dead, and the network still trains, just with a fraction of its capacity permanently switched off. Again, no error.

**Exploding and vanishing gradients in RNNs:** the same recurrent matrix appears in the product at every timestep, so the backward pass through $T$ steps involves that matrix raised to roughly the $T$th power. Spectral radius above one and the gradient explodes; below one and it vanishes. Clipping addresses the first. The second is why LSTMs exist, and Karpathy makes the point that vanilla RNNs are the default only because the failure is silent.

The framing generalises past its examples, which is why it has aged well. The claim is not that automatic differentiation is bad — it is that **a differentiable program can fail in ways only visible in the backward pass**, and the forward pass will not tell you. Every abstraction the field has added since has widened the gap the essay describes.

> The forward pass tells you the network computed something; only the backward pass tells you whether it can learn.

**What did not survive:** most of the specific pathologies. The essay's three examples were the live problems of 2016 and they have largely been engineered away — sensible initialisation schemes, batch and layer normalisation, residual connections that hand gradient an identity path, and activations like GELU and SwiGLU that do not have a hard zero-gradient region. A modern transformer will not die the way a 2016 ReLU stack died. What did survive is the argument, and it has strengthened: the stack is now several layers thicker than TensorFlow was, and a practitioner debugging a fine-tune through a trainer wrapper, a parameter-efficient adapter and a distributed sharding strategy is further from the arithmetic than any CS231n student ever was. The essay's title is still the correct advice; only the list of things that break has been replaced.
