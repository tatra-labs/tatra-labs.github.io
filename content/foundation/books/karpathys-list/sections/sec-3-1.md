**Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin · 2017 · [arXiv:1706.03762](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)**

The mechanism — scaled dot-product attention, the $\sqrt{d_k}$ term, multiple heads, sinusoidal positions, the $O(1)$ path length that replaced recurrence — is worked through in [Sutskever's List](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-2-5), and building it line by line is [lecture 7](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-4). This entry is about a different fact, which is the one Karpathy stresses when he introduces the paper in the talk and which almost every retelling omits.

**This is a machine translation paper.** The abstract is about WMT 2014 English-to-German and English-to-French. The headline results are `28.4` and `41.8` BLEU. The task, the baselines, the ablations and the entire experimental section are translation. The architecture that runs the industry appears in it as the means to that end, and the paper makes no claim whatsoever about language modelling, few-shot learning, instruction following, or anything a person would now describe as what a transformer is for.

The consequence is that **the industry kept half the architecture and threw the other half away.** The paper's model is an encoder-decoder: a bidirectional encoder reads the source sentence, a causal decoder writes the target, and cross-attention connects them. That is the right shape for translation, where you genuinely have two sequences. GPT and everything descended from it kept only the decoder, dropped cross-attention entirely, and trained on the objective the paper does not use. The most consequential architecture of the last decade is a subset of a translation model, arrived at by deletion.

Two details are worth holding on to because they explain why the deletion worked. First, the decoder's self-attention was already causally masked, for the ordinary reason that at generation time you cannot see the future — so a decoder-only stack was sitting inside the paper, fully specified, needing only a corpus. Second, the paper's argument for the architecture was never about quality. Table 1 is a table of *complexity*: constant sequential operations against $O(n)$ for recurrence, constant maximum path length between any two positions against $O(n)$. The claim was that this thing parallelises. Quality was the evidence, parallelism was the thesis, and parallelism is what made the next eight years possible.

Karpathy's framing in the talk is that the architecture has since been remarkably stable — that a 2017 diagram still describes a 2026 model, up to substitutions — and that this is unusual and slightly suspicious. It is also, in his account, the least interesting part of a modern system, because everything that distinguishes one frontier model from another now lives in data, in post-training, and in serving.

> The transformer was proposed as a way to make translation parallelisable; it became the substrate for everything else because its decoder half was already a complete autoregressive model and nobody had noticed.

**What did not survive:** most of the paper as written. The encoder-decoder shape survives only in translation and some speech work. Sinusoidal positional encodings are gone, replaced by rotary embeddings and ALiBi; the authors themselves reported learned embeddings performed about the same and chose sinusoids on an extrapolation hypothesis the paper did not test. Post-layer normalisation was replaced by pre-normalisation, without which deep stacks do not train. The $4\times$ feedforward is now typically a gated SwiGLU. Even the base configuration — `6` layers, `8` heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ — reads as a rounding error against a frontier model. What survived is the attention operation itself, the parallelism argument, and the $O(n^2)$ cost, which is still the reason long context is expensive and still the thing everyone is trying to get rid of.
