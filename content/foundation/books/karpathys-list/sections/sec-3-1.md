**Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser & Polosukhin · 2017 · [arXiv:1706.03762](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)**

The mechanism — scaled dot-product attention, the $\sqrt{d_k}$ term, multiple heads, sinusoidal positions, and the constant-length path that replaced recurrence — is worked through in [Sutskever's List](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-2-5), and building it line by line is [lecture 7](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-4). This entry is about a different fact, the one Karpathy stresses when he introduces the paper and which almost every retelling omits.

**This is a machine translation paper.** The abstract is about WMT 2014 English-to-German and English-to-French. The headline results are `28.4` and `41.8` BLEU — a translation score. The task, the baselines, the ablations and the entire experimental section are translation. The architecture that now runs an industry appears in it as a means to that end, and the paper makes no claim whatsoever about language modelling, few-shot learning, instruction following, or anything a person would now say a transformer is for.

The consequence is that **the industry kept half the architecture and threw the other half away.** The paper's model has two halves: an encoder that reads the source sentence with every word able to see every other, and a decoder that writes the target one word at a time, with cross-attention connecting the two. That is the right shape for translation, where you genuinely have two separate sequences. GPT and everything descended from it kept only the decoder, dropped cross-attention entirely, and trained on an objective the paper does not use. **The most consequential architecture of the last decade is a subset of a translation model, arrived at by deletion.**

Two details explain why that deletion worked at all. First, the decoder's self-attention was already masked so each position could not see the ones after it — for the ordinary reason that when you are generating text, the future has not happened yet. So a decoder-only model was sitting inside the paper, fully specified, needing nothing but a corpus. Second, the paper's argument for the architecture was never about quality. Table 1 is a table of *complexity*: a constant number of sequential operations against $O(n)$ for recurrence, a constant maximum path between any two positions against $O(n)$. **The claim was that this thing parallelises.** Quality was the evidence, parallelism was the thesis, and parallelism is what made the next eight years possible.

Karpathy's framing is that the architecture has since been remarkably stable — a 2017 diagram still describes a 2026 model, up to substitutions — and that this is unusual and slightly suspicious. It is also, in his account, the least interesting part of a modern system, because everything distinguishing one frontier model from another now lives in data, in post-training, and in serving.

> The transformer was proposed as a way to make translation parallelisable; it became the substrate for everything else because its decoder half was already a complete autoregressive model and nobody had noticed.

**What did not survive:** most of the paper as written. The encoder-decoder shape survives only in translation and some speech work. Sinusoidal positional encodings are gone, replaced by rotary embeddings and ALiBi; the authors themselves reported that simply learning the positions performed about the same, and chose sinusoids on an extrapolation hypothesis the paper never tested. Normalising after each sublayer was replaced by normalising before it, without which deep stacks do not train. The $4\times$ feedforward is now typically a gated SwiGLU. Even the base configuration — `6` layers, `8` heads, $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ — reads as a rounding error against a frontier model. What survived is the attention operation itself, the parallelism argument, and the $O(n^2)$ cost, which is still the reason long context is expensive and still the thing everyone is trying to get rid of.
