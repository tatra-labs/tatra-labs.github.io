**Haotian Liu, Chunyuan Li, Qingyang Wu & Yong Jae Lee · 2023 · [arXiv:2304.08485](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)**

[InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) turned a text model that completes documents into one that follows instructions, using demonstration data. There was no equivalent for vision. [CLIP](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-6-4) could match an image to a caption and [ViT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-6-3) could encode one, but no dataset existed of people conversing about images the way people converse about text, and collecting one looked expensive.

LLaVA's answer is to **generate the data with a model that cannot see.** COCO images already carry human-written captions and bounding boxes with coordinates. Feed those — the text, not the pixels — to text-only GPT-4, and ask it to produce conversations, detailed descriptions and multi-step reasoning about the scene the annotations describe. The result is `158K` multimodal instruction-following examples produced without a single image being looked at, because the symbolic description was sufficient to write plausible dialogue about the scene.

The architecture is deliberately minimal, which is the second contribution. A frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encodes the image. **A single linear projection** maps its patch features into the language model's token embedding space. A Vicuna language model consumes the result as if the image were a short sequence of ordinary tokens. Training is two stages: first the projection alone, aligning visual features to word embeddings while both large models stay frozen; then the projection and language model together on the instruction data. The whole thing trained in about a day on eight A100s.

The lesson practitioners took from it was the assembly, and it became the standard playbook: **take a strong vision encoder, take a strong language model, train a small adapter between them, and supply instruction data synthetically.** Nearly every open multimodal model since is a variation on those four steps, and the reason is that the expensive components are reused rather than retrained.

> Two frozen models and a linear layer are enough to make a language model see, provided somebody generates the conversation data — and that can be done from annotations, by a model with no eyes.

**What did not survive:** the specifics, at a fairly brisk pace. The linear projector became a two-layer MLP in LLaVA-1.5 within months, then resamplers, cross-attention adapters and tile-based schemes for higher resolution. The `336`-pixel input was the binding constraint on anything text-heavy — a document, a chart, a screenshot — and most of the subsequent engineering effort in this area went into resolution rather than into reasoning.

**The data pipeline is the part that needs stating plainly**, because it is clever and it is also the source of the model's worst behaviour. The instruction data was written by a model that saw a caption and a list of boxes, so it describes what the annotations imply rather than what is in the image. Anything the annotations omit is absent; anything GPT-4 reasonably inferred but that is not actually depicted is present, and is now a training target. Fine-tuning on that teaches the model to produce confident, fluent descriptions of scenes it is partly reconstructing from plausibility rather than reading off the pixels — which is precisely the object hallucination that early LLaVA-class models became known for. It is a good illustration of a general rule: synthetic data inherits the blind spots of whatever generated it, and here the generator was blind in the most literal available sense.
