**Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child, Gray, Radford, Wu & Amodei · 2020 · [arXiv:2001.08361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)**

The fits themselves — `L(N)`, `L(D)`, `L(C)` as power laws with small exponents, the finding that architecture barely registers, and the allocation rule Chinchilla later overturned — are worked through in [Sutskever's List](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-5-3). What that entry does not dwell on, and what matters most for reading the rest of this chapter, is **the quantity being predicted.**

Scaling laws predict **cross-entropy loss on held-out text.** That is all. They do not predict whether the model can add four-digit numbers, write a correct SQL query, follow a negated instruction, or refuse a jailbreak. There is no law of that form. The plot that persuades a company to spend nine figures on a training run forecasts a number that no user, product manager or regulator has ever cared about, and the bridge from that number to anything anyone wants is left entirely to precedent and nerve.

**This gap is the single most important thing to understand about the pre-training era**, and it produced one of the field's better arguments. Wei et al. (2022) catalogued "emergent abilities" — tasks where accuracy sits at chance across several orders of magnitude of scale and then jumps, apparently discontinuously, at some threshold. If true, that is alarming and also useless for planning: it means capability arrives without warning. Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) then argued the discontinuities are largely an artifact of the **metric**, not the model. Exact-match accuracy on a five-token answer is a step function of the underlying per-token probability: the model improves smoothly and the score stays at zero until every token happens to be right at once. Swap in a continuous measure — token edit distance, or the log-likelihood of the correct answer — and most of the famous emergence curves straighten out into the same smooth power laws the loss follows.

That rebuttal is now the mainstream reading, and it is more reassuring than it first appears and less than people take it to be. Reassuring: the underlying capability really does scale predictably, so the forecasting instrument is not lying. Less so: **the thing that jumps is the thing you ship.** A user does not experience a smooth improvement in per-token log-likelihood; they experience the query working, on the day it starts working. Whether the discontinuity is "real" is a question about metrics. Whether it is *predictable in advance for the metric you actually care about* is a question about products, and the answer is still mostly no.

Karpathy's use of the plot in the talk is the industry's: it is the reason large training runs get approved. You fit a ladder of small runs, extrapolate, and commit. What he adds is the honest coda — that the loss is a proxy everyone has agreed to trust because it is the only smooth thing available.

> The curve that justifies the expenditure predicts held-out cross-entropy; every capability anyone is actually buying has to be inferred from it by analogy.

**What did not survive:** two assumptions rather than the fits. The first is the allocation rule, corrected by [Chinchilla](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-4) and traced by Pearce and Song (2024) largely to Kaplan counting non-embedding parameters at small scale. The second is quieter and now binding: the paper assumes an effectively infinite supply of fresh, unique tokens. Muennighoff et al. (2023) studied the data-constrained regime and found repeated data holds its value for roughly four epochs and is worth close to nothing past sixteen, which changes the shape of the problem for anyone training at frontier scale today. Lilian Weng's [Scaling Laws, Carefully](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-4) goes further and shows how procedurally fragile these fits are — that rounding, loss averaging and what you count as a parameter can move the extrapolation more than the science does.
