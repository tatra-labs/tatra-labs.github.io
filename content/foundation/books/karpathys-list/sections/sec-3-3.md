**Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child, Gray, Radford, Wu & Amodei · 2020 · [arXiv:2001.08361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)**

The fits themselves — loss falling as a smooth power law in model size, data and compute, the finding that architecture barely registers, and the allocation rule Chinchilla later overturned — are worked through in [Sutskever's List](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-5-3). What that entry does not dwell on, and what matters most for reading the rest of this chapter, is **what quantity is actually being predicted.**

Scaling laws predict **cross-entropy loss on held-out text** — roughly, how surprised the model is by the next word in text it has never seen. That is all they predict. They do not predict whether the model can add four-digit numbers, write a correct SQL query, follow a negated instruction, or refuse a jailbreak. There is no law of that form.

Sit with the consequence. The plot that persuades a company to spend nine figures on a training run forecasts a number that no user, product manager or regulator has ever cared about, and the bridge from that number to anything anyone actually wants is left entirely to precedent and nerve.

**This gap is the single most important thing to understand about the pre-training era**, and it produced one of the field's better arguments. Wei et al. (2022) catalogued "emergent abilities" — tasks where accuracy sits at chance across several orders of magnitude of scale and then jumps, apparently discontinuously, at some threshold. If true, that is alarming and also useless for planning, because it means capability arrives without warning.

Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) then argued the discontinuities are largely an artifact of the **metric** rather than a fact about the model. The argument is clean enough to follow without any background. Suppose the answer is five tokens long and you score it by exact match — all five right, or nothing. The model's per-token accuracy can climb smoothly for a long time while the score stays pinned at zero, because getting four of five right earns nothing. Then the fifth falls into place and the score leaps. Swap in a continuous measure — how many characters are wrong, or how much probability the model puts on the right answer — and most of the famous emergence curves straighten out into the same smooth power laws the loss follows.

That rebuttal is now the mainstream reading, and it is both more reassuring than it first appears and less so than people take it to be. Reassuring: the underlying capability really does scale predictably, so the forecasting instrument is not lying. Less so: **the thing that jumps is the thing you ship.** A user does not experience a smooth improvement in per-token probability; they experience the query working, on the day it starts working. Whether the discontinuity is "real" is a question about metrics. Whether it is *predictable in advance for the metric you actually care about* is a question about products, and the answer is still mostly no.

Karpathy's use of the plot is the industry's: it is the reason large training runs get approved. Fit a ladder of small runs, extrapolate, commit the cluster. What he adds is the honest coda — the loss is a proxy everyone has agreed to trust because it is the only smooth thing available.

> The curve that justifies the expenditure predicts held-out cross-entropy; every capability anyone is actually buying has to be inferred from it by analogy.

**What did not survive:** two assumptions, rather than the fits themselves. The first is the allocation rule, corrected by [Chinchilla](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-4) and traced by Pearce and Song (2024) largely to how Kaplan counted parameters at small scale. The second is quieter and now binding: the paper assumes an effectively infinite supply of fresh, unique tokens. Muennighoff et al. (2023) studied the data-constrained case and found that repeated data holds its value for roughly four passes and is worth close to nothing past sixteen, which changes the shape of the problem for anyone training at frontier scale today. Lilian Weng's [Scaling Laws, Carefully](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-4) goes further and shows how procedurally fragile these fits are — rounding, loss averaging and what you count as a parameter can move the extrapolation more than the science does.
