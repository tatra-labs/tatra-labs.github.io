**Radford, Wu, Child, Luan, Amodei & Sutskever · 2019 · [OpenAI](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf)**

The paper's argument is contained in its title, and the title is a claim about *supervision* rather than about architecture. The architecture is the [transformer decoder](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-1), barely modified. What is new is the assertion that **a language model trained on enough varied text is already a multitask system** — because the text it is trained on contains demonstrations of the tasks.

The mechanism is task conditioning, and it is worth slowing down for. An ordinary supervised model learns to produce an output given an input. A general system would need to produce an output given an input *and* a specification of which task to perform — three things rather than two, which normally means new machinery. The insight is that when the medium is text, the task specification is *also* text, so all three collapse into a single sequence and no architectural change is needed at all.

"Translate to French, *English text*, *French text*" is just a string. A model that predicts the next token well on a corpus containing enough such strings will translate — not because anyone trained it to, but because translating is what minimises loss on that region of the data.

**The dataset is the contribution.** WebText was built by scraping outbound links from Reddit posts with at least three karma — a cheap stand-in for human curation, on the theory that a link somebody bothered to upvote is likelier to be worth reading than a random crawl. That yielded about `8M` documents and `40GB` of text, with Wikipedia deliberately excluded so that evaluation on Wikipedia-derived benchmarks would not be testing on training data. That exclusion is a small, careful decision most later work stopped making, and contamination has been a running sore ever since.

Four models were trained, from `117M` to `1542M` parameters, with a `50257`-token vocabulary and a `1024`-token context. The headline result is zero-shot: state of the art on seven of eight language-modelling datasets **without training on any of them**. The secondary results are more honest than they are usually reported. Reading comprehension on CoQA reached `55` F1, competitive with early supervised baselines. Summarisation was poor. Translation was weak. And the paper reports that even the `1.5B` model still *underfits* WebText — that more capacity would help — which is the observation [the scaling papers](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-3) turned into a research programme a year later.

**The release is part of the paper's history and cannot be separated from it.** OpenAI initially withheld the `1.5B` model, publishing the smaller ones and citing misuse risk, then released progressively larger checkpoints over nine months. The decision was criticised as both overcautious and as marketing, and the full model shipped in November 2019 without the harms materialising in the predicted form. Whatever one concludes, this is the moment the field acquired a release-norms debate, and every staged rollout since inherits the template.

> Specify the task in the same medium as the data and supervision becomes unnecessary — not because the model learned to follow instructions, but because instruction-shaped text was in the corpus and predicting it well requires performing it.

**What did not survive:** zero-shot prompting as the interface. The paper's method was to coax a base model with a cleverly shaped prefix, and it worked about as well as the paper reports, which is to say erratically. GPT-3 replaced it with worked examples placed in the prompt; [InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) then replaced *that* by training the behaviour in directly, which is why nobody prompt-engineers a base model any more. The Reddit-karma heuristic did not survive either — frontier corpora are now filtered by learned quality classifiers at a scale where a `3`-karma threshold is not a meaningful signal. What survived is the central claim, and it survived so completely that it is now hard to see as a claim at all.
