**Yao, Yu, Zhao, Shafran, Griffiths, Cao & Narasimhan · 2023 · [arXiv:2305.10601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10601)**

Chain-of-thought is a single path through the problem, sampled left to right and never revisited. If the third step is wrong, everything after it elaborates the error, and the model has no way to notice or to go back. Tree of Thoughts asks what happens if you let it branch, and the framing is explicit about its debt to Newell and Simon's account of problem-solving as search through a space of states.

Four components. **Thought decomposition**: choose a unit of progress appropriate to the problem — an equation, a paragraph plan, a candidate word — rather than a token. **A thought generator**: sample or propose several next thoughts from the current partial state. **A state evaluator**: ask the model itself to judge how promising each partial state is, either by scoring it directly or by voting across candidates. **A search algorithm**: breadth-first or depth-first over the resulting tree, keeping the best `b` states at each level and pruning the rest.

The evaluator is the load-bearing piece and the genuinely new one. [AlphaGo](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-5-1) had a value network trained on millions of self-play outcomes; there is no such thing for text, so ToT substitutes the model's own judgement of a partial solution. That works when partial progress is checkable — in the Game of 24, whether the remaining numbers can still reach `24` is something the model can assess reasonably well.

The headline result is on that task: GPT-4 with chain-of-thought solved `4%` of Game of 24 problems, and with Tree of Thoughts `74%`. On mini crosswords, word-level success rose from around `16%` to `60%`. Those are enormous gaps, and they are honest — this is the clearest demonstration that a single greedy pass leaves a great deal on the table for problems where you can tell a bad partial answer from a good one.

> Deliberation needs three things: a unit of progress smaller than the answer, a way to generate several, and a way to tell which is promising — and for text the third has to come from the model itself.

**What did not survive:** the algorithm, quite thoroughly. The costs are the obvious problem — the search issues something like a hundred times the model calls of a single chain for one answer, which is defensible for a puzzle and absurd for a product. The self-evaluator is the deeper one: it is the same model, with the same blind spots, asked to grade its own partial work, so on any problem where the model cannot recognise a bad state the search prunes the right branch and explores the wrong one confidently. That confines the method to problems with checkable intermediate structure, which is a much smaller set than the paper's framing suggests, and the evaluation reflects it — Game of 24 and a set of `20` mini crosswords are narrow tasks chosen because the evaluator works on them.

The reason nobody runs ToT in 2026 is that the behaviour got absorbed. Reinforcement learning on long reasoning traces produced models that branch, evaluate, abandon and restart **inside one linear generation** — reading an o-series or R1-style trace, the backtracking is plainly there, expressed as "wait, that's wrong, let me try instead" rather than as a tree in an orchestration layer. That is cheaper, it needs no external search harness, and the model learns its own evaluator as part of the policy instead of being asked to improvise one at inference. What Tree of Thoughts contributed was the argument that deliberate search over intermediate states is the missing capability. It was right about that, and wrong that the search should live outside the model.
