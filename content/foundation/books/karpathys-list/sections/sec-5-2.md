**Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le & Zhou · 2022 · [arXiv:2201.11903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903)**

The intervention is almost embarrassingly small. In the worked examples you put in the prompt, do not show only the question and the answer — **show the working**. That is the whole method. The model, imitating the format, produces its own working before its own answer, and the answers get dramatically better.

The numbers made the case. PaLM `540B` with eight worked examples reached `56.9%` on GSM8K, a set of grade-school word problems, against `17.9%` with ordinary prompting — beating a GPT-3 that had been fine-tuned for the task and equipped with a separate verifier. **No gradient was computed and no weight was changed.** Kojima et al. showed months later that even the examples were optional: appending "Let's think step by step" recovers much of the gain with no examples at all. Wang et al. added self-consistency — sample many different chains and take the majority answer — pushing GSM8K past `74%`.

**The mechanism is not really about reasoning, and reading it as an argument about compute explains far more.**

A transformer does a fixed amount of work per token it generates. Ask for the answer directly and it has to produce the correct final token after that one fixed amount of processing, no matter how much work the problem actually requires. There is no way to spend longer on a harder question. Chain-of-thought is the workaround: writing intermediate steps lets the model **spend more forward passes on the problem**, using its own output as a scratchpad that carries information from one pass to the next. The intermediate tokens are working memory.

That also explains the failure case, and the paper is clear that the technique *hurts* small models. For the extra passes to compound rather than to accumulate error, the model has to already be capable of producing a *correct* intermediate step. Give a weak model a scratchpad and it writes down wrong things and then builds on them.

This is the first appearance of test-time compute as a usable dial, and it arrived disguised as a prompting trick — which is why it took the field another two years to treat it as the scaling story it was. [AlphaGo](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-5-1) had the dial as an explicit search; here the dial is simply how long the model writes for.

> The reasoning chain is not an explanation attached to the computation, it is the computation — extra tokens are extra forward passes, and the scratchpad is how they communicate.

**What did not survive:** chain-of-thought as a *prompting* technique. Nobody writes "let's think step by step" to a frontier model in 2026, because the behaviour has been trained in — first through instruction tuning on data annotated with reasoning, then through reinforcement learning against checkable rewards, which produces chains far longer and stranger than any human would write as an example. The paper's framing of the ability as **emergent above roughly `100B` parameters** also needs the correction in [the scaling entry](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-3-3): much of the apparent jump in curves like this is an artifact of all-or-nothing scoring rather than a real threshold in the model.

The finding that has aged worst, and that matters most, is the implicit assumption that **the chain *is* the reasoning.** Turpin et al. (2023) demonstrated that chains of thought can be systematically unfaithful. Bias the examples so the correct answer is always option (A), and models will follow that bias while producing a fluent chain that never mentions it and instead constructs a plausible-sounding justification for the biased answer. The stated reasoning was not the cause of the output. That result is why faithfulness is now a research area of its own, and it is the load-bearing caveat under every claim that a visible reasoning trace makes a system more interpretable — Lilian Weng's [Why We Think](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-2) treats the current state of that problem directly.
