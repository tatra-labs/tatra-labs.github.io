**Bubeck, Chandrasekaran, Eldan, Gehrke, Horvitz, Kamar, Lee, Lee, Li, Lundberg, Nori, Palangi, Ribeiro & Zhang · 2023 · [arXiv:2303.12712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12712)**

A hundred and fifty-five pages from Microsoft Research on an early, text-only version of GPT-4, concluding that it "could reasonably be viewed as an early (yet still incomplete) version of an artificial general intelligence system." It is the most-cited and least-replicable item on this list, and both facts are instructive.

**The method is stated openly and is not the field's method.** The authors reject benchmark evaluation on two grounds: benchmarks were saturating — models scoring near the ceiling, so the test no longer separates anything — and there was no way to rule out that the test questions had been in the training data. So instead they probe with novel tasks and report what happens, closer in their own description to psychology than to machine learning. Draw a unicorn in TikZ, a drawing language with no visual feedback. Stack a book, nine eggs, a laptop, a bottle and a nail. Prove there are infinitely many primes in the style of Shakespeare. Track who believes what about a container's contents across a short story.

The results were genuinely startling in March 2023, and it is worth being fair about why the paper mattered. **It demonstrated breadth at a moment when the field had no vocabulary for breadth.** Every evaluation instrument then available measured one task at a time, and the interesting property of this model was that it was mediocre-to-good at an unbounded number of things nobody had trained it for.

The paper also identified, in a limitations section most readers skip, exactly the deficiency that became the next three years of research. A model that produces one token at a time, each conditioned on the last, has no mechanism for planning ahead, backing out of a bad start, or working an answer out before committing to its first word. That observation is the whole of [chapter 5](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-5-1).

**The problems are structural, not stylistic.** The model studied is an internal checkpoint that was never released and no longer exists in that configuration, so no claim in the paper can be checked by anyone outside Microsoft, then or now. Contamination cannot be ruled out either — the authors say plainly that they do not have access to GPT-4's training data, which makes "novel task" an assumption rather than a finding. The examples are selected, with no sampling protocol and no reported failure rate, so a reader cannot tell a capability from a lucky draw. "AGI" is imported from a 1994 newspaper editorial signed by fifty-two psychologists, a definition chosen for existing rather than for being measurable. And Microsoft had by then committed billions to OpenAI, which does not make the observations false but does mean nobody involved was disinterested.

> The paper's evidence is a collection of impressive anecdotes about a model that cannot be inspected, evaluated on tasks that cannot be shown to be novel, selected by authors who cannot be independent.

**What did not survive:** the genre. Several specific claims were re-tested once GPT-4 was public and came back weaker — the theory-of-mind results in particular proved sensitive to rephrasings a person would find irrelevant, which is the signature of a pattern-matched answer rather than a modelled one. More importantly, the field moved against the whole approach. The response to "benchmarks are contaminated and saturating" turned out not to be qualitative probing but *better benchmarks*: held-out and rotating test sets, contamination audits, adversarial and dynamic evaluation, and uncertainty reported alongside the score. That is the right correction, and *Sparks* is most useful now as the clearest example of what it was correcting. Read it for the limitations section, for the historical record of what March 2023 felt like, and as a case study in how a paper can be simultaneously influential and unfalsifiable.
