**Lee, Phatale, Mansoor, Mesnard, Ferret, Lu, Bishop, Hall, Carbune, Rastogi & Prakash · 2023 · [arXiv:2309.00267](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00267)**

Human preference labelling is the bottleneck in the [InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) pipeline. It is slow, expensive, hard to keep consistent, and it does not scale with anything else in the system — every other input to a frontier model has fallen in cost per unit by orders of magnitude while a considered human judgement has not. This paper asks the obvious question: can an off-the-shelf language model produce the preference labels instead?

The setup is a substitution and nothing more. Where RLHF shows a pair of candidate responses to a person and records which they prefer, RLAIF shows the pair to a labelling model with a preamble describing the criterion, a few worked exemplars, and a request for a judgement. Everything downstream is unchanged: a reward model is trained on those labels, and the policy is optimised against it. The craft is in the prompting. Chain-of-thought before the verdict improves alignment with human labels. Position bias is real and substantial — the labeller favours whichever response it sees first — so each pair is scored in both orders and averaged.

**The result is a tie, and the tie is the finding.** On summarisation, both RLAIF and RLHF policies were preferred over the supervised baseline at closely comparable rates, and when the two were compared head to head, human evaluators split roughly evenly. Similar outcomes held for helpful dialogue. A later refinement, direct RLAIF, skips the reward model entirely and uses the labelling model's score as the reward signal, removing another learned component.

Nobody claimed the AI labels were better. The claim is that they are **not detectably worse on these tasks**, at a cost difference of several orders of magnitude, which for anyone building a system is the same as being better. This paper, along with Anthropic's earlier Constitutional AI work, is why synthetic preference data is now the default and human preference data is reserved for the cases that need it.

> Preference labelling was assumed to be the irreducibly human part of the pipeline; on tasks where a model can already judge quality, it turns out to be the cheapest part to automate.

**The problem the paper does not solve** is circularity, and it is worth stating precisely rather than as a vague worry. The labelling model and the policy are trained on overlapping data and share a prior. Where they share a blind spot, the preference signal will not reveal it — it will reinforce it, because the labeller cannot prefer a quality it does not itself possess. This bounds the method exactly where it would be most valuable: a model cannot bootstrap past its own ceiling on the axes it is being judged for. Summarisation is a benign case, since judging a summary is easier than writing one. Factual accuracy in a specialist domain is not, and neither is anything where the *right* answer is contested rather than merely hard.

**What this collides with** is worth reading alongside it. Lilian Weng's [Thinking about High-Quality Human Data](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-4) makes the case that annotator disagreement is often signal rather than noise — that on subjective or culturally loaded questions there is more than one defensible label, and a process that forces convergence destroys information. An AI labeller does the opposite of preserving that: it returns one confident verdict, cheaply, with no variance to inspect and no demographic spread behind it. Read together, the two papers describe a genuine trade the field has largely made without discussing: cheap unanimity in place of expensive disagreement.
