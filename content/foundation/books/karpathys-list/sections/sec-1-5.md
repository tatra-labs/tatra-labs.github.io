**Andrej Karpathy · 2024 · [Lecture 8](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zduSFxRajkE) · [github.com/karpathy/minbpe](https://github.com/karpathy/minbpe)**

The lecture opens with a list of complaints about language models and gives the same answer to every one. Why can a model not spell, or reverse a string, or do arithmetic reliably? Why is it worse in Hindi than in English? Why did GPT-2 write bad Python? Why does a trailing space in your prompt degrade the output? **Tokenization.** The joke lands because the list is not rhetorical — each item has a mechanical explanation that the lecture then supplies.

First, what tokenization is. A model does not see letters. It sees a sequence of numbers, each standing for a chunk of text, and those chunks are decided in advance by a separate procedure. Which chunks exist turns out to matter enormously.

**Byte-pair encoding** is simple enough to state in a sentence. Encode the text as UTF-8, which gives you an alphabet of `256` byte values. Count which adjacent pair occurs most often, mint a new token standing for that pair, replace every occurrence, and repeat until the vocabulary is the size you wanted. The tokenizer *is* that ordered list of merges. Encoding applies them in order; decoding is a lookup. minbpe implements this in a few hundred readable lines, and the training loop is a dictionary and an `argmax`.

What makes the lecture worth two hours is everything that is *not* the algorithm.

**The regex split comes first.** GPT-2 does not run the merge procedure on raw text. It first chops the text with a hand-written pattern so that merges can never cross certain boundaries — punctuation cannot glue onto the preceding word, runs of whitespace stay separate. GPT-4's pattern differs in ways that each encode a lesson: contractions are matched regardless of case, whitespace is grouped so Python indentation costs roughly half as many tokens, and **runs of digits are capped at three** — a direct attempt to fix arithmetic by making numbers chunk less arbitrarily.

**Vocabulary size is a real trade-off, not a free parameter.** A larger vocabulary means shorter sequences, which buys effective context and cheaper attention. But each token then appears less often in training, so its representation is fitted from less evidence, and both the embedding matrix and the final output layer grow. GPT-2 chose `50257`; GPT-4's `cl100k_base` roughly doubles it.

**The most instructive part is the failure gallery.** Consider **SolidGoldMagikarp**. It is a token that exists because the tokenizer was trained on a corpus containing a particular Reddit username thousands of times — while the *model* was trained on a corpus that had been cleaned of it. So the token has a representation that received no gradient at any point; it sits wherever random initialisation happened to put it. Prompt the model with it and you get evasion, insults and non-sequiturs. It is a genuinely unaligned input that no amount of safety training touches, because it was never in the training distribution at all.

The trailing-whitespace problem is the same class of error in miniature: " world" is one token, while " " followed by "world" is two, and the second is a sequence the model has essentially never seen.

> The tokenizer is a second model, trained on a different corpus, with its own vocabulary and its own failure modes, and every complaint about the language model's competence has to be checked against it first.

**What did not survive:** the resignation. The lecture ends by saying the ideal is to delete the tokenizer and feed raw bytes, and that nobody has shown this working at scale — accurate in early 2024, no longer the whole story. Meta's Byte Latent Transformer (December 2024) groups bytes into dynamically sized patches using a small model to decide where the boundaries go, spending more compute where the next byte is unpredictable, and matched tokeniser-based training at comparable scale; H-Net and related schemes followed. None of this has displaced BPE in production, so the practical advice stands. But the framing to carry forward is that tokenisation is a compression choice made before training and largely unexamined afterwards, not a permanent fact about language models. Several of the lecture's own complaints were then fixed by *better* tokenisers rather than by removing them: digit capping and per-digit splitting substantially repaired arithmetic, which is the strongest evidence for the lecture's thesis and against its pessimism.
