**Andrej Karpathy · 2022 · [Lectures 2-6, nn-zero-to-hero](https://github.com/karpathy/nn-zero-to-hero)**

**makemore** makes more of the things you give it. The dataset is a text file of about thirty-two thousand names from US Social Security records, the model is character-level, and the task is to produce plausible names that are not in the file. It is deliberately small enough to train in seconds and deliberately real enough that everything that goes wrong in language modelling goes wrong here first.

The opening move is worth the whole lecture. A bigram model is built twice. First by counting: a `27x27` matrix of "how often did character `j` follow character `i`", rows normalised to probabilities. Then as a neural network: one-hot the input character, multiply by a `27x27` weight matrix, softmax, minimise negative log-likelihood by gradient descent. **The two converge to the same table.** The weights end up as the logarithms of the counts. Add-one smoothing on the counting side turns out to be exactly L2 regularisation on the network side. This is a genuinely clarifying result — the neural network is not a different kind of object from the count table, it is a parameterisation of it that happens to generalise once the table gets too sparse to fill.

Part 2 takes the obvious next step, following Bengio et al. (2003): embed each character into a low-dimensional vector, concatenate a context window of three, push through a `tanh` hidden layer, read out logits. The embedding table is the point — characters that behave alike end up near each other without anyone specifying which ones should. This lecture also introduces the machinery around training rather than in it: train/dev/test splits, minibatches, and a crude but effective learning-rate sweep.

Part 3 is the most useful of the five and the least glamorous. It opens by asking what the loss should be at initialisation. Twenty-seven characters, uniform, gives `-log(1/27)`, about `3.29`. The network starts near `27` instead, because the output layer's initial weights are large enough to produce confidently wrong logits, and the first few hundred steps are spent squashing them back toward zero — the "hockey stick" in the loss curve. Scale the last layer down at init and the hockey stick disappears. The same reasoning applied to the `tanh` layer gives Kaiming initialisation, and then batch normalisation as the general solution. What makes the lecture valuable is the diagnostics: histograms of activations, histograms of gradients, and the ratio of update magnitude to parameter magnitude, which should sit near `1e-3`. These are the plots that tell you a network is sick before the loss does.

Part 4 removes autograd entirely and backpropagates the whole thing by hand — cross-entropy, the `tanh`, the batch-norm statistics, the embedding gather. Part 5 replaces the flat concatenation with a hierarchical one in the style of WaveNet, fusing two characters at a time up a tree so that context is combined progressively rather than crushed into one layer.

> A one-layer neural network trained with cross-entropy on one-hot inputs is a smoothed count table; everything after that is a way of not having to store the table.

**What did not survive:** the destination. Part 5 ends by pointing at recurrent architectures as the natural continuation, and that lecture never arrived — the course jumps to [a transformer](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-1-4) instead. In hindsight that is the right call, and the series is honest enough to leave the seam visible. Part 5 is also the weakest of the five: the hierarchical model wins only modestly over the flat MLP, and a good part of it goes on shape debugging and module plumbing rather than on the idea. That makes it an accurate depiction of what the work actually feels like, and a poor argument for dilated convolutions, which lost anyway.
