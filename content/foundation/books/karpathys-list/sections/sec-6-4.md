**Radford, Kim, Hallacy, Ramesh, Goh, Agarwal, Sastry, Askell, Mishkin, Clark, Krueger & Sutskever · 2021 · [arXiv:2103.00020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)**

Every vision model before this was trained against a **fixed taxonomy**. ImageNet has a thousand classes; a model trained on it can express exactly one thousand thoughts about an image, and adding the thousand-and-first requires collecting labelled examples and retraining. The taxonomy is a design decision made by annotators years earlier, and the model can never exceed it.

CLIP's premise is that the internet already contains supervision in a form that has no taxonomy at all. Images appear alongside text people wrote about them — captions, alt text, surrounding prose — and that text is a description drawn from the whole of language rather than from a list. Collect `400M` such pairs and the label space becomes open.

**The training objective is contrastive, and the choice was made on efficiency grounds.** An earlier version tried to predict the caption's exact words from the image and was slow to converge, because predicting the precise phrasing of a caption is much harder than the task anyone wants. So instead: take a batch of $N$ pairs, embed all images and all texts, and compute the $N \times N$ matrix of similarities. Train so that the $N$ correct pairings score high and the $N^2 - N$ incorrect ones score low — a symmetric cross-entropy over rows and columns. The model must decide which caption goes with which image, which is exactly the alignment being sought and nothing more. The paper reports this trained several times faster than the predictive objective.

**Zero-shot classification then falls out as a special case.** To classify an image into any set of categories, write each category as a sentence — "a photo of a *label*" — embed those sentences, and pick the nearest. No training, no fine-tuning, and a taxonomy chosen at inference time by whoever is asking. CLIP reached `76.2%` zero-shot on ImageNet, matching the original ResNet-50 **without seeing a single one of its `1.28M` training images.**

The robustness result deserves more attention than the headline. On distribution-shifted variants — sketches, adversarially filtered natural images, artistic renditions — CLIP degraded far less than models trained directly on ImageNet, which collapse. A model trained on one dataset learns that dataset's idiosyncrasies; a model trained on the internet's variety learns something closer to the concept.

> Supervision does not need a label space — a caption is a label drawn from all of language, and contrastive matching is enough to learn from it at scale.

**What did not survive:** the specific model, and one assumption underneath it. SigLIP's pairwise sigmoid loss removed the requirement that the whole batch participate in each softmax, scaling better and training more cheaply, and it or its successors is what sits inside most current vision-language systems. CLIP itself is now mostly a frozen encoder — the role it plays in [LLaVA](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-6-5) — rather than a classifier anyone deploys.

The deeper limitation is compositional. Later work established that CLIP behaves substantially like a **bag of words**: it scores "the horse is eating the grass" and "the grass is eating the horse" almost identically, because the contrastive objective is satisfied by matching content words to visual content and never forces word order to matter. That failure is inherited by everything built on it and is a live problem. The paper is also honest about its own weaknesses — counting, fine-grained distinctions, systematic reasoning, and a sensitivity to prompt wording substantial enough that the authors engineered prompts to get their headline numbers. And the `400M` pairs were scraped without consent or documentation, which set a precedent the field is still litigating.
