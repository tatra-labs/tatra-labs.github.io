**Fisher Yu, Vladlen Koltun · 2016 · [arXiv:1511.07122](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.07122)**

**Segmentation** means labelling every pixel — road, person, sky — rather than giving the whole image one label. In 2015, segmentation networks were classification networks with the head cut off, and that is a bad inheritance, because a classification stack is *built* to destroy resolution. Pooling repeatedly shrinks the map, which is exactly right when the output is a single label and exactly wrong when the output is one label per pixel. VGG-16 pools five times, so a $224 \times 224$ input is down to a $7 \times 7$ map by the end.

The fixes were retroactive. FCN upsampled the coarse map back up and added skip connections from earlier, finer layers; others ran the image at several scales and fused the results. Both recover detail after it has already been thrown away. And both are stuck with the same trade: the only way to widen how much of the image a unit could see was to shrink the map underneath it.

**Yu and Koltun, at ICLR 2016, separated the two.** A dilated convolution simply spreads the kernel out — it samples the input with gaps of $l$ between taps instead of at adjacent positions, $(F \ast_l k)(p) = \sum_{s + l \cdot t = p} F(s) \cdot k(t)$. A $3 \times 3$ kernel with $l = 4$ therefore covers a $9 \times 9$ area of the image using the same nine weights, and returns a map the same size as its input. Nothing is shrunk and nothing is added.

Then stack them with the gap doubling each time. The areas compose, so after layers of dilation $1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^i$ each unit sees a square of side $2^{i+2} - 1$. **The view grows exponentially with depth, the parameter count grows linearly, and the resolution does not change at all** — three things that used to be one knob are now separate.

They shipped it as two parts. A front end: delete the last two pooling layers from VGG-16, dilate the convolutions that followed them by 2 and 4 so their coverage is unchanged, and drop the padding on intermediate maps. Then a bolt-on context module — eight layers, seven $3 \times 3$ with dilations $1, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 1$ and a closing $1 \times 1$ — carrying the view out to $67 \times 67$ without touching resolution. That module needed a specific trick to train at all: initialise it as a pass-through, centre tap 1 on the matching channel and everything else 0, so it starts by doing nothing and learns only a departure from that. The front end alone reached 67.6 mean IoU on VOC-2012 test against 62.2 for FCN-8s and 62.1 for DeepLab — **most of the gain coming from deletion rather than addition**, which is the paper's quiet lesson. On validation the context module moved 69.8 to 71.3 (basic) and 72.1 (large).

Dilation is now the standard way to widen context in any network that predicts something per pixel. It went into DeepLab's atrous spatial pyramid pooling, into dilated residual networks, and out of vision entirely — WaveNet's stack of causal dilated convolutions over raw audio is the same operator on a 1-D signal. The operator itself was older, borrowed from the à trous algorithm in wavelet decomposition. What this paper contributed was the exponential stack and the demonstration that the classification scaffolding could simply be removed.

> Receptive field and resolution are independent knobs, and only pooling ever tied them together.

**The caveat:** gridding. A unit computed with gap $l$ only ever reads inputs whose coordinates match its own modulo $l$ — so a stack of layers whose gaps share a common factor quietly splits into $l^2$ separate populations that never exchange information, and thin structures come out checkered. The paper did not notice. Yu's own follow-up, Dilated Residual Networks, added degridding layers to undo it; later work chose dilations with no common factor. The serial context module did not last either — parallel dilated branches and encoder-decoder skips replaced it.
