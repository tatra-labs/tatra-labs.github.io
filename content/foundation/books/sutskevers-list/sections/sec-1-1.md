**Geoffrey E. Hinton & Drew van Camp · 1993 · [COLT '93](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~hinton/absps/colt93.pdf)**

By 1993 the defences against overfitting a small training set were structural: cut connections, tie weights into shared subsets, or quantize every weight and charge `-log p` for the bin it lands in, a cost with no smooth derivative. Weight decay worked, but was justified mostly by the fact that it worked. Even the best of these, an adaptive mixture-of-Gaussians codebook, prices a weight by the height of its density under the prior and ignores the width — how precisely it must be stated before the outputs move. Hinton and van Camp call that "a terrible waste of bits."

Their frame is a transmission: a sender sees the inputs and the targets, a receiver only the inputs. The sender ships the weights, then the residual on each training case; the receiver recovers the targets exactly. Cost is bits for weights plus bits for misfits. Coding residuals under a zero-mean Gaussian reproduces squared error; coding the weights under another reproduces weight decay, which is therefore MDL with both variances frozen in advance.

The new part replaces each weight with a Gaussian posterior `Q` — one mean, one variance, no off-diagonal terms. The sender burns random bits drawing a precise value from `Q` to within a fine tolerance `t`, then codes that value under an agreed prior `P` at `-log t - log P(w)`. Given the sampled weights and the residuals, the receiver reruns the same learning algorithm, recovers `Q`, and from `Q` plus the value reconstructs every random bit spent on the draw. Those bits arrive for free and are refunded, so the expected net cost collapses to `∫ Q(w) log[Q(w)/P(w)] dw`, the divergence from `P` to `Q`. Expected misfit under `Q` plus `KL(Q‖P)` is the variational free energy. Two approximations make it computable: `P` is an adaptive mixture of five Gaussians fitted during training, so the divergence is replaced by an upper bound, and expected squared error is propagated through a precomputed table, exact only for one hidden layer with linear outputs.

The one task tried was predicting peptide effectiveness: `128` inputs, `105` training cases, `420` test cases, `4` hidden units, `521` adaptive weights. Relative error, against guessing the training mean, was `0.286`, versus `0.967` for the same network unpenalized and `0.317` for the best simple weight decay. The objective, not the number, earns its place. Expected log-likelihood plus a divergence to a prior is what the variational autoencoder and Bayes by Backprop later maximize; the coding derivation gives that divergence a meaning the Bayesian reading lacks — it counts the bits the model costs to send.

> Bits spent specifying a weight are refundable if the receiver can rederive the distribution it was drawn from, so a noisy weight's real cost is exactly `KL(Q‖P)`.

**The caveat the authors name themselves:** the lowest total description length was reached by a degenerate solution — every weight except the output bias equal and negative, relative error about `1.0`. They call it "a serious embarrassment for the Minimum Description Length Principle or for our method of describing the weights." The `0.286` came from escaping that basin by hand, ramping the weight-cost coefficient from `.05` to `1.0` on a twelve-step schedule. The reported number is not the MDL optimum.
