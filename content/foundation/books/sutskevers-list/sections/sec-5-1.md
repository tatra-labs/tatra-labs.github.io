**Yanping Huang, Youlong Cheng, Ankur Bapna, Orhan Firat et al. (Google Brain) · 2018 (NeurIPS 2019) · [arXiv:1811.06965](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.06965)**

A model too large for one accelerator meant a bespoke parallel implementation, tied to one architecture and not transferable. The generic fallback — cut the layer stack into contiguous groups, one per device — worked but wasted the hardware. A mini-batch flows through stage 1, then stage 2, so `K-1` of the `K` accelerators sit idle. The ceiling was low: on an 8 GB Cloud TPUv2 core the largest AmoebaNet that fit was 82M parameters; on a 16 GB TPUv3 core a Transformer topped out at 282.2M.

GPipe takes a model expressed as a sequence of `L` layers, partitions it into `K` cells on `K` accelerators, then splits each mini-batch of `N` examples into `M` micro-batches of `N/M`. A stage that finishes micro-batch `i` passes its activations forward and starts `i+1` immediately, so in steady state every stage works on a different micro-batch. Gradients from all `M` micro-batches are accumulated and applied in one update at the end of the mini-batch. The result is identical to a single-device run at batch `N` — no staleness, no weight-version skew — so `K` and `M` stay performance knobs, not hyperparameters. The idle time left is filling and draining the pipe, `O((K-1)/(M+K-1))` of the step, which the paper found negligible once `M` reaches about `4K`.

The second half is re-materialisation. On the forward pass each accelerator keeps only the activations at its cell boundary and discards everything inside. When the backward pass arrives, the stage recomputes its forward from that stored input, holds the internal activations long enough to produce gradients, then frees them. Peak activation memory per accelerator falls from `O(N x L)` to `O(N + (L/K) x (N/M))`, bought with one extra forward pass. That alone lifted a single accelerator from 82M to 318M parameters.

Together they move the ceiling by orders of magnitude: AmoebaNet to 1.8B parameters across 8 partitions, and the Transformer to 83.9B across 128, a 298x increase over one core. The paper trained a 557M-parameter AmoebaNet across 4 partitions to 84.4% top-1 on ImageNet-2012, and one 6B-parameter, 128-layer Transformer over 103 languages that beat separately trained bilingual models on all 102 pairs. Published at NeurIPS 2019 as *Efficient Training of Giant Neural Networks using Pipeline Parallelism*, it earns its place less for those results than for two defaults: pipeline parallelism as the third axis beside data and tensor parallelism, and activation checkpointing as a flag rather than a research project.

> Cut the mini-batch small enough and a pipeline of layer stages keeps every accelerator busy while still producing exactly the gradient one device would.

**The caveat:** near-linear speedup holds only when stages are balanced, and often they are not. At 8 partitions with 32 micro-batches the Transformer reached 6.3x throughput; AmoebaNet reached 3.48x, because layer costs are uneven and the slowest stage sets the pace. A single layer must still fit on one accelerator, and operations that compute across the batch, such as batch normalisation, need special handling. The schedule did not survive: GPipe runs all `M` forwards before any backward, so a stage holds boundary activations for all `M` micro-batches at once. Later 1F1B schedules interleave forward and backward to keep far fewer alive.
