**Yanping Huang, Youlong Cheng, Ankur Bapna, Orhan Firat et al. (Google Brain) · 2018 (NeurIPS 2019) · [arXiv:1811.06965](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.06965)**

A model too large for one accelerator used to mean a bespoke parallel implementation, tied to one architecture and not transferable to the next. The generic fallback was obvious enough — cut the layer stack into contiguous groups, one group per device — and it worked while wasting most of the hardware. **A mini-batch flows through stage 1, then stage 2, then stage 3, so while any one stage is working, the other `K-1` accelerators sit idle.** You bought `K` devices and are using one at a time. The ceiling was low: on an 8 GB Cloud TPUv2 core the largest AmoebaNet that fit was 82M parameters; on a 16 GB TPUv3 core a Transformer topped out at 282.2M.

**GPipe's first idea is to stop sending the whole mini-batch through as one lump.** Take a model expressed as a sequence of `L` layers, partition it into `K` cells on `K` accelerators, then split each mini-batch of `N` examples into `M` smaller micro-batches. A stage that finishes micro-batch `i` passes its activations forward and immediately starts on `i+1`. In steady state every stage is working on a different micro-batch, like an assembly line rather than one car at a time.

Gradients from all `M` micro-batches are accumulated and applied in a single update at the end. **This is the part that makes the technique free:** the result is bit-for-bit identical to a single-device run at batch `N` — no stale weights, no version skew — so `K` and `M` remain performance knobs rather than hyperparameters you have to retune. The idle time left over is only filling and draining the pipe, $O((K-1)/(M+K-1))$ of the step, which the paper found negligible once `M` reaches about `4K`.

**The second idea is re-materialisation, and it is a deliberate trade of compute for memory.** Normally every intermediate activation must be kept alive through the forward pass, because the backward pass needs them. Here, each accelerator keeps only the activations at its cell boundary and throws away everything inside. When the backward pass arrives, the stage recomputes its own forward from that stored input, holds the internal activations just long enough to produce gradients, then frees them again. Peak activation memory per accelerator falls from `O(N x L)` to `O(N + (L/K) x (N/M))`, bought with one extra forward pass. That alone lifted a single accelerator from 82M to 318M parameters.

Together they move the ceiling by orders of magnitude: AmoebaNet to 1.8B parameters across 8 partitions, and the Transformer to 83.9B across 128, a 298x increase over one core. The paper trained a 557M-parameter AmoebaNet across 4 partitions to 84.4% top-1 on ImageNet-2012, and one 6B-parameter, 128-layer Transformer over 103 languages that beat separately trained bilingual models on all 102 pairs. Published at NeurIPS 2019 as *Efficient Training of Giant Neural Networks using Pipeline Parallelism*, it earns its place less for those results than for two defaults it established: pipeline parallelism as the third axis beside data and tensor parallelism, and activation checkpointing as a flag rather than a research project.

> Cut the mini-batch small enough and a pipeline of layer stages keeps every accelerator busy while still producing exactly the gradient one device would.

**The caveat:** near-linear speedup holds only when the stages are balanced, and often they are not. At 8 partitions with 32 micro-batches the Transformer reached 6.3x throughput; AmoebaNet reached only 3.48x, because layer costs are uneven and the slowest stage sets the pace for everyone. A single layer must still fit on one accelerator. Operations that compute across the batch, such as batch normalisation, need special handling. And the schedule itself did not survive: GPipe runs all `M` forwards before any backward, so a stage is holding boundary activations for all `M` micro-batches simultaneously. Later 1F1B schedules interleave forward and backward to keep far fewer alive at once.
