**Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Geoffrey E. Hinton · 2012 · [NeurIPS 25](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html)**

Before 2012, an entry in the ImageNet image-recognition contest was a pipeline of hand-designed parts: local descriptors such as SIFT or HOG that a researcher had designed to detect edges and corners, an encoding step, and a simple classifier on top. Each stage was tuned separately, and crucially **the features never adapted to the task** — they were whatever a human had thought useful in advance. Convolutional networks, which learn their own features, had worked on small problems since LeNet-5 in 1998, but nobody had trained one on 1.2 million images across 1000 classes. The best non-network entry in the 2012 contest finished at 26.2% top-5 error, meaning the right answer was absent from its five best guesses a quarter of the time.

This paper trains one network end to end — five convolutional layers, three fully connected, a 1000-way softmax, 60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons — and makes four decisions that let it converge at all.

**First, the nonlinearity is $\max(0, x)$ rather than $\tanh$.** Every unit passes its input through some squashing function; $\tanh$ flattens out once the input gets large, and a flat function has almost no slope, so gradients arrive near zero and learning stalls. The rectifier's slope is exactly $1$ on the positive side and never decays however large activations grow. A four-layer net reached 25% training error on CIFAR-10 six times faster with rectifiers than with $\tanh$.

**Second, the model is cut in half across two GTX 580s**, because 3GB on a single card cannot hold it. Half the kernels sit on each GPU; layers 2, 4 and 5 read only from maps on their own card, while layer 3 reads from both, so the cards trade activations at exactly one point. Against a single-GPU net with half the kernels per layer, the split cut top-1 error 1.7% and top-5 1.2%. Ninety epochs took five to six days.

The other two decisions attack overfitting, which a 60-million-parameter model on 1.2 million images invites. **Dropout** switches off each neuron in the first two fully connected layers with probability `0.5` on every forward pass, so no unit can count on a particular partner being there and features cannot form brittle committees; at test time every unit is active with outputs halved to match the average. It roughly doubled the iterations needed to converge — the price for the effect. **Augmentation** manufactures new training data from labels already in hand: random $224 \times 224$ crops and left-right flips taken out of $256 \times 256$ images, a nominal factor of 2048 though those variants are heavily correlated with one another, plus a jitter that shifts RGB values along the principal directions of colour variation in the dataset, worth over 1% top-1 on its own. Test predictions average ten patches — four corners, the centre, and the mirror image of each.

The ILSVRC-2012 entry finished at 15.3% top-5 test error against 26.2% for the runner-up. An eleven-point margin, in a contest normally decided by one or two. Within a year essentially every serious entry was a convolutional net on GPUs. The paper earns its place as the first clean evidence that the binding constraint had been arithmetic and labelled data rather than the model class — the argument Sutskever spent the following decade extending.

> Given enough labelled data and enough arithmetic, one generic deep network beats a decade of hand-designed features.

**What did not survive:** local response normalization, a step that divided each activation by a function of its neighbours, in imitation of lateral inhibition between real neurons. The paper credits it with 1.4% top-1 and 1.2% top-5; the VGG authors reported two years later that it gave no ILSVRC gain at all while costing memory and compute time, and batch normalization replaced the idea outright. The headline 15.3% is also an average over seven networks, two of them pre-trained on the larger 2011 release — a single model scored 18.2% top-5 on validation.
