**Alexander Rush · 2018 · [nlp.seas.harvard.edu](https://nlp.seas.harvard.edu/2018/04/03/attention.html)**

"Attention Is All You Need" specifies the model as equations and a block diagram. It fixes $N = 6$ layers, an inner feedforward width of 2048, dropout 0.1, the widths listed above, and states that each sublayer output is $\operatorname{LayerNorm}(x + \operatorname{Sublayer}(x))$.

**What it does not give is everything you need to actually build it.** The shape of the mask tensor. The exact point where a `(batch, len, 512)` activation becomes `(batch, 8, len, 64)`. How the mask that hides padding and the mask that hides the future combine into one object. How the optimiser's internal state interacts with a warmup schedule. These are not details a careful reader can infer, and reimplementations duly trained, produced plausible-looking loss curves, and landed short. The post itself records an OpenNMT-py replication reaching 26.9 BLEU on WMT English–German against the 28.4 in the paper.

**The post is the paper's own text cut into pieces, each followed by the PyTorch that implements it** — 400 lines of library code, runnable as a notebook.

Seen as code, `attention()` is disarmingly small: a scale, a mask, a softmax and two matrix multiplies. Divide $q \cdot k^{\mathsf T}$ by $\sqrt{d_k}$, apply `masked_fill(mask == 0, -1e9)` so that forbidden positions get a score so low the softmax gives them nothing, softmax over the last dimension, dropout on the attention *weights* — which the paper leaves implicit and which matters — then multiply by $v$.

Multi-head attention turns out to be a reshape rather than new mathematics. One linear layer each for queries, keys and values, then `.view(nbatches, -1, h, d_k).transpose(1, 2)` to fold the heads into the batch dimension so that one call to `attention()` does all eight at once, then transpose back and `.view(nbatches, -1, h*d_k)` into a final projection. Causal masking — stopping a position from seeing the future — is one line, `np.triu(np.ones((1, size, size)), k=1) == 0`, an upper-triangular block of zeros. The leading `1` broadcasts across the batch, and the `Batch` class ands it together with the padding mask so a single tensor carries both constraints.

The training scaffolding, which the paper compresses into a paragraph, gets the same treatment. `NoamOpt` wraps Adam with a learning rate of $\mathrm{lrate} = d_{\mathrm{model}}^{-0.5} \cdot \min\left(\mathrm{step}^{-0.5}, \mathrm{step} \cdot \mathrm{warmup}^{-1.5}\right)$ at 4000 warmup steps — it rises during warmup, then decays. `LabelSmoothing` is written honestly as a KL divergence against a target distribution that holds back 0.1 of its mass from the correct answer, rather than as a patched-up cross entropy. Batches are assembled by token count rather than sentence count, `BATCH_SIZE = 12000` tokens in the training script, against the 25000 source and target tokens the paper reports. A toy copy task trains in seconds, which means you can break each piece deliberately and watch what goes wrong — the fastest way anyone learns this architecture.

The post became the standard on-ramp, and its naming — `EncoderDecoder`, `SublayerConnection`, `PositionwiseFeedForward`, `subsequent_mask` — propagated into other codebases. Rush published it at NLP-OSS 2018 as an argument about reproduction: a paper can carry enough detail to be replicated and still be hard to get right, so the implementation belongs inside the paper.

> An executable paper pins down what prose leaves free: tensor rank, where the reshape happens, and what the mask actually contains.

**What did not survive:** the infrastructure. The real-data path used torchtext's field and iterator API and a `MultiGPULossCompute` over `nn.DataParallel`; both were superseded, and the loss class was reported to fail on batches too small to scatter across every device. A 2022 refresh by Huang, Subramanian, Sum, Almubarak and Biderman rewrote that layer. One discrepancy sits in the code itself: `SublayerConnection` computes `x + dropout(sublayer(norm(x)))`, normalising *before* the sublayer, while the paper text printed beside it specifies normalising after. The code is closer to what trains stably. It is not what the paper says.
