**Wojciech Zaremba, Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals · 2014 · [arXiv:1409.2329](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2329)**

By 2014 dropout was the standard regularizer for feedforward networks, and it did not work on recurrent ones. The practical consequence was a size ceiling: RNNs had to be kept small, because large ones memorized the training set. The paper's own baseline shows the cost — the best unregularized LSTM they trained on the Penn Treebank was two layers of 200 units, stopped after 13 epochs, at 114.5 test perplexity. Wider was not better, only faster to overfit. The prevailing explanation, from Bayer et al. (2013), was that recurrence amplifies dropout noise; their fix was marginalized dropout, a deterministic approximation rather than real sampling.

A stacked LSTM cell at layer `l`, timestep `t` reads two vectors: `h_t^{l-1}` from the layer below, and `h_{t-1}^l` from the same layer one step earlier. The change is to apply the dropout operator `D` to the first and leave the second untouched, so the gate pre-activations come from `T_{2n,4n}(D(h_t^{l-1}), h_{t-1}^l)`. Nothing else moves: the same gates and the same additive cell update described above, the same training loop.

The justification is a counting argument. Trace a signal from the input at step `t-2` to the prediction at step `t+2`: it climbs the layers, runs along the recurrent path, and exits. Only the vertical hops carry a mask, so the signal is "corrupted by the dropout operator exactly `L + 1` times", where `L` is depth — a count independent of how many timesteps it crossed. Standard dropout masks every recurrent hop as well, so corruption accumulates with sequence length, and anything the cell tries to hold for thirty steps is noise by the time it is read. Leaving the recurrent path clean buys regularization without charging for it in memory.

The numbers carried the argument. A 650-unit two-layer LSTM at 50% dropout, unrolled 35 steps, reached 82.7 test perplexity; a 1500-unit version at 65% dropout reached 78.4, against 114.5 unregularized and 100.0 for the best prior single model in their table. Averaging 38 large regularized LSTMs gave 68.7. The effect transferred: WMT'14 English–French BLEU moved 25.9 → 29.03 at dropout 0.2, and Icelandic acoustic frame accuracy rose 68.9 → 70.5 on validation while training accuracy fell 71.6 → 69.4, the signature of a regularizer doing its job. Pham et al. (2013) had reached the same recipe independently for handwriting; the contribution here is the demonstration across four domains, and 78.4 became the number later work reported against.

> Dropout is safe on the vertical connections and destructive on the horizontal ones, because only the horizontal ones accumulate masks as the sequence grows.

**What did not survive:** the rule itself. Gal and Ghahramani (2016) showed the diagnosis was right and the prescription too strong — the damage came from resampling a fresh mask every timestep, not from touching the recurrent path at all. Draw one mask per sequence, reuse it at every step, and dropout can go on the recurrent connections and the embeddings too: their large variational LSTM reached 75.2 test perplexity against 78.4, and 73.4 with Monte Carlo averaging over many masks at test time, which is expensive to run. The original paper also concedes more than its abstract suggests. On image captioning, dropout lifted BLEU from 23.5 to 24.3, while an ensemble of ten unregularized models scored 24.4 without it.
