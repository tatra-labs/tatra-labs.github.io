**Andrej Karpathy · 2015 · [karpathy.github.io/rnn-effectiveness](https://karpathy.github.io/2015/05/21/rnn-effectiveness/)**

Before 2015, well-formed output was something a model class was given rather than something it learned. n-gram language models with smoothing, HMMs, CRFs, parsers with a grammar bolted in: if the output had to have matched brackets or closed tags, the matching came from structure the modeller imposed. Recurrent networks existed and trained, but the working assumption was that a network fed nothing but raw characters would produce noise with the right letter frequencies. There was no shared sense of how much structure the next-symbol objective alone could recover.

The setup is deliberately plain. Fix a vocabulary of characters, encode each as a one-hot `x_t`, and run `h_t = tanh(W_hh h_{t-1} + W_xh x_t)`, then `y_t = W_hy h_t`, then a softmax over the vocabulary. The loss is cross-entropy against the character that actually came next. That is the entire supervision: one number per position, the log probability assigned to the true next character. Gradients come from backpropagation through time over a truncated window. No parse trees, no labels, no grammar. Generation runs the same network as a loop — seed it with a character, sample from the softmax, feed the sample back as the next input. A temperature divides the logits before the softmax; dropping it from 1 to 0.5 makes the samples more conservative. Karpathy released a roughly 100-line numpy version, the `min-char-rnn.py` gist, alongside the Torch implementation `char-rnn`.

The samples are the argument. 4.4MB of Shakespeare through a 3-layer RNN with 512 hidden units per layer yields speaker names and stage directions in the right places. 16MB of LaTeX source from a book on algebraic stacks and geometry yields lemmas, proofs, and commutative diagrams. 474MB of concatenated Linux kernel C through a 3-layer LSTM of roughly 10 million parameters yields functions with plausible indentation, GNU-style comment blocks, and braces that mostly close. A visualisation of individual hidden units found about 5% of them running something legible on their own — one cell that switches on inside quotation marks, another that tracks position within a line.

What changed was the status of the objective. Next-symbol prediction stopped looking like a proxy standing in for real supervision and started looking like the supervision. Syntax, long-range agreement, and register all fall out of being forced to guess character 4,001 given the first 4,000. The generation loop in the post is structurally the loop in a modern chat model, with subword tokens and attention swapped in for characters and a hidden state.

> Anything that makes the next symbol more predictable is something the model is paid to learn.

**What did not survive:** the results are softer than the retellings. The LaTeX only "almost compiles" — Karpathy and his labmate Justin Johnson patched it by hand, and `\begin{proof}` sometimes closed with `\end{lemma}`. The C uses variables that were never declared, declares variables it never uses, and returns names that do not exist. Nothing shown builds unedited. Character-level modelling later lost to subword tokenisation, and the vanilla RNN lost to the LSTM and then to the Transformer. The tidy "5% of cells are interpretable" picture has aged poorly too: later work found features mostly smeared across many units rather than owned by one. The objective survived; almost every implementation detail did not.
