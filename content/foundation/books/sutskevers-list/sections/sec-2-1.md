**Andrej Karpathy · 2015 · [karpathy.github.io/rnn-effectiveness](https://karpathy.github.io/2015/05/21/rnn-effectiveness/)**

Before 2015, well-formed output was something you *gave* a model rather than something it learned. If the output had to have matched brackets or closed tags, the matching came from structure the modeller had built in by hand — n-gram models with smoothing, HMMs, CRFs, parsers with a grammar bolted on. Recurrent networks existed and trained fine, but the working assumption was that a network fed nothing but raw characters would produce noise with roughly the right letter frequencies. Nobody had a shared sense of how much structure the plain next-symbol objective could recover on its own.

**The setup is deliberately plain, and it is worth walking through, because everything since is a variation on it.** Fix a vocabulary of characters. Encode each one as a vector that is all zeros except for a single 1 marking which character it is — a **one-hot** vector, written $x_t$. Then run two lines of arithmetic per step:

$$
h_t = \tanh(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t), \qquad y_t = W_{hy} h_t
$$

The vector $h_t$ is the network's memory, carried from one character to the next; $\tanh$ squashes it to keep the numbers in range. The scores $y_t$ then go through a **softmax**, which turns a list of arbitrary numbers into probabilities that sum to one. The loss is cross-entropy against the character that actually came next — in plain terms, the network is penalised by how much probability it failed to put on the right answer.

**That is the entire supervision: one number per position.** No parse trees, no labels, no grammar. Gradients come from backpropagation through time over a truncated window, which just means the error is traced back a fixed number of characters rather than all the way to the beginning. Generation runs the same network as a loop: seed it with a character, sample from the softmax, feed the sample back in as the next input. A **temperature** divides the scores before the softmax — drop it from 1 to 0.5 and the samples get more conservative, because the gap between likely and unlikely characters is exaggerated. Karpathy released a roughly 100-line numpy version, the `min-char-rnn.py` gist, alongside the Torch implementation `char-rnn`.

**The samples are the argument.** 4.4MB of Shakespeare through a 3-layer RNN with 512 hidden units per layer yields speaker names and stage directions in the right places. 16MB of LaTeX source from a book on algebraic stacks and geometry yields lemmas, proofs and commutative diagrams. 474MB of concatenated Linux kernel C through a 3-layer LSTM of roughly 10 million parameters yields functions with plausible indentation, GNU-style comment blocks, and braces that mostly close. A visualisation of individual hidden units found about 5% of them running something legible on their own — one cell that switches on inside quotation marks, another that tracks how far along a line it is.

What changed here was the *status* of the objective. Next-symbol prediction stopped looking like a cheap proxy standing in for real supervision and started looking like the supervision. Syntax, long-range agreement and register all fall out of being forced to guess character 4,001 given the first 4,000. The generation loop in this post is structurally the loop in a modern chat model, with subword tokens and attention swapped in for characters and a hidden state.

> Anything that makes the next symbol more predictable is something the model is paid to learn.

**What did not survive:** the results are softer than the retellings. The LaTeX only "almost compiles" — Karpathy and his labmate Justin Johnson patched it by hand, and `\begin{proof}` sometimes closed with `\end{lemma}`. The C uses variables that were never declared, declares variables it never uses, and returns names that do not exist. Nothing shown builds unedited. Character-level modelling later lost to subword tokenisation, and the vanilla RNN lost to the LSTM and then to the Transformer. The tidy "5% of cells are interpretable" picture has aged poorly too: later work found features mostly smeared across many units rather than owned by one. The objective survived; almost every implementation detail did not.
