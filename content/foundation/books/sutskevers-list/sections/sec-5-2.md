**Dario Amodei, Sundaram Ananthanarayanan, Rishita Anubhai, Jingliang Bai, Eric Battenberg et al. (Baidu Research) · 2015 (ICML 2016) · [arXiv:1512.02595](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.02595)**

A 2014 speech recognizer was a stack of separately trained parts. An acoustic model scored short frames against context-dependent phoneme states. A lexicon written by linguists mapped each word to phoneme sequences. An HMM handled alignment between frames and states. An n-gram language model and a decoder combined the scores. No stage optimized transcription accuracy directly. A new language meant a new phoneme inventory, a new lexicon, and new forced alignments before a single number improved.

Deep Speech 2 deleted the middle of that stack. The network reads a spectrogram and emits, at every output timestep, a distribution over the character alphabet plus a `blank` symbol. CTC defines the loss as the total probability of every frame-level symbol sequence that collapses to the target transcript — merge repeats, then drop blanks — summed by dynamic programming over the alignment lattice. Because the loss marginalizes over alignments, none is ever supplied: the label is the raw transcript string.

The stack is plain. The best English model puts three layers of 2D convolution, striding over time and frequency, under seven bidirectional recurrent layers and a fully connected output: roughly 100M parameters, with simple recurrent units, since GRUs stopped paying at that size. Two things made a network that deep trainable. Sequence-wise batch normalization computes each hidden unit's mean and variance over all minibatch items and all timesteps, not per timestep. SortaGrad orders the first epoch by the longest utterance in each minibatch, so short sequences come first; on the 9-layer model without batch norm it moved dev WER from 11.96 to 10.83. Training ran on 8 or 16 GPUs, synchronous SGD with a hand-written all-reduce — the exact-gradient discipline GPipe later carried across pipeline stages — over 11,940 hours of English and 9,400 hours of Mandarin, sustaining about 50 teraFLOP/s on 16 GPUs. That was a 7x speedup over the group's previous system, turning multi-week experiments into days.

Porting to Mandarin meant swapping the output layer to about 6,000 Chinese characters and the decoder to a character-level language model. The architecture did not change: no phoneme set, no lexicon, no tone model. On WSJ eval'92 the system reached 3.60 WER against 5.03 for Mechanical Turk transcribers, and 5.33 against 5.83 on LibriSpeech test-clean. Batch Dispatch grouped asynchronously arriving streams, giving a 98th-percentile compute latency of 67 ms under 10 simultaneous streams. It earns a place for treating training throughput as a research variable, not an ops detail, and for making on the model, three years early, the trade GPipe made on parallelism: generic mechanism in place of hand-built structure.

> Iteration speed is an architectural choice: the 7x systems speedup is what let a generic model out-search a hand-engineered pipeline.

**The caveat:** human parity held on clean read speech and nowhere else. On LibriSpeech test-other the system scored 13.25 against 12.69 for humans, and the human baseline was crowdworkers, not careful transcription. The model was also not the whole system: decoding still ran beam search against a 5-gram language model, so "end-to-end" stopped short of the output. Bidirectional recurrence cannot stream; the deployed system swapped in forward-only layers. Attention and transducer models displaced the RNN-plus-CTC stack within a few years.
