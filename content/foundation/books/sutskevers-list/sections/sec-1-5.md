**Scott Aaronson, Sean M. Carroll, Lauren Ouellette · 2014 · [arXiv:1405.6903](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.6903)**

Three years on, nobody had actually plotted the curve. Carroll had argued informally that some quantity rises and falls as the cream mixes, and Gell-Mann before him, but every measure on offer was unusable for one of two reasons. Some are maximised by noise — Boltzmann, Shannon and Kolmogorov entropy all rate the fully mixed cup as the most complex state, which is exactly backwards. The others, sophistication and logical depth included, have no way of being approximated on a real computer. This paper takes the one quantity that *can* be computed and runs the experiment.

**The recipe is to compress a blurred copy, and that one sentence is the whole idea.** Here is the setup. The state is an `n x n` grid of bits, `1` for cream and `0` for coffee, starting with the top half all `1`. Two mixing rules are tested. The interacting rule picks one neighbouring pair of differing cells per step and swaps them, so no cell ever holds more than one particle — this is the symmetric exclusion process. The non-interacting rule lets each cream particle wander independently, with any number allowed to pile into a cell.

Estimated entropy is just the `gzip` size of the raw grid. Estimated **apparent complexity** is the `gzip` size of a deliberately smeared copy: replace each cell by the average of the `g x g` square around it, sort those averages into three buckets — mostly coffee, mixed, mostly cream — and compress that instead. The blurring is doing the real work. It deletes randomness that happens at the scale of single cells, so the fully mixed end state collapses back to something short to describe, and only structure at the scale of the tendrils survives to be counted.

**Both models produced the rise-and-fall curve**, which is what the paper set out to show. Peak entropy scaled with the square of `n` (r² = 0.9999), peak complexity scaled linearly (r² = 0.9798, interacting), and time-to-peak scaled quadratically (r² = 0.9878, interacting).

The authors also proved something about the non-interacting case: its blurred image has complexity no greater than `log2(n) + log2(t) + O(1)`. The argument is that for a large enough blur, a standard concentration bound keeps every block close to its expected value, which leaves nothing but a smooth top-to-bottom gradient that can be recomputed from `n` and `t` alone. **Theory said there should be no bump there, and the experiment showed one** — so something was wrong with the measurement. The culprit was rounding at the cream-coffee border, where small fluctuations flip cells between buckets and manufacture fake detail. The patch used seven buckets instead of three and snapped each cell to its row's majority when it sat within one threshold of it. The non-interacting bump duly flattened. The interacting one held, and that is the paper's evidence.

What earns the paper its place is the recipe together with its named failure mode: complexity as compressed size after a smoothing pass, with the choice of smoothing carrying the entire distinction between structure and noise. The authors concede the choice is theirs, defended only on the grounds that squares of adjacent cells follow the way influence actually spreads in the automaton.

> Structure is whatever survives blurring, and the compressed size of the blurred state is a cheap way to weigh it.

**What did not survive: the experiment.** A week after posting, Brent Werness showed that the interacting automaton is not an example at all. It follows as a corollary of a 2009 result of Liggett on the symmetric exclusion process, with bounds controlling the interacting case just as tightly as the non-interacting control. The surviving bump was most likely the same border artifact, incompletely removed. Aaronson's update of 3 June 2014 says exactly this and calls it a bad choice of model system. arXiv still carries only v1, from 27 May 2014, with the original claim intact. And there is a deeper limit: compression can only ever bound Kolmogorov complexity from above, so no amount of running `gzip` could have proved complexity high in the first place.
