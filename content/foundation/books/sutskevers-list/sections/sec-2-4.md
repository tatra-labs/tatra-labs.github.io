**Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, Yoshua Bengio · 2014 · [arXiv:1409.0473](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473)**

The translation systems of 2014 worked in two stages. An **encoder** read the source sentence and boiled it down to one fixed-length vector; a **decoder** then generated the translation from that vector alone. In the baseline used here, encoder and decoder each have 1000 hidden units, which means a fifty-word sentence and a five-word sentence are both squeezed into the same 1000 numbers.

State that plainly and the problem is obvious: **the budget for the source does not grow with the source.** Cho et al. (2014) had already measured the damage — translation quality falls off sharply as the input gets longer, because the bottleneck is fixed and the sentence is not.

The fix, presented at ICLR 2015, has two halves.

**First, stop summarising.** The encoder becomes a bidirectional RNN — one pass left to right, one right to left, 1000 units each — and emits one vector per source *position*, $h_j = [\overrightarrow h_j ; \overleftarrow h_j]$, each carrying that word together with context from both sides. The encoder's output is now a variable-length sequence of $T_x$ vectors rather than a single summary. A long sentence gets more vectors.

**Second, let the decoder build its own summary fresh at every output step.** Before emitting word $i$, it does three things. It *scores* each source vector against its own current state $s_{i-1}$ using a small one-hidden-layer network, $e_{ij} = v_a^{\mathsf T} \tanh(W_a s_{i-1} + U_a h_j)$ — a learned measure of "how relevant is this source word to what I am about to say". It *normalizes* those scores across all source positions with a softmax, turning them into weights $\alpha_{ij}$ that sum to one. Then it takes the *weighted average* $c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} h_j$, and conditions on that instead of on one shared vector. Different output words look at different parts of the input. That is attention.

**What makes it work is that nothing is discrete.** Classical translation treats the source-to-target correspondence as a hidden variable to be inferred in a separate stage, with its own machinery. Here the weighted average is a smooth, differentiable operation, so gradients from the translation loss flow straight back into the scoring network, and alignment is learned jointly with translation under one objective. Nobody ever labels which word aligns to which. Note also that $U_a h_j$ does not depend on $i$, so it is computed once per sentence and reused.

On WMT'14 English–French — 348M words after data selection, a 30,000-word shortlist per language, tested on news-test-2014 — the old approach scores 17.82 BLEU at fifty words and the new one 26.75, or 28.45 trained to convergence. Moses, the strong non-neural system of the day, reaches 33.30 on the full test set. Restricted to sentences with no unknown words the ordering reverses: 36.15 for RNNsearch-50* against 35.63 for Moses, which also had an extra 418M-word monolingual corpus. Tellingly, the new model trained on short sentences (21.50) still beats the old one trained on long ones (17.82). The length curve flattens — no degradation past fifty words. And the weights $\alpha_{ij}$ are legible when you plot them, showing genuine reordering: *European Economic Area* into *zone économique européenne*, crossing over rather than marching left to right. Score, normalize, average: every attention variant since is that recipe with a different scoring function.

> A decoder that recomputes what it reads at every step never has to compress the whole input into one vector.

**What the paper got wrong:** it waved off its own cost. The scoring network "needs to be evaluated $T_x \times T_y$ times for each sentence pair" — once for every source position against every output position — which the authors judged tolerable because "most of input and output sentences are only 15–40 words". That product is now the binding constraint on context length and the target of most efficiency work in the field. The recurrent scaffolding around it did not survive either.
