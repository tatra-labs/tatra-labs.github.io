**Shane Legg · 2008 · [Machine Super Intelligence (dissertation)](https://www.vetta.org/documents/Machine_Super_Intelligence.pdf)**

The field had no definition of its subject that could be written as a formula — no scalar quantity a general agent could be said to maximise. The Turing test is pass/fail, anthropocentric, and depends on the judge; IQ tests assume a human is taking them. Benchmarks measure one task at a time: a chess engine tops the chess table and nothing else, and no benchmark says whether it or a maze solver is the more generally intelligent.

Legg's dissertation — University of Lugano, supervised by Marcus Hutter, research at IDSIA — supplies one. Fix the usual loop: an agent `π` emits actions, an environment `μ` returns observations and rewards. Value is expected total reward, `V^π_μ := E(Σ r_i)`. The space `E` holds the computable environments whose total returned reward is bounded by `1` — Legg calls them reward-summable. That bound forces `V^π_μ ≤ 1` and removes the discount factor, so scores compare across environments of any length. Universal intelligence is that value averaged over all of `E`, weighted by description length: `Υ(π) := Σ_{μ∈E} 2^-K(μ) V^π_μ`, where `K(μ)` is the shortest program computing `μ` on a reference machine. The weighting is Solomonoff's universal prior moved from sequences to environments; the sum equals `V^π_ξ`, the value under the universal mixture `ξ`. Simple worlds dominate; complicated ones are discounted exponentially. An agent that fails the simple environments scores near zero however well it handles elaborate ones.

The second half closes the loop. Strip the discounting out of Hutter's AIXI and define it over reward-bounded environments, and it becomes the agent maximising `Υ` by construction: at each step it weights every program consistent with the history by `2^-length` and takes the action with the highest expected reward under that mixture. Solomonoff induction with an expectimax on top. Legg sets the ceiling as `Ῡ := max_π Υ(π) = Υ(π^ξ)`, and describes his chapter as an alternate characterisation of Hutter's intelligence order relation rather than a fresh optimality claim.

Here the compression thread stops being a claim about training objectives and becomes one about intelligence itself. Both halves are Kolmogorov complexity: a description-length prior over environments, and universal induction as the policy. MDL says a good model is a short description of the data; Legg says a general agent does well in worlds with short descriptions. Two years later he co-founded DeepMind with Demis Hassabis and Mustafa Suleyman, which is why the thesis is still cited.

> Intelligence, formalised, is expected reward across all computable environments weighted by `2^-K` — the same compression prior, applied to worlds instead of data.

**What did not survive:** `Υ` is incomputable, and not in a way engineering closes: `K` is incomputable and `E` is infinite. His earlier result (*Is There an Elegant Universal Theory of Prediction?*, ALT 2006, Chapter 5 here) shows that computable predictors general enough to handle every sequence below a moderate complexity bound must themselves be highly complex, and that past a certain point no consistent formal system can prove one powerful. `K(μ)` is defined only up to a machine-dependent constant, so the ranking of any finite set of agents depends on the reference machine. Evaluation went to benchmark suites instead, and Chollet's ARC took the opposite branch: human-like priors and skill-acquisition efficiency over universality.
