**Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish, Tom Henighan, Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Chess, Rewon Child, Scott Gray, Alec Radford, Jeffrey Wu, Dario Amodei · 2020 · [arXiv:2001.08361](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)**

GPipe and Deep Speech 2 make a cluster usable. Neither says how large a model to put on it. Before this paper that choice was craft knowledge. Everyone knew larger models did better, but nobody could say by how much, or whether a fixed budget was better spent on more parameters, more tokens, or more steps. Effort went into architecture search — depth against width, head counts, activation functions — on the assumption that the shape of the network was where the gains lived. A team could not predict the final loss of a run before doing the run.

Kaplan et al. trained decoder-only transformers on WebText2 across a deliberately wide grid: `768` to `1.5B` non-embedding parameters, datasets from `22M` to `23B` tokens. The method is a controlled sweep. Vary one of `N`, `D`, or `C` while keeping the other two large enough not to bottleneck the result, then fit the cross-entropy test loss. Each fit comes out a clean power law: `L(N) = (N_c/N)^0.076`, `L(D) = (D_c/D)^0.095`, `L(C_min) = (C_c/C_min)^0.050`, with some trends holding over more than seven orders of magnitude. The exponents are small, which is the substance of the claim — a tenfold increase in parameters multiplies the fitted loss by roughly `0.84`, so progress is real, steady, and expensive.

Two secondary results carried as much weight. First, architecture barely registered: within a wide range, depth and width traded off with minimal effect on loss at fixed parameter count. Shape was noise; size was signal. Second, the paper combined the fits into an allocation rule. Given extra compute, it recommended `N ∝ C^0.73` and only `D ∝ C^0.27` — spend most of a new budget on a bigger model, feed it a modest amount of data, and stop well short of convergence, because large models reach any given loss in fewer steps and fewer tokens than small ones.

The effect was to convert training from an experimental art into a forecasting problem. Fit curves on a ladder of small runs, extrapolate, commit the cluster. That is now standard practice; the scaling plot is a routine pre-flight artifact, not a research result. It also justified eight figures on a single run, since the loss at the far end was predictable rather than hoped for.

> Loss falls as a smooth power law in scale, and the fitted exponents are small enough that the only reliable path to a better model is a much larger budget.

**What did not survive:** the allocation rule. Hoffmann et al. (2022, Chinchilla) trained over 400 models from `70M` to over `16B` parameters on `5B` to `500B` tokens and found parameters and data should scale roughly equally — double one, double the other. Chinchilla at `70B` parameters, trained on 4x Gopher's data at the same compute, beat Gopher (`280B`) across the board and reached `67.5%` on MMLU. Pearce and Song (TMLR 2024) traced most of the gap to Kaplan counting non-embedding rather than total parameters at small scale, where embeddings are a large share of the total. The functional form held. The recommendation it produced was wrong, and models built on it were undertrained.
