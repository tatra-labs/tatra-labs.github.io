# 16.7 The Deep Learning Approach to Structured Probabilistic Models

The chapter closes by describing how deep learning's use of graphical models differs from the tradition's,
and the differences are consistent enough to amount to a different discipline.

## Three differences

**Deep learning uses many latent variables and does not interpret them.** The traditional approach uses a
small number of latent variables with intended meanings — a variable representing whether the patient has
the flu. Deep learning uses very large numbers of latent variables with no individual interpretation, and
the book is explicit that no attempt is made to give them one. What matters is that the ensemble captures
the structure, not that any unit is nameable.

**The graphs are dense, not sparse.** The traditional emphasis is on sparse connectivity, which is what
makes exact inference tractable. Deep models connect entire layers to entire layers, so a latent unit is
connected to every visible unit. The book puts the point bluntly: the connections are **not sparse in the
graph-theoretic sense**, and efficiency comes instead from using efficient matrix operations and
distributed computation on the dense structure.

**Loopy belief propagation is almost never used.** The traditional workhorse for approximate inference on
graphs with cycles is essentially absent from deep learning. The reason given is that deep models rely on
distributed representations, and the interactions are strong and dense enough that message passing works
poorly; Gibbs sampling and variational inference are used instead.

## The example

The **restricted Boltzmann machine** is offered as the canonical illustration, and Chapter 20 develops it.
It is an undirected bipartite model with a layer of visible units and a layer of hidden units, no
connections within a layer, and every connection between them. That structure is chosen for a specific
reason: because the hidden units are conditionally independent given the visible ones and vice versa, both
conditional distributions factorise and can be computed in a single matrix operation. It is a graphical
model designed around what hardware does well.

## My take

The RBM's design principle is the most instructive thing in the section. Its bipartite structure is not
motivated by anything about the data; it is motivated by making a conditional distribution computable in
one matrix multiply. That is an architecture chosen for the machine rather than for the problem, and it is
the same logic that later produced attention — a mechanism whose defining property is that it reduces to
dense matrix multiplication.

The density point is the one that most clearly separates the two traditions, and it is worth stating as a
reversal. The entire graphical-model programme is built on the idea that **sparsity makes inference
tractable**. Deep learning abandons sparsity and accepts that inference is intractable, then either avoids
needing it — by using directed models that require no inference to sample, per 16.3 — or approximates it
crudely. Dense computation on a parallel machine turned out to be worth more than tractable inference on
a sparse graph.

Read as the closing of Chapter 16, this section is an admission that the formalism the chapter carefully
built is used by deep learning mostly as **vocabulary**. It supplies the language for saying what a model
assumes — latent variables, factorisation, conditional independence, the partition function — and almost
none of its algorithms. That is not a small contribution, and it is much smaller than the chapter's length
implies.
