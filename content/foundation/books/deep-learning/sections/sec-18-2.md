# 18.2 Stochastic Maximum Likelihood and Contrastive Divergence

Given that the negative phase requires samples from the model, the obvious approach is to run an MCMC
chain at every gradient step. This section explains why that is too slow and gives the two approximations
that made training possible at all.

## The naive algorithm

At each gradient step, initialise a set of Markov chains from a random distribution, burn them in until
they mix, and use the resulting samples for the negative phase. The book's judgement is that the cost is
prohibitive: burning in a chain from scratch on every update multiplies the cost of training by the
mixing time.

## Contrastive divergence

**CD-$k$** initialises the chain at the **data distribution** rather than at random, then runs $k$ steps —
often $k = 1$. Since the data distribution is already close to the model distribution once training has
progressed, the burn-in is short.

The book is clear about what this costs. CD is **biased**, because the chain has not converged. Its
characteristic failure is **spurious modes**: regions far from the data where the model assigns high
probability, which the short chain never reaches and which the negative phase therefore never suppresses.
The model is never told those regions are wrong.

CD is described as useful for pretraining shallow models to be stacked, and less suitable for training
deep models directly.

## Stochastic maximum likelihood

**SML**, also called **persistent contrastive divergence** (PCD), fixes the initialisation differently.
Rather than restarting the chain at every gradient step, it keeps a **persistent** set of chains and
continues them from where the previous step left off.

The justification is that the parameters change only slightly per step, so the model distribution changes
only slightly, and the chain's current state remains a good starting point. The chains explore
continuously across the whole run rather than being repeatedly reset.

The book notes SML is more accurate than CD and is vulnerable if the learning rate is large enough that
the model moves faster than the chain can follow, and that a common practical check is to inspect the
samples and the weights for signs that the chain has fallen behind.

## My take

The spurious-mode failure of CD is worth understanding, because it is the clearest illustration of what
the negative phase is for. Maximum likelihood works by suppressing probability wherever the model puts it
and the data does not. If the sampler never visits a region, that region is never suppressed, and the
model can accumulate arbitrarily much mass in places it was never contradicted. A generative model's
quality depends on the sampler's coverage as much as on the objective.

Persistent chains are the better idea and the reasoning generalises. The assumption is that the target
changes slowly relative to the sampler, so state can be reused across steps rather than recomputed. The
same reasoning justifies replay buffers in reinforcement learning and momentum-updated key encoders in
contrastive learning — both are persistent caches of samples from a slowly-moving distribution, and both
break for the same reason SML does, when the model moves faster than the cache can track.

Historically these two algorithms are what made restricted Boltzmann machines trainable, and therefore
what made the 2006 deep belief network results possible. They were the practical enabler of the third
wave, and they are essentially unused today.
