# 18.1 The Log-Likelihood Gradient

Chapter 18 is about one number. This section shows why it cannot be ignored and what its gradient looks
like.

## The decomposition

An undirected model is defined by an unnormalised density $\tilde p(x; \theta)$ divided by the partition
function $Z(\theta)$. Taking logarithms and differentiating splits the gradient in two:

$$
\nabla_{\theta} \log p(x; \theta) = \nabla_{\theta} \log \tilde p(x; \theta) - \nabla_{\theta} \log Z(\theta)
$$

The book names the two terms. The first is the **positive phase**, which pushes up the unnormalised
probability of the observed data. The second is the **negative phase**, which pushes down the probability
everywhere the model currently puts mass.

## The negative phase

The negative phase is where the difficulty lives. Differentiating $Z$ and dividing through gives

$$
\nabla_{\theta} \log Z = \mathbb E_{x \sim p(x)}\left[ \nabla_{\theta} \log \tilde p(x) \right]
$$

an expectation **under the model's own distribution**. Computing it requires samples from the model, which
is precisely what Chapter 17 established to be expensive and unreliable for these models.

The book notes the derivation holds for discrete and continuous variables under mild conditions, and that
for models where $Z$ is tractable — where the normalising constant can be computed in closed form — none
of this arises.

The intuitive reading: **maximum likelihood raises the probability of the data and lowers the probability
of everything the model believes**, and the second half requires knowing what the model believes.

## My take

The positive/negative phase framing is the most portable idea in the chapter, and it describes far more
than Boltzmann machines.

**Contrastive learning** is this structure exactly: pull the representation of a positive pair together,
push negative pairs apart. The negatives play the role of the negative phase, and choosing them well is
the central engineering problem — which is the same problem as sampling from the model, in a different
costume. **GAN training** has the same shape, with the discriminator supplying the negative phase and the
generator producing the samples. **Noise-contrastive estimation** in 18.6 makes the correspondence
explicit by replacing model samples with samples from a fixed noise distribution.

Seen this way, the question the chapter asks is one that never went away: *where do the negative examples
come from?* Sampling from the model is principled and slow. Sampling from a fixed noise distribution is
fast and gives a weaker learning signal. Sampling from a learned adversary is powerful and unstable.
Every method in Chapters 18 and 20 is one of those three answers.

The reason autoregressive models won is that they never face the question. A normalised conditional
distribution over a small vocabulary has an exactly computable partition function — the softmax
denominator — so the negative phase is a sum over tokens, not an expectation requiring samples. The whole
of Chapter 18 is a tax that directed models do not pay.
