# 11.3 Determining Whether to Gather More Data

A decision procedure, and one of the most immediately usable pages in the book. More data is usually the
most reliable improvement available — and it is also expensive, so the question is whether it will help.

## The procedure

**First, check training performance.** If the model performs poorly on its own training set, gathering
more data will not help — the model is not using what it already has. Increase capacity by adding layers
or units, tune the learning rate, and check for defects in the software. If training performance remains
poor after that, the book's suggestion is that the problem may be the *quality* of the data: it may be
too noisy, or it may lack the inputs needed to predict the outputs. That points at starting over rather
than at collecting more of the same.

**If training performance is acceptable, measure test performance.** A small gap means the model is fine
and the work is done. A large gap means overfitting, and gathering more data is the most effective single
remedy — with the alternatives being to reduce model size, improve regularisation, or tune
hyperparameters, all of which are cheaper and less reliable.

## How much more

If more data is to be gathered, the useful question is how much. Adding a handful of examples will not
move a curve. The book's recommendation is to plot **generalisation error against training set size on a
logarithmic scale**, extrapolate the trend, and read off the size needed to reach the target. In practice
the increments that matter are multiplicative — doubling, or an order of magnitude — not additive.

Where more data cannot be obtained, the remaining option is to reduce the error some other way, which
returns the problem to Chapter 7.

## My take

The ordering is the whole value here, and it inverts what people usually do. The instinct on seeing poor
test accuracy is to get more data; the correct first move is to look at the *training* accuracy, because
it distinguishes two problems with opposite remedies. A model underfitting its training set will not
improve with more examples, and a team that spends a quarter collecting them will have learned nothing.

This maps directly onto the bias/variance vocabulary of 5.4 and is more actionable in this form. High
training error is a bias problem — fix the model. Low training error with high test error is a variance
problem — fix the data or the regularisation.

The log-scale extrapolation is the part that grew into something larger than the book imagined. That
curve, taken seriously and measured across orders of magnitude, is a **scaling law**, and the practice of
fitting it to predict the performance of a model that has not been trained yet is now how large training
runs are planned. The advice here is to use it to decide whether to buy more data; the same curve is now
used to decide how to divide a fixed budget between data and parameters. Same measurement, much larger
consequence.

Worth keeping the data-quality remark, which is easy to skip. If training error stays high after capacity
and tuning have been addressed, the inputs may simply not contain the answer. No amount of modelling
recovers information that was never collected, and recognising that early is worth more than any
technique in this book.
