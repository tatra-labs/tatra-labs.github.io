# 18.6 Noise-Contrastive Estimation

The third strategy for avoiding the partition function, and the one with the most direct descendants in
current practice. Rather than cancelling $Z$ or differentiating it away, NCE **estimates it as a
parameter**.

## The construction

Write the model as

$$
\log p_{\text{model}}(x) = \log \tilde p_{\text{model}}(x; \theta) + c
$$

where $c$ is a scalar standing in for $-\log Z$, treated as an ordinary parameter to be learned rather
than computed. Maximum likelihood cannot fit this — it would drive $c$ to infinity — so a different
objective is needed.

NCE supplies one by turning density estimation into **binary classification**. Introduce a noise
distribution $p_{\text{noise}}$ that is easy to sample from and to evaluate, and construct a joint model
over a data point and a switch variable $y$ indicating whether the point came from the data or from the
noise. Train a classifier to distinguish the two.

Because the classifier's optimal solution depends on the ratio of the two densities, and the noise density
is known, the learned discriminator determines the data density — including its normalisation, since $c$
is learned along with everything else. Sampling from the model is never required.

## The limitation

The book identifies the constraint that bounds the method: the noise distribution must be **easy to
evaluate and sample**, and it must be close enough to the data distribution for the classification problem
to be hard. If the two are easily separable the task is trivial and no useful learning signal is produced.
For high-dimensional data, finding such a noise distribution is difficult, and the book notes the method is
most successful when applied to problems with few random variables.

**Self-contrastive estimation** is mentioned as the variant where the noise distribution is the model's own
current parameters — which makes the gradient resemble maximum likelihood's, and connects the method back
to the negative phase of 18.1.

## My take

NCE is the ancestor of an enormous amount of modern practice, and the lineage is worth tracing because it
is not obvious from the presentation here.

**Word2vec's negative sampling** is NCE with a simplification: sample a few words from a unigram noise
distribution and train the model to distinguish them from the true context word. The entire embedding
revolution of Section 12.4 rests on this trick, adopted because it avoided the expensive softmax over the
vocabulary — which is the same partition function problem in a directed model.

**Contrastive self-supervised learning** — SimCLR, MoCo, CLIP — is NCE with the noise distribution
replaced by other examples in the batch. The InfoNCE objective is this section's construction with
multiple negatives, and the book's warning applies directly: if the negatives are too easy the signal
vanishes, which is why hard negative mining and large batch sizes matter so much in that literature.

The self-contrastive remark is the connection to GANs, made explicit two chapters later. A discriminator
separating real data from generated samples is NCE with a *learned* noise distribution, and the difficulty
the book names — that the noise must stay close enough to the data to make the problem hard — becomes, in
that setting, the entire training dynamic.
