# 18.3 Pseudolikelihood

The first of several methods that avoid the partition function entirely rather than approximating it. The
trick is that $Z$ cancels in a ratio.

## The idea

Consider a conditional probability under an undirected model:

$$
p\left( x_i \mid x_{-i} \right) = \frac{\tilde p(x)}{\sum_{x_i} \tilde p(x)}
$$

Both numerator and denominator carry the same factor $1/Z$, so it cancels. A conditional distribution
requires only a sum over the values of **one** variable, which is cheap, where the joint requires a sum
over all configurations of all variables.

**Pseudolikelihood** exploits this by replacing the joint likelihood with the product of the
per-variable conditionals:

$$
\log p(x) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p\left( x_i \mid x_{-i} \right)
$$

This is not the likelihood — it is a different objective — but the book reports it is **asymptotically
consistent**: with enough data and a correctly specified model, maximising it recovers the same parameters
as maximum likelihood.

**Generalised pseudolikelihood** interpolates between the two by conditioning blocks of variables jointly,
trading cost against fidelity.

## Where it works and where it does not

The book's assessment is specific. Pseudolikelihood performs **poorly on tasks requiring a good model of
the full joint distribution**, such as density estimation and sampling, and performs **better than
maximum likelihood on tasks requiring only the conditionals**, such as filling in missing values or
classification.

It is also noted to be incompatible with methods that only provide a lower bound on $\tilde p$ —
variational approaches among them — because pseudolikelihood needs the unnormalised probability in the
denominator as well as the numerator, and a lower bound there gives an upper bound on the objective, which
is useless.

Its cost is high in a different way: evaluating the objective requires $n$ conditional evaluations per
example rather than one.

## My take

The task-dependence is the honest and interesting finding. An objective that optimises conditionals
produces a model good at conditionals and mediocre at the joint, which sounds obvious and is routinely
forgotten. It is a specific instance of a general rule worth carrying: **a model is good at what its
objective asked for, and the objective is rarely what was wanted.**

The technique itself is not used in deep learning, but the observation that $Z$ cancels in a conditional
is used constantly, and it is the reason Gibbs sampling in 17.4 is possible. It is also worth seeing that
pseudolikelihood is close in spirit to masked prediction — model each variable given the others — which is
what BERT does. The difference is that BERT masks a subset and predicts them from the rest with a directed
parameterisation, so there is no $Z$ to cancel in the first place, and no consistency argument is needed
because nobody is claiming to recover a joint distribution.

That comparison is a fair summary of Chapter 18's fate. The methods here are clever ways to train a model
whose normalising constant is intractable. The field's answer was to stop building such models.
