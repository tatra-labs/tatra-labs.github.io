# 14.7 Contractive Autoencoders

The third regularised autoencoder, and the most explicit about what it wants: a representation that
changes as little as possible when the input changes.

## The penalty

The contractive autoencoder adds a penalty on the squared Frobenius norm of the encoder's Jacobian:

$$
\Omega(h) = \lambda \left\lVert \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x} \right\rVert_F^2
$$

the sum of squared partial derivatives of every code unit with respect to every input. Minimising it makes
the encoder **contractive** — it maps a neighbourhood of the input to a smaller neighbourhood of the code
space, shrinking distances.

## Relation to denoising

The book states the connection: in the limit of small Gaussian corruption noise, the denoising
reconstruction error is equivalent to a contractive penalty on the reconstruction function $g(f(x))$. The
difference is where the penalty lands — the denoising autoencoder makes the whole reconstruction resistant
to perturbation, while the contractive autoencoder makes the **feature extraction function** resistant.
The authors describe the CAE as making the feature-extraction function resist infinitesimal perturbations
and the DAE as making the reconstruction function resist finite-sized ones.

## What it learns

The trade-off is the one from 14.6. The contractive penalty pulls toward a constant function; the
reconstruction error prevents that. The result is a mapping that is contractive in most directions and
expansive only in the few that are needed — which are the tangent directions of the data manifold. The
book notes the singular value spectrum of the learned Jacobian is informative: most singular values are
near zero and a few are large, and the number of large ones estimates the manifold's local dimension.

## The costs

Two practical problems are named. The Jacobian penalty is **cheap for a single hidden layer** and
expensive for deep encoders, because it requires derivatives of every code unit with respect to every
input; the book's suggested remedy is to train a stack of single-layer autoencoders separately. And the
contraction penalty can be **cheated** by an encoder that shrinks its outputs while the decoder scales
them back up, so the two must be tied — the decoder's weights set to the transpose of the encoder's — to
prevent it.

## My take

The cheating problem is the interesting one, because it is a general failure of penalties applied to
intermediate quantities. Any penalty on the magnitude of something the network also controls the scale of
can be satisfied by rescaling rather than by changing behaviour, and the remedy — weight tying — is a
constraint added specifically to close the loophole. The same pattern appears wherever a normalisation or
penalty is applied mid-network, and it is worth looking for.

The singular-value result is the most attractive part and the reason this method is remembered. A trained
CAE reports the local dimension of the data manifold, which is a genuinely useful measurement and one that
nothing else in the book provides. It is the basis of the manifold tangent classifier in 7.14.

Contractive autoencoders are not used. The cost is the reason — a Jacobian penalty needs gradients of
gradients, which is expensive and awkward, and the payoff over denoising is small. The book's own analysis
predicts this: if DAE and CAE are equivalent in the small-noise limit and the DAE achieves it by simply
adding noise, then noise wins on cost. It is another instance of the chapter's pattern, where the
analytically principled method loses to the sampled approximation of itself.
