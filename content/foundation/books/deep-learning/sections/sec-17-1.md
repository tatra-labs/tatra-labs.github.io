# 17.1 Sampling and Monte Carlo Methods

Chapter 17 gives the tool used whenever a sum or integral is intractable: replace it with an average over
samples.

## Why sample

Two reasons the book distinguishes. Sampling can be a cheap **approximation** to an expensive exact
computation, and it can be the **goal itself** — training a model that generates data means being able to
draw from it.

## The estimator

An intractable sum or integral written as an expectation,

$$
s = \mathbb E_{x \sim p}\left[ f(x) \right]
$$

is approximated by drawing $n$ independent samples from $p$ and averaging:

$$
\hat s_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f\left( x^{(i)} \right)
$$

Three properties justify this. The estimator is **unbiased** — its expectation equals $s$ for any $n$. By
the law of large numbers it **converges** to $s$ almost surely as $n$ grows, provided the variance of $f$
is finite. And its variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left[ \hat s_n \right] = \frac{\operatorname{Var}\left[ f(x) \right]}{n}
$$

so the standard error falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$. The central limit theorem then says the estimate is
approximately normal around the true value, which permits confidence intervals.

The book notes the requirement that makes this all work and that is often unavailable: it must be possible
to sample from $p$ in the first place. When it is not, the alternatives are importance sampling in 17.2
and Markov chain methods in 17.3.

## My take

The $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate is the number to internalise, and it is the same rate that appeared in Section 5.4
for estimator standard error and in 8.1 for minibatch gradients — three apparently unrelated results that
are one result about averaging.

It is also the reason Monte Carlo methods are used despite being slow. The rate does not depend on the
dimension. A quadrature rule that discretises each axis costs exponentially more as dimensions are added;
a Monte Carlo estimate costs the same at any dimension, with the dimension entering only through the
variance of $f$. That independence is what makes sampling the only viable approach in high dimensions, and
it is worth stating explicitly since the book leaves it implicit.

The practical corollary is that reducing variance is worth far more than taking more samples. Going from
$n$ to $4n$ halves the error; halving the variance of $f$ does the same for free. Every technique in this
chapter and much of the machinery in Chapter 20 — importance sampling, control variates, the
reparameterisation trick — is variance reduction rather than sample-count increase, and this equation is
why.
