# 9.11 Convolutional Networks and the History of Deep Learning

Chapter 9 closes with a historical claim: convolutional networks were the bridge that carried neural
networks through the period when nothing else worked.

## The list of firsts

The book sets out what convolutional networks did before anything else did.

They were among the **first deep models to perform well**, at a time when fully connected deep networks
were widely believed to be untrainable. They were the **first neural networks to solve important
commercial problems** — LeNet reading handwritten digits, and the book notes that by the 1990s a system
deployed by NEC was reading a significant share of all cheques in the United States. They were the first
to be successfully trained with back-propagation at any depth, and among the first to be used at scale in
competition, from 2012 onward.

The reason offered for why they worked when others did not is the one Chapter 9 has been building toward:
the architecture's constraints make the optimisation problem easier and the statistical problem smaller.
Sparse connectivity, parameter sharing and pooling reduce both the number of parameters and the effective
depth of the credit-assignment path.

The book adds a point about historical accident. Convolutional networks continued to receive attention
during the 1990s and 2000s partly because they *worked* commercially, which sustained a research
community through a period when funding for neural networks generally had collapsed. They were, in the
authors' framing, what kept the field alive between the second and third waves.

## My take

The claim that convolutional networks were the survivors is well supported and slightly self-serving in
its emphasis, since it credits architecture for what was also a matter of the tasks available. Digit
recognition happened to be a commercially valuable problem that was small enough to solve with 1990s
hardware, and having a paying application is what keeps a research line alive during a winter. LeNet's
significance is as much economic as scientific.

The deeper point survives, though, and it is the one to take from the chapter as a whole. Convolutional
networks demonstrated that **the way to make a hard learning problem tractable is to constrain the model
with what you know about the data**, rather than to search harder over an unconstrained space. That
lesson generalised past vision: recurrence for sequences, and later attention for everything, are the
same move made with different knowledge.

It is worth being clear-eyed about where convolutional networks stand now. They have been displaced at the
frontier of vision by transformers on large datasets, and the hybrid architectures that remain competitive
mostly reintroduce convolutional stems for the local structure while using attention for the global. The
prior that Chapter 9 spends fifty pages defending is still correct — images really are translation
equivariant and locally structured — but a model with enough data can learn that from scratch and then
learn the exceptions too. Which is exactly what Section 9.4 predicted would happen.
