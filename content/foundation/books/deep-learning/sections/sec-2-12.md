# 2.12 Example: Principal Components Analysis

The chapter closes by deriving a real algorithm from the tools it has assembled. PCA is chosen because
the derivation is short, uses almost every previous section, and produces a result that appears again in
Chapters 5, 13 and 14.

## The problem, stated as compression

Given $m$ points $x^{(1)}, \ldots, x^{(m)}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, find a lossy encoding into $\mathbb{R}^l$
with $l < n$. The book fixes the decoder to be linear,

$$
g(c) = D c
$$

with $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times l}$ constrained to have orthonormal columns. Two constraints make the
problem well posed: the columns are unit-norm (otherwise scale is ambiguous, since shrinking $D$ and
inflating $c$ leaves the product unchanged) and orthogonal.

## Deriving the encoder

With the decoder fixed, the optimal code minimises the squared reconstruction error, so
$c^{\ast} = \arg\min_c \lVert x - g(c) \rVert_2^2$. Expanding the square, dropping the term without $c$,
and using $D^{\top} D = I_l$ leaves a problem whose gradient vanishes at

$$
c = D^{\top} x
$$

The encoder is the transpose of the decoder. No search is needed — a consequence purely of orthonormal
columns.

## Deriving the decoder

Substituting back, the reconstruction is $r(x) = D D^{\top} x$, and $D$ is chosen to minimise the
Frobenius norm of the error over the whole dataset. Working through the trace identities of 2.10, the
objective becomes a maximisation of $\operatorname{Tr}(D^{\top} X^{\top} X D)$ subject to
$D^{\top} D = I_l$. The solution is the $l$ eigenvectors of $X^{\top} X$ with the largest eigenvalues —
equivalently, by 2.8, the top $l$ right-singular vectors of $X$.

## My take

The framing is doing more work than the algorithm. PCA is presented not as "find the directions of
maximum variance" but as "find the best linear autoencoder", and that is the framing the rest of the book
builds on: Chapter 14 opens by observing that an undercomplete autoencoder with linear activations and
squared error spans the same subspace PCA does. Read this way, Chapter 2 ends with the simplest possible
member of the model family the book is really about.

Two limits worth naming while the derivation is fresh. PCA is linear, so it cannot represent a curved
manifold — which is the motivation for Section 13.5 and for autoencoders generally. And it is defined by
squared error, which makes it a maximum-likelihood fit under a Gaussian noise assumption; Section 13.1
makes that explicit as probabilistic PCA.
