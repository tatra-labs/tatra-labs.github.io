# 5.11 Challenges Motivating Deep Learning

The chapter closes with the argument the whole book rests on: why the classical methods of 5.7 fail on
the problems deep learning is aimed at. Three challenges, each a different view of the same difficulty.

## The curse of dimensionality

As the number of dimensions rises, the number of distinct configurations grows exponentially. Divide each
of $d$ dimensions into $v$ cells and there are $v^d$ regions. With any realistic dataset, almost all of
those regions contain no training examples at all — and an algorithm that answers a query by looking at
what is nearby has nothing to look at.

## The local constancy prior

Classical methods work by assuming the target function is smooth: $f(x) \approx f(x + \epsilon)$ for
small $\epsilon$. Nearest neighbours, kernel machines with a local kernel, and decision trees all embody
this in different forms.

The book's objection is a counting argument. To distinguish $O(k)$ regions, a purely local method needs
$O(k)$ examples — one per region, since nothing constrains the function between them. So learning a
function with many variations requires a number of examples proportional to the number of variations, and
in high dimensions the number of variations of interest is exponential.

The book's checkerboard illustration is the clean version: no local method can generalise to squares it
has not seen, because local smoothness says nothing about what happens across a boundary.

## Manifold learning

The escape is that real data does not fill its space. Images, sounds and text occupy a **manifold** — a
connected region of much lower dimension than the ambient space, along which the data varies smoothly.
The evidence the book offers is direct: sample a uniformly random image and you get noise, essentially
never a natural scene, so natural images are a vanishingly small subset of pixel space. And real examples
are connected to one another by continuous transformations — dim the light, rotate the object, age the
face.

If the data lies on a low-dimensional manifold, the effective dimension is the manifold's, not the
ambient space's, and the counting argument loses its force.

## My take

The chapter's real claim is not that deep networks are more powerful but that they make a **different
prior assumption**, and that the difference is the whole story. Local smoothness says nearby inputs have
similar outputs. Depth and distributed representation say instead that the data was generated by
composing a moderate number of factors, and that a function built by composing layers can therefore
describe exponentially many regions with a linear number of parameters. Section 6.4.1 and Section 15.5
make the argument quantitatively.

The manifold hypothesis has held up unusually well for an assumption originally supported by a sampling
argument. Latent diffusion, which compresses images into a much smaller latent space before modelling
them, works precisely because the manifold is far lower-dimensional than the pixel grid. Interpolation
along that latent space producing plausible intermediate images is the connectedness claim being
demonstrated directly.

Where the framing needs updating is the implicit assumption that the manifold is low-dimensional in an
absolute sense. It is low-dimensional *relative to pixel space*, which is a much weaker statement — and
the reason the models that succeeded still needed hundreds of millions of parameters rather than the
handful the manifold picture might suggest.
