# 10.3 Bidirectional RNNs

A short section fixing an obvious limitation: the recurrent networks so far look only backwards, and many
tasks need context from both directions.

## The construction

A causal recurrent network computes $h^{(t)}$ from $x^{(1)}$ through $x^{(t)}$. That is the right
structure when the output must depend only on the past — as in real-time generation — and the wrong one
otherwise.

A **bidirectional RNN** runs two separate recurrences over the same sequence: one forward, producing
$h^{(t)}$, and one backward, producing $g^{(t)}$. The output at each step depends on both, so it has
access to the entire input while remaining most sensitive to values near $t$.

The book's motivating examples are exactly the cases where the disambiguating evidence arrives late:
speech recognition, where the correct phoneme may depend on the next few phonemes because of
coarticulation and on the next few *words* because of grammar; handwriting recognition; and many other
sequence-to-sequence tasks.

The idea extends beyond one dimension. For images, four recurrences — up, down, left and right — give a
model where each output depends on the whole image, with a bias toward local information. The book notes
this is typically more expensive than a convolutional network but allows long-range lateral interactions
within a feature map.

## My take

The forward/backward split names a distinction that outlived the architecture completely, and it is the
right lens for reading modern models.

**Causal** models see only the past. That is required for generation, and it is what every autoregressive
language model is — a decoder with a causal mask, which is the transformer's version of the
unidirectional recurrence here.

**Bidirectional** models see everything. That is better for understanding tasks and useless for
generation. BERT is the bidirectional transformer, and its masked-language-model objective exists
precisely because a bidirectional model cannot be trained by next-token prediction: with the future
visible, the task is trivial. The book does not anticipate BERT, but the constraint that forced its
training objective is stated here.

The reason this matters is that the choice is not about architecture at all. It is about what information
is available at inference time, and it determines the objective a model can be trained with. A great deal
of confusion about why encoder and decoder models are trained differently dissolves once the distinction
is read as being about causality rather than about direction.

The two-dimensional extension is a curiosity that lost to convolution and then to attention, but it is
worth noting the reason the book gives for wanting it: long-range lateral interaction within a feature
map. That is what self-attention over image patches eventually supplied.
