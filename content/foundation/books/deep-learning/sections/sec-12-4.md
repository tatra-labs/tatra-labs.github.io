# 12.4 Natural Language Processing

The longest application section, and the one whose subject matter changed most in the years after
publication. It is worth reading as a snapshot of the moment before the change.

## n-grams and their problem

The classical language model is an **n-gram**: estimate the conditional probability of a token given the
previous $n - 1$ tokens by counting occurrences in a corpus. Simple, and it fails for a structural
reason — the number of possible contexts is $\lvert V \rvert^{n-1}$ for a vocabulary $V$, so almost every
context observed at test time was never seen in training. This is the curse of dimensionality from 5.11 in
its purest form.

The classical remedies are **smoothing** — assign some mass to unseen counts — and **back-off**, using
shorter contexts when the long one is unseen. Both are patches. The deeper problem the book identifies is
that n-grams have no notion of similarity between words: *dog* and *cat* are as unrelated as *dog* and
*Tuesday*, so nothing learned about one transfers to the other.

## Neural language models

The neural approach fixes exactly that. Words are mapped to **distributed representations** —
embeddings — in a continuous space, and the model predicts the next word from the embeddings of the
context. Because similar words receive nearby embeddings, an observation about one generalises to the
other. The book notes that this is the curse of dimensionality being addressed by sharing statistical
strength across words rather than by counting harder.

## The output-layer problem

A large vocabulary makes the softmax expensive: the cost of the output layer is proportional to
$\lvert V \rvert$, which may be hundreds of thousands. The section catalogues the responses:

- **Short lists** — use the neural model for the most frequent words and an n-gram for the rest.
- **Hierarchical softmax** — arrange words as leaves of a tree so that predicting one costs
  $O(\log \lvert V \rvert)$ decisions rather than $O(\lvert V \rvert)$.
- **Importance sampling** — approximate the normalisation by sampling a subset of negative words.
- **Noise-contrastive estimation** and ranking losses, which avoid the normalising constant entirely.

## Machine translation and attention

Translation is presented as the flagship application: an encoder-decoder from 10.4, mapping a source
sentence to a target one. The bottleneck of a fixed-size context is named again, and **attention** is
introduced here as the fix — a weighted read over the encoder's states, with the weights computed per
output step, so the decoder can focus on different parts of the source as it goes.

## My take

This section is where the book comes closest to describing what happened next and stops short. It has
distributed word representations, the encoder-decoder, attention, and the observation that the softmax
cost is the binding constraint. What it does not have is the idea that attention alone suffices.

Almost everything specific here has been superseded, and the pattern of the supersession is consistent:

**The vocabulary problem dissolved.** Subword tokenisation — byte-pair encoding and its relatives —
bounds the vocabulary at tens of thousands of units while representing any string, so hierarchical
softmax, short lists and NCE all became unnecessary. A problem that consumed a quarter of this section was
solved by changing what a token is.

**Static embeddings became contextual.** The embeddings described here assign one vector per word type,
which cannot distinguish *bank* by a river from *bank* holding money. Contextual representations, computed
by the model as it reads, replaced them entirely.

**Word-level modelling stopped being a task.** Language modelling is no longer a component of a translation
system or a speech recogniser; it is the training objective from which those capabilities emerge. The
book treats it as one application among several, which is the last moment at which that was a reasonable
framing.

The one thing here that grew rather than aged is the *diagnosis*. The reason neural models beat n-grams is
that distributed representations share statistical strength between related inputs, and that argument
scaled up unchanged — it is still the best short answer to why these models generalise at all.
