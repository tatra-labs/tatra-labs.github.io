# 19.5 Learned Approximate Inference

The chapter's final idea, and the one that made variational methods practical for deep models: stop
solving the inference problem and train a network to predict its answer.

## Amortisation

Explicit variational inference runs an iterative optimisation for **every example**, which is expensive
and must be repeated at test time. The book's observation is that this optimisation is being solved
repeatedly on similar inputs, so its solution is a function of the input — and functions can be
approximated by networks.

Train an **inference network** $f(v)$ to output the parameters of $q$ directly. After training, inference
is a single forward pass. The cost of learning to infer is paid once and amortised over every subsequent
use.

The book links this back to Section 14.8: predictive sparse decomposition is exactly this construction,
with a network trained to predict the code that the sparse-coding optimisation would have found.

## The wake-sleep algorithm

The historical instance the section develops. Training an inference network requires targets — pairs of
$v$ and the correct $h$ — which are not available, since finding $h$ is the problem. The **wake-sleep**
algorithm solves this by **sampling from the model**: draw $h$ from the prior and $v$ from the generative
model given $h$, giving a pair for free, and train the inference network on it.

The book names the flaw: the model is trained on inference for values of $v$ that have **high probability
under the model**, which early in training is not the same as the values that have high probability under
the data. The network learns to invert the model in regions the data never visits.

The section closes by noting that the biologically-inspired framing — a wake phase and a sleep phase, the
latter as a role for dreaming — is one of several such interpretations, and that other approaches train the
inference network jointly against the same bound the generative model is optimising.

## My take

That final clause is the whole difference between wake-sleep and what came after. A variational
autoencoder trains the inference network on the **same objective** as the generative model, using real
data, so both are optimised against the ELBO on the actual distribution of interest. Wake-sleep trains
them on separate objectives with the inference half fed by model samples, and inherits exactly the
mismatch the book describes.

Amortisation itself is the durable idea, and it is worth naming as a general pattern because it recurs far
outside inference. Any expensive per-instance optimisation can be replaced by a network trained to predict
its outcome: sparse coding's codes, a posterior's parameters, an optimiser's update, a diffusion sampler's
many steps distilled into a few, a search procedure's result cached in a policy network. The trade is
always the same — a fixed training cost, a constant-time query, and an approximation error that depends on
how well the target function generalises.

Chapter 19 ends here, and it ends one step short of the variational autoencoder. It has the bound, the
restricted family, the reparameterisation-free optimisation, and the inference network. What it does not
have is the trick that lets gradients flow through the sampling step, which is what allowed all four
pieces to be trained together by back-propagation. Section 20.10.3 covers the VAE itself, and this chapter
is the reason it works.
