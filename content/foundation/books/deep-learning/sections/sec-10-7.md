# 10.7 The Challenge of Long-Term Dependencies

The core difficulty of the chapter, stated as a fact about repeated matrix multiplication. Everything from
10.8 to 10.11 is a response to it.

## The mechanism

Consider a recurrence with no nonlinearity and no input, so that the state is multiplied by the same
matrix at every step. After $t$ steps the transformation is $W^t$. Writing $W$ in its eigendecomposition
from 2.7, that becomes

$$
W^t = Q \Lambda^t Q^{\top}
$$

so every eigenvalue is raised to the power $t$. Eigenvalues with magnitude less than one **vanish**, and
those with magnitude greater than one **explode**. Any component of the initial state not aligned with the
largest eigenvector is eventually annihilated.

The practical consequence is that gradients through long chains either shrink to nothing or blow up, and
the two problems are the same problem with a different sign. Vanishing is the more insidious of the two:
an exploding gradient is visible immediately, while a vanishing one simply means the model never learns
the dependency and gives no indication that it tried.

## Why this is worse for recurrent networks

Feedforward networks also compose many transformations, but with **different** weights at each layer, so
the products do not concentrate on a single eigenvalue. Some cancellation occurs; the effective scaling is
much less extreme. A recurrent network reuses one matrix, which is precisely what makes the power law
apply.

This is the cost of the parameter sharing that made the architecture attractive in 10.1. The same property
that lets a recurrent network generalise across sequence length is what makes its gradients degenerate
with sequence length.

The book gives the empirical scale of the problem, and it is sobering: experiments show that as the span
of the dependency increases, gradient-based optimisation becomes increasingly unlikely to succeed, with
the probability of successful training reaching vanishingly small values for sequences of length only
**10 or 20**.

## My take

The number is the thing to remember. A vanilla recurrent network struggles at spans of ten to twenty
steps, which is roughly one clause of a sentence. Every architecture in the rest of the chapter exists to
push that number up, and the reason attention won is that it removed the multiplication entirely — a path
from step $t$ to step $t + 1000$ passes through one attention operation rather than a thousand matrix
products, so there is no power of $W$ to vanish.

Worth being precise about what LSTM does and does not fix, because the folk account overstates it. LSTM
does not eliminate the problem; it provides a path along which the multiplicative factor is close to one
by construction, so information can be carried without repeated rescaling. That extends the usable range
by roughly an order of magnitude — hundreds rather than tens of steps — and it does not make the range
unbounded. LSTM language models were still limited to a few hundred tokens of effective context, and that
limit is what made the transformer's arbitrary-range attention so immediately valuable.

The asymmetry between vanishing and exploding is also worth carrying into practice. Exploding gradients
have a cheap and effective fix — clipping, in 10.11 — and vanishing gradients have no fix at the optimiser
level at all. They require an architectural change, which is why the rest of the chapter is about
architectures rather than about training tricks.
