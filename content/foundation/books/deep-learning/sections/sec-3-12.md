# 3.12 Technical Details of Continuous Variables

The book's one concession to measure theory. It is short, it is honest about being incomplete, and it
contains one result used constantly in modern generative modelling.

## Measure zero and almost everywhere

Continuous probability needs measure theory to be stated rigorously, and the authors give only the two
pieces of vocabulary a reader needs to parse later chapters.

A set of **measure zero** occupies no volume in the space under consideration — a single point on the
real line, or a line inside a plane. A union of countably many such sets still has measure zero. A
property holds **almost everywhere** if it holds except on a set of measure zero. Statements about
continuous distributions are routinely true only in this weaker sense, and the phrase is a signal that
an exception set exists and does not matter.

This also resolves a puzzle from 3.3: every individual real value has probability zero, which is not a
contradiction, because zero probability is not impossibility.

## Change of variables

If $y = g(x)$ with $g$ invertible and differentiable, the density does **not** simply carry over as
$p_y(y) = p_x(g^{-1}(y))$. That would break normalisation, because $g$ stretches and compresses volume.
The correct rule in one dimension is

$$
p_x(x) = p_y(g(x)) \left\lvert \frac{\partial g(x)}{\partial x} \right\rvert
$$

and in higher dimensions the derivative is replaced by the absolute determinant of the Jacobian:

$$
p_x(x) = p_y(g(x)) \left\lvert \det\left( \frac{\partial g(x)}{\partial x} \right) \right\rvert
$$

The book's worked example is $y = x/2$ on a uniform variable, where forgetting the correction gives a
density integrating to $1/2$.

## My take

This section is short in the book and is the entire theoretical basis of **normalizing flows**. A flow
defines a complicated density by pushing a simple one — usually a standard Gaussian — through an
invertible network, and evaluating its likelihood means evaluating exactly the formula above. The whole
design problem of a flow architecture is making $\det(\partial g / \partial x)$ cheap: coupling layers
give a triangular Jacobian whose determinant is a product of diagonal entries, and autoregressive flows
do the same by construction. Every one of those designs is engineering around the cost of one determinant.

The measure-zero vocabulary matters for a smaller but recurring reason. The claim that ReLU is
"differentiable almost everywhere" — non-differentiable only at zero, a set of measure zero — is what
lets gradient-based training use it without apology. Section 6.3.1 makes that argument, and this is where
the licence for it is issued.
