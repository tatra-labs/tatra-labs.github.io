# 14.3 Representational Power, Layer Size and Depth

A page and a half on why an autoencoder should be deep, reusing the arguments of Section 6.4 in a new
setting.

## The universal approximation argument

An autoencoder is usually drawn as a single hidden layer, but nothing requires that. Both the encoder and
the decoder are feedforward networks, and the universal approximation theorem applies to each: a
single-hidden-layer encoder with enough units can approximate any mapping from input to code to arbitrary
accuracy.

That is a statement about representation and, as in 6.4, it says nothing useful about cost. The book notes
that a single hidden layer is not sufficient in a practical sense — it cannot enforce arbitrary
constraints, such as a requirement that the code be sparse, and it may need infeasibly many units.

## Why depth helps

Three benefits, all inherited from the feedforward case:

- **Exponential reduction in cost.** Some functions requiring exponentially many units in a shallow
  network need only polynomially many in a deep one.
- **Exponential reduction in training data.** Depth reduces the amount of data needed to learn some
  functions, not only the number of parameters.
- **Better compression, empirically.** The book cites experimental results showing that deep autoencoders
  yield much better compression than corresponding shallow or linear autoencoders.

## The training strategy

The book gives a practical recommendation that reflects its era: a common approach to training a deep
autoencoder is to greedily pretrain the stack by training a series of **shallow** autoencoders, so the
deep model is assembled from parts that were each easy to train. A deep autoencoder can often be trained
this way even when training it directly is difficult.

## My take

The greedy pretraining recommendation is the most dated sentence in the chapter, and the book itself
undermines it two chapters later — Section 15.1 concludes that unsupervised pretraining had stopped being
useful for most purposes. Deep autoencoders are trained end to end now, and the reason is the same set of
developments that made deep supervised networks trainable: rectifiers, better initialisation,
normalisation, and Adam. The layer-wise strategy was a workaround for an optimisation problem that got
solved.

The depth arguments themselves held up, though the strongest evidence is not in the book. A modern latent
diffusion model uses a deep convolutional autoencoder to compress images into a latent space roughly
forty-eight times smaller by element count, and the entire economics of the method depends on that
compression being good enough that generation can happen in the small space. That is the "much better
compression" claim, at a scale that makes it load-bearing rather than merely interesting.

Worth noting the one asymmetry this section does not draw out. Encoder and decoder need not be the same
size or shape, and in practice they are often deliberately unbalanced — a heavy encoder and a light
decoder when the code is what matters, the reverse when generation is the goal. The section treats them
as symmetric, and almost nothing built since does.
