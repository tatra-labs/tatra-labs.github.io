# 3.14 Structured Probabilistic Models

The chapter closes by introducing the vocabulary of graphical models — just enough to read the notation
when it reappears, with the full treatment deferred to Chapter 16.

## Why structure

A distribution over many variables has an enormous number of parameters if written out directly, and most
of them encode interactions that do not exist. Factorising the joint into a product of small factors is
both statistically and computationally cheaper. A graph makes the factorisation explicit: one node per
random variable, edges for direct interactions.

## Two kinds of graph

**Directed** models use edges with arrows and factorise into conditional distributions, one per variable,
conditioned on its parents:

$$
p(x) = \prod_i p\left( x_i \mid Pa_{\mathcal{G}}(x_i) \right)
$$

The book's example chain $a \to b \to c \to d \to e$ gives
$p(a, b, c, d, e) = p(a) p(b \mid a) p(c \mid b) p(d \mid c) p(e \mid d)$.

**Undirected** models use edges without direction and factorise over **cliques** — sets of nodes all
mutually connected. Each clique gets a non-negative factor $\phi$, and because the product of arbitrary
non-negative factors does not integrate to one, the result must be divided by a normalising constant $Z$:

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_i \phi^{(i)}\left( \mathcal{C}^{(i)} \right)
$$

The factors are not conditional probabilities and need not be normalised individually.

## The point the section is careful to make

Directed and undirected are **not competing model families**. They are two languages for describing a
factorisation, and each can express independence structures the other cannot express compactly. Choosing
between them is a question of which structure a given distribution has, not which formalism is better.

## My take

The asymmetry hidden in these two equations is the single most practically important fact in Part III,
and the book states it here almost in passing: the directed form has no $Z$.

A directed model is normalised by construction, because each factor is already a distribution. You can
evaluate its likelihood exactly, sample from it in one ancestral pass, and train it by plain maximum
likelihood. An undirected model carries $Z$, a sum over every configuration of every variable, and that
sum is intractable for anything interesting. Chapter 18 exists entirely to work around it, and every
method there — contrastive divergence, score matching, noise-contrastive estimation — is a way of
training without computing $Z$.

Seen from 2026, that asymmetry largely settled the argument. The undirected models this book devotes
Chapter 20 to are historical; the directed autoregressive factorisation of Section 3.6 is what scaled. It
is not that Boltzmann machines were wrong, but that they had to be trained around a normalising constant
while transformers never had one.
