# 14.4 Stochastic Encoders and Decoders

A short but pivotal section: it recasts the encoder and decoder as **conditional distributions** rather
than as functions, which is the step that turns an autoencoder into a generative model.

## The reframing

An ordinary feedforward network's output layer is understood probabilistically — Section 6.2 showed that
a linear output parameterises a Gaussian, a sigmoid a Bernoulli, and the loss is the negative
log-likelihood. The same reading applies to an autoencoder's decoder.

Given a hidden code $h$, the decoder defines $p_{\text{decoder}}(x \mid h)$, and training minimises
$-\log p_{\text{decoder}}(x \mid h)$. The familiar losses fall out: for real-valued $x$ with a Gaussian
decoder, the negative log-likelihood is mean squared error; for binary $x$ with a Bernoulli decoder, it is
the cross-entropy. The choice of reconstruction loss was always a choice of decoder distribution.

Symmetrically, the encoder can be **stochastic** rather than deterministic, defining
$p_{\text{encoder}}(h \mid x)$ — a distribution over codes given an input, rather than a single code.

The book notes that a general autoencoder can then be described by two conditional distributions, and that
unlike a standard feedforward network the encoder and decoder distributions are not required to be
compatible with any single joint distribution — though training them toward compatibility is what the
generative versions do.

## My take

This is a two-page section that contains the hinge of the whole chapter. Everything before it treats an
autoencoder as a compression device evaluated by reconstruction error. Once the decoder is a distribution,
reconstruction error *is* a log-likelihood, and the autoencoder becomes a probabilistic model that can in
principle be evaluated and sampled from rather than merely used to produce features.

The variational autoencoder — which Section 20.10.3 covers — is precisely this section plus one more
ingredient. Make the encoder stochastic, as here. Add a prior $p(h)$ and require the encoder's output
distribution to stay close to it, which is the KL term. Then the objective becomes a lower bound on
$\log p(x)$, and the model is a real generative model rather than an analogy to one. The pieces are all
laid out here and not assembled.

The practical lesson is the same one Section 5.5 gave for supervised losses, and it is worth having met
twice: **choosing a reconstruction loss is choosing a noise model**. Using MSE on image reconstruction
asserts Gaussian pixel noise, which is why it produces blurry outputs — the conditional mean of a
multimodal distribution is a blur. Every complaint about VAE sample quality traces back to a decoder
distribution chosen for convenience, and this is the section that makes that visible.
