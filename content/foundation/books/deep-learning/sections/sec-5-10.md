# 5.10 Building a Machine Learning Algorithm

The chapter's synthesis. Nearly every algorithm in the book is one recipe with four slots filled in
differently.

## The recipe

1. **A dataset.**
2. **A cost function.**
3. **An optimisation procedure.**
4. **A model.**

Linear regression fills them with the design matrix, the negative log-likelihood, the normal equations,
and $p(y \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(y; x^{\top} w + b, 1)$. Swap the model for a logistic sigmoid and the
optimiser for gradient descent and the result is logistic regression. Swap the cost for one containing a
weight-decay term and the result is regularised regression. Nothing else changes.

The cost function usually contains at least one term measuring statistical estimation error — commonly
the negative log-likelihood — and may contain a regularisation term. The optimiser can be replaced
without touching anything else, which is why a closed-form solution and gradient descent are
interchangeable where both exist.

The recipe also covers **unsupervised** algorithms: PCA has a cost function measuring reconstruction
error and a model that is a linear encoder-decoder pair. And it covers cases where the closed form
disappears — adding weight decay to linear regression keeps the closed form, while adding $L^1$ or
switching to a non-linear model removes it, at which point an iterative optimiser is required rather than
merely convenient.

## My take

This is the most useful two pages in Chapter 5 for anyone who is going to *build* things rather than
read about them, because it tells you where to look when something is wrong. A training failure lives in
exactly one of four places, and the four are independent enough to be checked separately: the data, the
loss, the optimiser, the model. Debugging that does not begin by localising the problem to one slot tends
to consist of changing several at once.

The decomposition also explains why deep learning frameworks are shaped the way they are. A `Dataset`, a
loss function, an `Optimizer`, and a `Module` are the four slots, and the reason those APIs compose
freely is that the recipe says they are genuinely independent. That design was not arrived at by accident
— it is this section expressed as software.

What the recipe hides is that the slots are not equally consequential. In practice the dataset dominates,
the model matters less than its size, the loss is almost always cross-entropy, and the optimiser is
almost always Adam. A framing that presents four equal choices describes the space of possible
algorithms accurately and the distribution of actual ones very poorly.
