# 18.5 Denoising Score Matching

Half a page in the book, and the single most consequential half page in Part III.

## The modification

Score matching as given in 18.4 fits the score of the empirical distribution, which is a sum of Dirac
spikes at the training points — not a well-behaved target, and the reason the estimator is unstable where
data is sparse.

Denoising score matching fits the score of a **smoothed** distribution instead:

$$
p_{\text{smoothed}}(x) = \int p_{\text{data}}(y) \, q(x \mid y) \, dy
$$

where $q$ is a corruption process, typically adding Gaussian noise. Rather than matching the score of the
data, the model matches the score of the data convolved with noise.

The book gives the reason this is not merely a technical fix: in practice, with a finite training set, one
does not want to fit the empirical distribution exactly. Fitting the smoothed version is closer to what is
actually wanted, and regularises the estimate.

The section then states the connection that matters: several autoencoder training criteria are equivalent
to score matching or to denoising score matching, and **a denoising autoencoder is estimating the score**.
Training a network to remove Gaussian noise from a corrupted input teaches it, up to a scale factor, the
gradient of the log-density of the smoothed data distribution.

## My take

This is where the book, without noticing, describes the training objective of every diffusion model.

The chain of equivalences is complete in the text and spread across two chapters. Section 14.5 shows that
a denoising autoencoder learns a vector field pointing toward the data manifold. This section shows that
the vector field is the score of the noise-smoothed data distribution. Section 18.4 shows that the score
determines the distribution up to a constant and does not require the partition function. Put together: a
network trained to denoise has learned a generative model, and can sample by following its own output.

Two additions turned that into diffusion. Train **one network across many noise levels**, conditioned on
the level, rather than fixing a single one — which fixes the trade-off the book leaves unresolved, since
a large noise level gives signal far from the data and a small one gives fidelity near it, and there is no
single right choice. Then **sample by annealing**: start at the largest noise level, take a few steps
following the score, reduce the level, repeat.

Everything else — the variance schedules, the reweighted objectives, the ODE and SDE formulations — is
refinement. The core is here, in a section of a chapter about how to train Boltzmann machines.

It is worth sitting with why it was missed. Denoising score matching appears in this book as a *training
trick for undirected models*, evaluated by whether it produces good Boltzmann machines. Nobody was asking
whether the denoiser itself was the model. The generative capability was present in the mathematics and
invisible in the framing, which is a reasonably common way for a good idea to wait five years.
