# 13.2 Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

The same linear factor template as 13.1, with one change: the prior on the latent variables is
**non-Gaussian**. That change is what makes the factors identifiable.

## The method

ICA seeks a linear transformation of the observed data producing components that are as **independent** as
possible. The canonical application is the cocktail-party problem: $n$ microphones record $n$ speakers,
each microphone capturing a different mixture, and ICA recovers the individual voices from the mixtures.

The book emphasises that ICA is a family rather than one algorithm. Some variants add noise to the
generative equation and some do not; many are not fully probabilistic models with a tractable
$p(x)$ at all, and are instead defined by maximising some proxy for non-Gaussianity such as kurtosis.

## Why the prior must be non-Gaussian

The crucial constraint is that $p(h)$ must not be Gaussian. If the latent variables were Gaussian, then
any rotation of them would give an equally good explanation of the data — a Gaussian is rotationally
symmetric, so the model cannot distinguish $W$ from $WR$ for an orthogonal $R$. The factors would not be
identifiable, and the model would recover only the subspace, which is what PCA already does.

With a non-Gaussian prior, rotations are distinguishable and the individual components can be recovered.
The book notes that the user must generally specify the prior by hand — a sparse, heavy-tailed prior gives
sparse features; other choices give other behaviour.

The section closes by noting the extension to **nonlinear ICA**, in which the mixing function is learned
rather than linear, and observing that nonlinear variants have been generalised in ways that connect to
other generative models.

## My take

The identifiability argument is the reason to read this section, and it generalises far past ICA. It is a
clean example of a fact that recurs throughout Part III: **a model can fit the data perfectly and still
fail to recover the structure that generated it**, because several parameter settings produce the same
distribution. The Gaussian's rotational symmetry is the simplest instance of that failure, and breaking
it requires an assumption that has nothing to do with fit.

That connects directly to Section 15.3, which wants disentangled representations, and to the modern
disentanglement literature, where the key theoretical result is negative: without inductive biases or
supervision, disentanglement is **not identifiable**, essentially for the reason given here. ICA solved
its version of the problem by assuming non-Gaussianity. There is no comparable free assumption available
in the nonlinear case, and it took the field about twenty years after the results in this section to
establish that.

The other thing worth noticing is that ICA is one of the few models in this book with a genuinely
scientific use rather than a predictive one. It is standard for separating sources in EEG and fMRI, where
the goal is to recover something real about the signal rather than to predict a label — and the reason it
is trusted there is precisely the identifiability property this section establishes.
