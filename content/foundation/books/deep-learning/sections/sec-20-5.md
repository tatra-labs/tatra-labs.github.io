# 20.5 Boltzmann Machines for Real-Valued Data

Boltzmann machines are defined over binary variables. Images are not binary, and this section catalogues
the attempts to fix that — a good record of how much machinery an inconvenient assumption can generate.

## Gaussian-Bernoulli RBM

The straightforward extension: keep the hidden units binary and make the visible units real-valued with a
conditional Gaussian distribution. The energy function gains a quadratic term in $v$, and
$p(v \mid h)$ becomes a Gaussian whose mean is a linear function of the hidden units.

The book notes several parameterisations exist, differing in whether the covariance is fixed, diagonal or
tied, and that they are not equivalent. Crucially, the model captures the **mean** of the visible units as
a function of the hiddens, and the covariance is not conditioned on them at all.

## Modelling covariance

That limitation is the motivation for everything else in the section. In natural images the conditional
covariance matters — the intensity of one pixel is informative about the *variability* of its neighbours,
not only their mean — and a Gaussian-Bernoulli RBM cannot represent that.

Three models address it. The **mean and covariance RBM** (mcRBM) uses two sets of hidden units, one
encoding the mean and one the conditional covariance. The **mean-product of Student t-distributions**
model (mPoT) does something similar with a different distributional form. The **spike-and-slab RBM**
(ssRBM) gives each hidden unit two components — a binary spike indicating whether the unit is active and a
real-valued slab giving its magnitude — which the book presents as the most tractable of the three, since
its conditionals remain analytically available.

The book records the practical difficulty running through all of them: these models are hard to sample
from, and mcRBM in particular requires Hamiltonian Monte Carlo rather than Gibbs sampling, because its
conditionals are not simple enough for coordinate updates.

## My take

This section is the most dated in the chapter and it earns its place as a cautionary tale. Three model
families, each more elaborate than the last, all built to work around the fact that the base model assumed
binary variables — and all of them abandoned. The complexity is not incidental; it is what happens when a
convenient mathematical assumption meets data that does not satisfy it.

The spike-and-slab construction is the one idea with an afterlife. A binary gate multiplying a continuous
magnitude is a good way to represent something that is either absent or present-with-a-value, and the
pattern recurs in sparse coding, in gated architectures, and in the sparse autoencoders used for
interpretability. It is worth knowing the name.

What replaced all of this is worth stating plainly, because it is a one-line answer to a section-length
problem. A modern generative model of images does not extend a binary model to real values; it works in a
continuous space from the start, and it does not model the covariance structure explicitly because a deep
network computing the mean of a conditional distribution at every step of a denoising chain captures those
dependencies implicitly. The correlations that mcRBM was built to represent are handled by depth and
iteration rather than by a dedicated parameterisation.
