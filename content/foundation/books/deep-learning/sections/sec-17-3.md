# 17.3 Markov Chain Monte Carlo Methods

What to do when neither direct sampling nor importance sampling is available — which, for undirected
models, is the normal situation.

## The setting

Ancestral sampling works for directed models. For an undirected model there is no ordering of conditionals
to follow, and the partition function makes the joint unavailable in closed form. The book's response is to
sample **approximately**, by constructing a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the model
distribution.

## The chain

A Markov chain is a state $x$ and a **transition operator** $T(x' \mid x)$ giving the probability of
moving to $x'$ from $x$. Running the chain updates the state repeatedly. If $q^{(t)}(x)$ is the
distribution over states at step $t$, then applying the transition operator gives $q^{(t+1)}$, and under
appropriate conditions this converges to a fixed point — the **stationary distribution** or
**equilibrium distribution** — satisfying

$$
q(x') = \sum_x T\left( x' \mid x \right) q(x)
$$

For a finite discrete state space this is an eigenvector equation: the transition matrix has an
eigenvector with eigenvalue exactly $1$, and that eigenvector is the stationary distribution. The book
notes that under mild conditions this eigenvalue is unique and all others have magnitude less than one, so
the chain converges from any starting point, and the rate of convergence is governed by the **second**
largest eigenvalue.

The number of steps needed to reach equilibrium is the **mixing time**, and the initial period discarded
before samples are usable is the **burn-in**.

## The practical costs

The book is candid about three:

- **There is no way to know when the chain has converged.** Theory guarantees eventual convergence and
  provides no usable test for it. In practice the chain is run for some heuristically chosen number of
  steps and the result is inspected.
- **Successive samples are correlated.** They come from the same trajectory, so $n$ steps do not give $n$
  independent samples. The remedy is to keep every $k$-th sample, or to run several chains in parallel.
- **The chain may mix slowly**, which is the subject of 17.5.

## My take

"No test for convergence" is the sentence that determines how much MCMC can be trusted, and it is worth
stating in its practical form: an MCMC estimate carries an error you cannot bound. The convergence
diagnostics that exist — comparing several chains from different starting points, watching autocorrelation
— detect obvious failures and cannot certify success. A chain that has spent its whole run in one mode
looks exactly like a converged chain.

The eigenvalue framing is the useful piece of theory, because it makes the difficulty concrete. Mixing time
is governed by the spectral gap between the first and second eigenvalues, and a distribution with
well-separated modes has a tiny gap, so the mixing time is exponentially long. That is not a defect of any
algorithm; it is a property of the target distribution.

The consequence for deep learning is decisive. Training an undirected model requires samples from the model
at every gradient step, per Chapter 18, so slow mixing is not an inconvenience at evaluation time — it is a
tax on every update. Directed models avoid the whole apparatus, and diffusion models get iterative
refinement without it by fixing the chain length in advance rather than waiting for a stationary
distribution.
