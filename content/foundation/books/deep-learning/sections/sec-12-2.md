# 12.2 Computer Vision

The application that made the field's reputation. The section is short on architecture — Chapter 9
covered that — and is mostly about preprocessing, which is the part that changed most.

## The scope

Vision covers recognition, detection, segmentation, and synthesis, and the book notes that even
reconstruction tasks such as denoising and super-resolution pave the way for the recognition ones.

## Preprocessing

The book's general position is that deep learning needs **less** preprocessing than classical computer
vision, because the model learns the invariances that were formerly hand-engineered. What remains is
mostly making inputs comparable.

**Scaling to a standard range** — pixel values mapped to $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$ — is the one requirement,
and mixing the two conventions is described as a guaranteed failure.

**Cropping and resizing** to a common size is required whenever the architecture cannot accept variable
input, though convolutional models can often avoid it.

**Contrast normalisation** gets the most space, because contrast is one of the largest sources of
variation in images that carries no information about content. **Global contrast normalisation** subtracts
the mean from each image and rescales so that the standard deviation across pixels is a constant. The book
notes the scaling parameter can be set either to fix the standard deviation or to fix the norm, and adds
a caution: GCN maps images onto a sphere, and for images with very low contrast the rescaling amplifies
noise or compression artefacts, so a small regularisation constant is added to the denominator.

**Local contrast normalisation** applies the same operation within small windows rather than over the
whole image, so that contrast is equalised across regions. The book reports this often works better,
because it removes variation at the scale at which it usually occurs, and it can be implemented
efficiently with separable convolutions.

**Dataset augmentation** is noted as a form of preprocessing applied only to the training set, per 7.4.

## My take

The section's own thesis — that deep learning replaces hand-designed preprocessing with learned
processing — turned out to be more true than the section itself allows. Global and local contrast
normalisation, which get most of the page here, essentially disappeared from practice. What replaced them
is per-channel standardisation with fixed dataset statistics, and then batch or layer normalisation inside
the network, which does the same job adaptively and at every depth rather than once at the input.

The trajectory is worth naming because it repeats. A preprocessing step exists because a model is not
robust to some variation; when the model becomes robust, the step is deleted. SIFT and HOG went first,
then contrast normalisation, then in large-scale training even careful resizing — models now consume
variable resolutions directly.

What remains from this section is the boring part, and it is the part that still causes bugs: input
scaling must match between training and inference. A model trained on $[0, 1]$ and served images in
$[-1, 1]$ produces confident nonsense, with no error message, and it is among the most common deployment
failures there is. The book calls this "guaranteed failure" and it is right.

The other durable point is that variation which carries no information about the label is exactly what
preprocessing and augmentation exist to remove — and that deciding which variation is uninformative is a
statement about the task, not about images.
