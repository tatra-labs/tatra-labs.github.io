# 20.14 Evaluating Generative Models

A methodological section, and one of the most useful in Part III, because it explains why the field's
reported numbers were so hard to compare.

## Likelihood is not comparable across preprocessing

The book's first point is a trap that caught published work. Log-likelihood depends on how the data was
preprocessed, and small differences make large differences to the number. Its examples: whether pixel
values are treated as integers in $[0, 255]$ or scaled to $[0, 1]$ changes the likelihood by a constant
per dimension, and adding noise to discrete data before modelling it with a continuous density — which is
necessary to avoid infinite likelihood on discrete values — changes it again.

The consequence is that two papers reporting likelihoods on the same dataset may not be measuring the same
quantity, and the book's advice is that comparisons are meaningful only when the preprocessing is
identical.

## Likelihood may not be what you want

Even measured correctly, likelihood does not track sample quality. A model can achieve good likelihood and
produce poor samples, and — the book notes this direction too — a model can produce excellent samples and
have poor or undefined likelihood. GANs, which have no tractable likelihood at all, are the obvious case.

## Visual inspection is unreliable

Looking at samples is the most common evaluation and the book is blunt about its problems. It is
**subjective**; a bad model can produce a few good samples by chance; and — the most serious failure — a
model that has **memorised** training examples produces excellent samples and has learned nothing. The
book recommends checking generated samples against their nearest neighbours in the training set, and notes
that even this can be fooled by a model that has memorised with small transformations applied.

## Parzen windows

Estimating the model's density by placing a kernel at each generated sample was a common practice. The
book states plainly that this **does not work in high dimensions** — the estimate is dominated by the
kernel bandwidth rather than by the model — and that reported Parzen-window likelihoods should not be
trusted.

## My take

The conclusion the book draws is the right one and it is uncomfortable: there is no single metric, and the
correct evaluation depends on the intended use. A model for imputing missing values should be evaluated on
imputation; one for producing images should be judged on images; one used as a compressor should be
measured in bits.

What happened after publication partly confirms this and partly illustrates the problem. The field
standardised on **FID**, which compares statistics of generated and real samples in the feature space of a
pretrained classifier, and it became the number reported in essentially every image-generation paper. It
is better than Parzen windows and it inherits the same structural weakness: it is a summary statistic, it
can be gamed, it is sensitive to implementation details of the feature extractor, and it does not detect
memorisation at all. A model that reproduced the training set would score perfectly.

The memorisation warning has aged into the most important sentence in the section. It is now a legal and
ethical question rather than only a methodological one — diffusion models have been shown to reproduce
training images verbatim under the right prompts — and the check the book recommends, comparing samples
against nearest neighbours in the training data, remains the right one and is still not standard practice.
