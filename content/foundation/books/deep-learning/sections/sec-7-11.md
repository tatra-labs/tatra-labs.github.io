# 7.11 Bagging and Other Ensemble Methods

Train several models and average their predictions. The section is short because the argument is a
two-line variance calculation, and that calculation is the setup for dropout in 7.12.

## The technique

**Bagging** — bootstrap aggregating — constructs $k$ datasets by sampling with replacement from the
original, each of the same size, so each contains duplicates and omits roughly a third of the original
examples. A model is trained on each, and the predictions are averaged.

The reason this works is a statement about error correlation. Suppose $k$ models each make an error
$\epsilon_i$ on a given example, with variance $v$ and pairwise covariance $c$. The expected squared
error of the average is

$$
\frac{1}{k} v + \frac{k - 1}{k} c
$$

Read the two extremes. If the errors are perfectly correlated, $c = v$, the expression reduces to $v$ and
the ensemble is no better than one model. If the errors are uncorrelated, $c = 0$, it reduces to $v / k$
— the error shrinks in proportion to the number of models. **Ensembling buys exactly as much as the
models disagree.**

The book adds that neural networks find enough different solutions — from random initialisation, random
minibatch order, different hyperparameters — that ensembling them is beneficial even when every member is
trained on the same dataset.

**Boosting** is mentioned as the counter-example: it builds an ensemble with *higher* capacity than its
members, incrementally adding models to correct the residual errors of the ones already present, and so
reduces bias rather than variance.

## My take

The variance formula is the most quotable result in Chapter 7 and it explains far more than ensembling.
It is why diversity is the thing to engineer rather than individual model quality — an ensemble of five
excellent, near-identical models is worth about as much as one of them, while five mediocre models that
fail differently can beat all of them.

The book's own caution deserves repeating: ensembles are excluded from most benchmark comparisons by
convention, because any method can be improved by ensembling and a comparison that does not control for
it is measuring compute rather than ideas. That convention is not universally honoured, and a reported
state-of-the-art result achieved by an ensemble is not comparable to a single-model one.

What has changed since is the economics. Ensembling costs $k$ times the training and $k$ times the
inference, and at the scale of modern models that is prohibitive — nobody trains five copies of a
frontier model to average them. The techniques that survived are the ones that approximate the ensemble
without paying for it, which is precisely what the next section is about, and — later — weight averaging
across a training trajectory, which gets much of the benefit for the cost of one run.

Worth noticing that self-consistency decoding in language models is this section returning by another
door: sample several reasoning paths, take the majority answer. It is bagging over stochastic decodes
rather than over models, and it works for the same reason — the errors are only weakly correlated.
