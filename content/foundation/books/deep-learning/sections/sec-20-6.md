# 20.6 Convolutional Boltzmann Machines

An attempt to combine the two most successful ideas the book has described — convolution from Chapter 9
and the Boltzmann machine from this one — and an honest account of why the combination did not work well.

## The motivation

High-dimensional images are not tractable for a fully connected Boltzmann machine, for the reasons of
Section 9.2: a dense connection between every pixel and every hidden unit is too many parameters and
ignores the spatial structure. Convolution solves this in the feedforward setting, so applying it here is
the obvious move.

## The problems

The book lists difficulties that are specific to the undirected setting and do not arise in a
convolutional network.

**Pooling.** Max pooling has no obvious probabilistic counterpart. The proposed solution is
**probabilistic max pooling**, which constrains the units in a pooling region so that at most one is
active, giving a valid distribution over the region. It works, and it constrains the model in a way
ordinary pooling does not.

**Padding.** A convolutional network handles image boundaries with the zero-padding conventions of 9.5. In
an energy-based model the boundary units genuinely have fewer neighbours and therefore different
statistics, and the model must account for it rather than papering over it.

**Scaling to large images.** Convolutional Boltzmann machines were demonstrated on small images. The book
notes that the partition function is affected by the image size, so a model trained at one resolution does
not transfer to another — a normalising constant that depends on the input shape is a problem a
feedforward network never has.

**Sampling.** All of Chapter 17's difficulties, now with weight sharing making the units strongly coupled
across the image.

## My take

The recurring theme of Part III appears here in its most concentrated form: **every operation that is easy
in a feedforward network becomes a research problem in an undirected one**, and the reason is always the
partition function. Pooling is a function call in a convolutional network and requires a new probabilistic
construction here. Changing image size is a non-issue there and invalidates the model here.

That is a good way to understand why the field went the direction it did. Feedforward networks are not more
expressive than energy-based models — in principle the reverse is arguable — but they compose freely. Any
differentiable operation can be dropped into a feedforward network and trained; almost nothing can be
dropped into an undirected model without a derivation.

Probabilistic max pooling is worth remembering as an idea, though. Constraining a group of units so that at
most one is active is a hard sparsity constraint of exactly the kind Section 7.10 discusses, and the
structure — a group with a winner — is the same one that maxout and top-$k$ expert routing use. It was
invented here to make pooling normalisable and it describes a pattern that outlived its purpose.
