# 16.6 Inference and Approximate Inference

The section that names the wall. Having latent variables is what makes structured models expressive, and
it is also what makes using them intractable.

## The problem

Two questions must be answered to use a latent-variable model. Predicting one variable from others
requires $p(x_i \mid x_j)$, and training by maximum likelihood requires $p(h \mid v)$ — the posterior over
latent variables given the visible ones — because the log-likelihood gradient depends on it.

The book states the result plainly: computing the marginal probability of a general graphical model is
**#P-hard**, and exact inference is intractable for most models of interest. The tractable cases are
narrow — models whose graph is a tree, or has small treewidth — and the models deep learning wants are
not among them.

Since the models are chosen for expressiveness rather than for tractable inference, approximation is not
a compromise made under pressure; it is the only available mode of operation.

## The response

The book previews the answer developed in Chapter 19: treat inference as **optimisation**. Rather than
computing the true posterior, search within a family of tractable distributions for the one closest to
it, where closeness is measured by KL divergence. That converts an intractable summation into a
maximisation, which gradient methods can attack.

## My take

The reframing of inference as optimisation is the most important idea in Part III and it is worth
appreciating what it costs as well as what it buys.

What it buys is that the problem becomes the kind of problem the field already knows how to solve. What it
costs is that the answer is now wrong in a specific and predictable direction. Variational inference
minimises $D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$ — the reverse direction from maximum likelihood, per 3.13 — which is
mode-seeking. The approximate posterior will concentrate on one mode and **underestimate uncertainty**,
systematically. That is not a bug in any implementation; it is a property of the objective.

Knowing this predicts the failure modes of a great deal of downstream work. Variational autoencoders
produce posteriors that are too narrow. Bayesian neural networks trained variationally are overconfident.
Any system whose uncertainty estimate comes from a variational approximation is reporting a lower bound
on its ignorance.

The hardness result is worth holding onto for a different reason. #P-hardness is a statement about the
worst case over all graphs, and the practical situation is worse than that suggests rather than better —
the graphs deep learning uses are dense by design, per 16.7, so they are nowhere near the tractable
special cases. The field did not find a clever exact algorithm and never will. Everything from here is
approximation, and the useful question is which approximation and with which bias.
