# 10.1 Unfolding Computational Graphs

Chapter 10 begins where Chapter 9 did — with a structural idea rather than an architecture — and the idea
is parameter sharing across *time* rather than across space.

## From recurrence to a graph

A classical dynamical system is defined by a recurrence:

$$
s^{(t)} = f\left( s^{(t-1)}; \theta \right)
$$

**Unfolding** the recurrence means writing it out for a finite number of steps, replacing the cycle by a
chain in which the same function $f$ appears at every position. For three steps the state $s^{(3)}$ is
$f(f(f(s^{(0)})))$, an ordinary acyclic computational graph of the kind Chapter 6 knows how to
differentiate.

A recurrent network adds an external input at each step:

$$
h^{(t)} = f\left( h^{(t-1)}, x^{(t)}; \theta \right)
$$

The hidden state $h$ is now a **lossy summary** of the sequence so far. Lossy is the operative word: the
state has fixed size while the history grows, so the network must choose what to keep. When the task is
predicting the next element, the state must retain enough of the past to be predictive of the future,
and no more.

## Why unfolding matters

The book gives two advantages, and they are the entire justification for the architecture.

**The learned model has fixed input size.** However long the sequence, the transition function maps one
state and one input to a new state. The model does not grow with the data.

**The same function with the same parameters is used at every step.** This is parameter sharing in the
sense of Section 7.9, applied along the time axis, and it has the same two benefits: far fewer parameters,
and the ability to generalise to sequence lengths never seen in training. A model with separate parameters
per position could not do either, and would have no way to share what it learned about position 5 with
position 500.

## My take

The lossy-summary framing is the most useful sentence in the section, because it identifies the bottleneck
before any architecture has been proposed. A fixed-size state carrying an unbounded history *must* discard
information, and every difficulty in the rest of the chapter follows from what gets discarded and when.
Sections 10.7 and 10.10 are about failing and succeeding at keeping the right things.

It is also the property attention removed. A transformer does not maintain a summary at all — it keeps
every previous token available and computes what to look at on demand. Stated in this section's terms,
attention abandons the fixed-size state and pays linear memory for it. Everything from 10.7 onward is
about how to make a fixed-size state work, and the eventual answer was to stop insisting on one.

Worth noticing that the unfolding trick is not specific to recurrence. It is the general move of turning a
program with loops into a straight-line graph so it can be differentiated, and it is what every framework
does when it traces a model. Recurrent networks were the first place the field needed it.
