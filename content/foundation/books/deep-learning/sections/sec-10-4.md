# 10.4 Encoder-Decoder Sequence-to-Sequence Architectures

How to map an input sequence to an output sequence of a *different* length. The section is three pages
long and it names, in its final paragraph, the limitation that produced attention.

## The architecture

Two recurrent networks. The **encoder** reads the input sequence and emits a context $C$, usually a simple
function of its final hidden state — often that state itself. The **decoder** is conditioned on $C$ and
generates the output sequence.

The two are trained jointly to maximise the average log-probability of the correct output given the
input, over all pairs in the training set. Crucially, the input and output lengths are unconstrained and
need not match, which none of the architectures in 10.2 permitted.

The context $C$ can be supplied to the decoder in more than one way: as its initial state, as an
additional input at every step, or both. The book notes it need not be a vector at all — it can be a
sequence.

## The limitation

The book states the problem directly: when $C$ is a fixed-size vector, it may be **too small to
adequately summarise a long sequence**. Everything the decoder will ever know about the input must pass
through that one vector, so a longer input does not get a larger channel.

The fix the authors cite is Bahdanau's: make the context a **variable-length sequence** and add a
mechanism that learns to associate elements of that sequence with elements of the output. That mechanism
is attention, and it is discussed further in Section 12.4.5.1.

## My take

This section is where the chapter's central problem becomes unavoidable. Section 10.1 described the hidden
state as a lossy summary and treated the lossiness as a feature; here the loss is quantified as a hard
bottleneck, and the numbers are stark — a single vector of a few hundred units carrying an entire
paragraph.

What is striking is how completely the book identifies the problem and how briefly it treats the solution.
Attention appears as a fix for one limitation of one architecture, cross-referenced to a subsection of the
applications chapter. Within a year of publication it had been shown that the recurrence could be dropped
entirely and the attention kept — that the mechanism introduced to patch the encoder-decoder bottleneck
was sufficient on its own. Nothing in the book hints at that, and it is the single largest thing it
missed.

The encoder-decoder *shape* nevertheless survived unchanged. Translation, summarisation, speech-to-text
and image captioning are all still framed as this section frames them, and the transformer's original
formulation is an encoder-decoder pair. What changed is what the context is: not one vector but the
encoder's entire output sequence, with the decoder computing a different weighted combination at every
step.

Worth flagging one thing the section gets exactly right and that is easy to forget: the two networks are
trained **jointly**, on the end-to-end objective. Nothing supervises the context representation
directly. Whatever $C$ ends up meaning is whatever turned out to be useful for the decoder, which is the
same argument for end-to-end training that Chapter 15 makes about representations generally.
