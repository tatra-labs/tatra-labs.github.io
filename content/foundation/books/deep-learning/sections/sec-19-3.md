# 19.3 MAP Inference and Sparse Coding

The crudest approximation in the chapter, and the one with the most surprising interpretation: taking a
single most-likely value for the latent variables is a form of variational inference.

## MAP inference

Rather than computing the full posterior, compute only its mode:

$$
h^{\ast} = \arg\max_h \, p(h \mid v)
$$

This is not usually called approximate inference, because it produces an exact answer to a different
question — the most likely $h$, rather than a distribution over $h$.

## The reinterpretation

The book's contribution here is to show that MAP inference **is** variational inference, with the family of
$q$ restricted to Dirac distributions:

$$
q(h \mid v) = \delta\left( h - \mu \right)
$$

With $q$ a point mass, the entropy term $H(q)$ in the ELBO of 19.1 is a constant — it does not depend on
where the point is — so maximising the bound reduces to maximising $\log p(h, v)$ over the location $\mu$.
That is exactly MAP inference.

Learning then proceeds like EM with an approximate E-step: alternate between finding $h^{\ast}$ for each
example and updating $\theta$ to increase $\log p(h^{\ast}, v)$.

## Sparse coding again

The book uses this to explain sparse coding properly. In sparse coding the prior on $h$ is factorial and
sparsity-inducing — Laplace, say — and the likelihood is Gaussian, so maximising the joint means minimising
reconstruction error plus an $L^1$ penalty on the code, which is the objective from Section 13.4. Sparse
coding is MAP inference in a linear factor model, and its alternating optimisation is the EM structure with
a Dirac $q$.

The book adds that the true posterior in sparse coding is not tractable and not unimodal, so a point
estimate is a real approximation rather than a convenience.

## My take

The Dirac framing is genuinely clarifying, and it makes the cost of MAP inference precise. A point estimate
discards the **width** of the posterior — all the information about how uncertain the model is — and the
formal statement of that loss is that the entropy term has been made constant. The ELBO has an entropy term
because a good $q$ should be as broad as the posterior it approximates; MAP throws that term away, so
nothing pushes $q$ to be broad at all.

The consequence is a systematic bias, and it is the same one noted for MAP parameter estimation in Section
5.6. A model trained with MAP inference over its latents will be overconfident about them, and any
downstream quantity that depends on posterior variance will be wrong.

It also makes clear what the variational autoencoder adds. A VAE is this section with the Dirac replaced by
a Gaussian whose mean *and variance* are produced by the encoder. Restoring the entropy term is the entire
difference, and it is what turns an alternating optimisation with two hand-weighted penalties into a proper
bound on the log-likelihood. Section 14.8's predictive sparse decomposition sits between them: it amortises
the inference but keeps the point estimate.
