# 20.13 Other Generation Schemes

A brief section on approaches that fit none of the previous categories. It is two paragraphs in the book
and one of them describes what generative modelling would become.

## Diffusion inversion

The book describes an approach in which a **known** process gradually destroys the structure in the data —
adding noise repeatedly until the distribution is simple — and a model is trained to **invert** each step
of that destruction. Generation runs the learned inverse process from the simple distribution back to the
data.

The book cites Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues for this, and gives it a few lines. The framing offered is
that because each step of the forward process is a small change, each step of the reverse process is also
a small change and is therefore easy to model — a hard generative problem decomposed into many easy
conditional ones.

## Approximate Bayesian computation

The other approach mentioned takes a different tack entirely: rather than matching a likelihood, match
**moments** or other summary statistics of the generated samples to those of the data. This is useful when
the model is a simulator whose likelihood cannot be evaluated at all, and the book notes it has seen little
use for deep generative models.

## My take

The first paragraph of this section is the seed of diffusion models, cited correctly, described correctly,
and given about a hundred words in a chapter of thirty pages. It is the single most striking near-miss in
the book.

What makes it worth dwelling on is that the surrounding material is all present. Section 14.5 has the
denoising autoencoder and its vector field. Section 18.4 has score matching and the fact that the score
avoids the partition function. Section 18.5 has the equivalence between the two, and states that a
denoising autoencoder estimates the score of the noise-smoothed distribution. Sections 20.11 and 20.12
have the iterative sampler. This section has the multi-step corruption process and its inversion. Every
component of a diffusion model appears somewhere in Part III, and the book never assembles them, because
each is introduced to solve a different local problem.

That is worth taking as a general observation about how fields progress. The missing ingredient was not a
new mathematical object; it was the recognition that these five things were one thing. The five years
between this book and the diffusion papers were spent on scale and engineering, and on someone noticing
that the pieces fit.

The ABC paragraph is a fair assessment that still holds — simulation-based inference remains a real
technique in scientific computing and has had almost no contact with deep generative modelling. The one
thread that connects it to the present is that moment matching, mentioned here and in 20.10, is what
generative moment matching networks were built on, and they lost to adversarial training for the reason
this section implies: summary statistics are a weak signal compared with a learned discriminator.
