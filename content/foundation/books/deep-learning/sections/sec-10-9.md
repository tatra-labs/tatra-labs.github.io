# 10.9 Leaky Units and Other Strategies for Multiple Time Scales

If the problem is that a single time scale cannot carry both fast and slow information, the response is to
build a model with several. Three strategies, in increasing order of how well they aged.

## Skip connections through time

Add direct connections from step $t$ to step $t + d$ for some $d > 1$, alongside the ordinary
step-by-step ones. Gradients then have a path that traverses $\tau / d$ edges rather than $\tau$, which
mitigates both vanishing and exploding by shortening the product.

The book notes the limitation: because the connections are added rather than substituted, the ordinary
path still exists, and so the exploding behaviour along it is not removed. This helps, but does not
eliminate the problem.

## Leaky units

A **leaky unit** maintains a running average of its input with a linear self-connection:

$$
\mu^{(t)} = \alpha \mu^{(t-1)} + (1 - \alpha) v^{(t)}
$$

When $\alpha$ is near one, the unit remembers information about the distant past for a long time. When it
is near zero, the past is discarded quickly and recent information dominates. The self-connection weight
is what sets the unit's time constant.

The advantage over integer-valued skip connections is that $\alpha$ is a real number, so the time scale is
continuous rather than quantised, and it can be **learned** by gradient descent rather than fixed. The
book suggests two approaches: sample the time constants from a distribution and fix them, or make them
free parameters.

## Removing connections

The third strategy is to actively **remove** the length-one connections and replace them with longer ones,
forcing units to operate on a long time scale rather than merely allowing it. This differs from skip
connections, which add without removing, and from leaky units, which adapt the scale continuously.

## My take

Leaky units are the important entry, because the equation above is the gated recurrent unit with the gate
removed. Compare it to Section 10.10: an LSTM's cell state update is exactly this running average with
$\alpha$ replaced by a forget gate that is computed from the input rather than fixed. The step from this
section to the next is the step from a *constant* time scale to a *content-dependent* one, and that single
change is what made gated architectures work.

Framed that way, the whole progression of the chapter is legible. A vanilla RNN has one time scale
determined by the eigenvalues of $W$. A leaky unit has a per-unit time scale, fixed or learned but the
same at every step. An LSTM has a per-unit, per-timestep time scale chosen by the network as it reads.
Attention has no time scale at all.

Skip connections through time are worth noting as an idea that migrated. The same construction applied
across layers rather than across time is the residual connection, and the reasoning is identical: shorten
the gradient path by adding an edge that skips the transformation. It worked far better in the layer
direction, because there the product is over *different* matrices and the ordinary path is not itself
degenerate.
