# 20.11 Drawing Samples from Autoencoders

Chapter 14 established that an autoencoder learns something about the data distribution. This section asks
how to turn that into a generator.

## The Markov chain

The construction is direct. A denoising autoencoder, per 14.5, maps a corrupted input to a point closer to
the data manifold. Alternate the two operations — corrupt, then reconstruct — and the result is a Markov
chain:

$$
\tilde x \sim C\left( \tilde x \mid x \right), \qquad x' \sim p_{\text{decoder}}\left( x' \mid f(\tilde x) \right)
$$

Run it repeatedly and the state wanders through the space, being pushed toward the manifold by the
decoder and away from it by the corruption.

The book states the theoretical result: under conditions including that the autoencoder is a consistent
estimator of the true conditional distribution, this chain's stationary distribution is the data
distribution. So an autoencoder trained purely to denoise implicitly defines a generative model, and
sampling from it requires no additional training.

## Conditions and difficulties

The book is careful about the qualifications. The result requires the autoencoder to be trained well
enough, and it inherits every difficulty of Chapter 17: the chain must be run to convergence, convergence
cannot be verified, and mixing between separated modes is slow for exactly the reasons of 17.5. The book
notes that the amount of corruption matters — too little and the chain moves slowly, too much and the
reconstruction is poor.

**Walkback training** is mentioned as a remedy: train the autoencoder on the chain's own multi-step
trajectories rather than on single-step corruptions, so that the model learns to correct the errors it
actually makes.

## My take

This section is one step from diffusion and the step it does not take is instructive.

The chain here alternates a **fixed** amount of corruption with reconstruction, and its stationary
distribution is the target — which means it inherits the mixing problem, because reaching a distant mode
requires a random walk through the space. That is the failure of 17.5, and it is why this method never
produced good samples.

Diffusion changes one thing and everything follows. The noise level is not fixed: it starts very large
and is **annealed down on a fixed schedule**. At high noise the distribution is nearly a Gaussian, so
every mode is reachable from the start and no walk between modes is required. At low noise the model
makes fine corrections. There is no stationary distribution and no convergence question — the chain has a
predetermined length and ends when the schedule does.

Walkback is the other near-miss. Training on the model's own multi-step trajectories, so that it learns to
correct its own errors, is the right instinct and it is what the exposure-bias problem of Section 10.2
calls for. Diffusion gets it for free, because training on a randomly-sampled noise level at each step
means the model is trained on every point the sampler will visit.

So the ingredients in this section are: a corruption process, a learned reverse step, an iterative chain,
and training on the trajectory. All four are in diffusion. What is missing is the noise schedule, which
sounds like a detail and is the whole thing.
