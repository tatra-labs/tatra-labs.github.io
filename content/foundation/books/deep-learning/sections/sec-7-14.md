# 7.14 Tangent Distance, Tangent Prop, and Manifold Tangent Classifier

Chapter 7 closes with three related methods built on the manifold hypothesis of Section 5.11. All three
are historical, and the section is worth reading for the idea rather than the algorithms.

## The three methods

**Tangent distance** is a non-parametric nearest-neighbour algorithm with a modified metric. Instead of
the ordinary distance between two points, it uses the distance between the *manifolds* they lie on,
approximated locally by the tangent planes at each point. Two images of the same digit at slightly
different rotations are far apart in pixel space and close in tangent distance, because the rotation
direction is one of the tangent directions being factored out. The tangent vectors must be specified in
advance, by knowing which transformations leave the class unchanged.

**Tangent prop** applies the same idea to a neural network by adding a penalty. The classifier output
should not change as the input moves along a known tangent direction $v^{(i)}$, so the penalty is on the
directional derivative:

$$
\Omega(f) = \sum_i \left( \nabla_x f(x)^{\top} v^{(i)} \right)^2
$$

This requires the tangent directions to be supplied by hand, from prior knowledge of the invariances the
task demands.

**The manifold tangent classifier** removes that requirement. It uses a contractive autoencoder — Section
14.7 — to *estimate* the tangent directions from unlabelled data, then applies the tangent-prop penalty
using the estimated directions. The invariances are learned rather than declared.

## The comparison with augmentation

The book draws the contrast directly. Dataset augmentation trains the network on explicitly transformed
inputs; tangent prop penalises the derivative along the transformation direction instead. The
regularisation is analytic rather than sampled, and it is therefore infinitesimal — it constrains
behaviour for small perturbations only, whereas augmentation with a large rotation constrains behaviour
at that rotation.

The authors also connect tangent prop to double backpropagation, which penalises the norm of the gradient
in all directions, and to adversarial training in 7.13, which penalises change in the worst direction.
The three are the same construction with different choices of which directions to care about: known ones,
all of them, or the worst one.

## My take

That last observation is the reason to read the section, and it is the most compact idea in the chapter.
Three techniques that look unrelated are one template — *penalise how much the output changes when the
input moves* — differing only in the set of directions considered. Tangent prop picks directions from
prior knowledge, double backprop takes all directions, adversarial training takes the maximising
direction, and the manifold tangent classifier learns the directions from data.

None of these methods is in use. They were displaced by augmentation, which is cruder, cheaper, and does
not require estimating tangent planes or taking gradients of gradients. That is a recurring pattern in
this chapter: the more principled method loses to the one that scales.

The idea did not disappear, though — it moved. The manifold tangent classifier's move of *learning* the
invariances rather than declaring them is what contrastive self-supervised learning does at scale, and
Jacobian penalties remain standard in flow models and in the spectral-normalisation family used to
stabilise GANs. Chapter 7 ends with a set of dead algorithms built on a live idea.
