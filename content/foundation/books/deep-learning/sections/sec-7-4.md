# 7.4 Dataset Augmentation

The most direct form of regularisation: if the model would generalise better with more data, make more
data.

## Where it works

The book is precise about scope. Augmentation is most effective for **classification**, because a
classifier must map a very high-dimensional input to a single category and is therefore expected to be
invariant to a wide range of transformations. New examples can be produced by transforming the inputs and
keeping the labels. Density estimation, by contrast, admits no such trick — there is no transformation
that yields a new sample without already knowing the density.

**Object recognition** is the standard beneficiary. Translating an image by a few pixels, rotating it
slightly, or scaling it produces a valid new example, and the authors note that these operations improve
generalisation substantially even for networks that already have translation invariance built in.

## The caution

Transformations must preserve the label, and the book's example is exact: rotation and horizontal flips
are unsafe for optical character recognition, because they turn `b` into `d` and `6` into `9`. The
correct augmentation set is a property of the task, not of the data type.

## Noise as augmentation

Injecting noise into the input is a form of augmentation, and the book notes that neural networks are not
automatically robust to it — training with noise deliberately is what produces robustness. This connects
to 7.5, and the authors mention that a denoising autoencoder is built on exactly this principle. Noise
can also be added to hidden layers, which is best understood as augmentation at multiple levels of
abstraction, and is the reading of dropout the book will develop in 7.12.

Speech recognition gets its own note: adding random noise to the input waveform is standard practice.

## The methodological point

The section closes with an argument about benchmarking. When comparing two algorithms, the augmentation
used must be controlled, because a hand-designed augmentation scheme can dramatically improve results and
a comparison that varies both is measuring nothing. What looks like a better algorithm is often a better
augmentation pipeline.

## My take

That last argument deserves more prominence than it gets, because the decade after publication proved it
repeatedly. AutoAugment, RandAugment and the various learned policies produced accuracy gains on ImageNet
comparable to architectural changes that received far more attention, and a good deal of the reported
progress on standard benchmarks over that period is augmentation improving rather than models improving.

Augmentation also turned out to matter for a reason the section does not anticipate. In **contrastive
self-supervised learning**, the augmentation set is not a regulariser bolted onto the task — it *is* the
task, since the objective is to make two augmented views of the same image agree. The choice of
transformations determines what the representation becomes invariant to and therefore what it can
represent. A framing that treats augmentation as a way of manufacturing extra examples misses this
entirely, and it is the largest thing this section is missing from the vantage of 2026.

The label-preservation caution generalises too. The reason augmentation is harder for text than for
images is that almost no cheap transformation of a sentence reliably preserves its meaning, and the
augmentations that do work are mostly generated by other models.
