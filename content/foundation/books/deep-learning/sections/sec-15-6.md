# 15.6 Providing Clues to Discover Underlying Causes

Chapter 15 closes by answering the question it has been circling: if a good representation is one that
recovers the underlying causal factors, and the data alone does not determine them, where does the extra
information come from?

## Regularisation as a clue

The answer is that the learner needs **hints** about which factors to care about, and those hints take the
form of regularisation strategies. The book lists the ones it has covered, reframing each as a statement
about the underlying causes:

- **Smoothness** — the function changes slowly, so nearby inputs share causes.
- **Linearity** — some relationships are linear, which permits extrapolation but can produce extreme
  predictions far from the data.
- **Multiple explanatory factors** — the data is generated by several underlying causes, which motivates
  distributed representations.
- **Causal factors** — the learned factors are causes of the observed data, not effects.
- **Depth** — the causes are organised hierarchically, with high-level concepts defined in terms of
  simpler ones.
- **Shared factors across tasks** — the multi-task assumption of 7.7.
- **Manifolds** — probability mass concentrates on low-dimensional regions.
- **Natural clustering** — connected regions of the manifold correspond to single classes.
- **Temporal and spatial coherence** — factors change slowly, the slowness principle of 13.3.
- **Sparsity** — most factors are irrelevant to most examples.
- **Simplicity of factor dependencies** — good high-level representations relate to each other simply,
  often linearly.

## My take

This list is the best single summary of what deep learning assumes about the world, and it is buried at
the end of a chapter most readers skim. It is worth reading as the answer to the no-free-lunch theorem
from 5.2: since no learner is universally good, a learner must encode assumptions, and these are the
assumptions.

Two of these have done nearly all the work in the decade since, and the others much less.

**Depth and multiple explanatory factors** are the two that scaled. Every large model is a deep
distributed representation, and nothing has displaced that combination.

**Simplicity of factor dependencies** is the underrated entry, and its confirmation was one of the more
surprising empirical results of the period. High-level concepts really do turn out to relate linearly in
learned representations — this is why linear probes work, why steering a model's behaviour by adding a
vector to its activations works at all, and why the word-embedding arithmetic that made *king − man +
woman ≈ queen* famous is not a coincidence. The assumption was listed here as a plausible prior; it
appears to be broadly true.

**Causal factors** is the entry that has not been vindicated. There is no strong evidence that large
models recover causal structure rather than statistical structure, and the robustness benefit that would
follow has not materialised.

The most useful way to read this section is as a checklist for a new problem. Which of these hold for your
data? The ones that hold tell you which architectures and regularisers are appropriate, and the ones that
fail tell you why the standard toolkit is underperforming. Tabular data violates depth, manifolds and
spatial coherence simultaneously, which is a compact explanation of why gradient-boosted trees still win
there.
