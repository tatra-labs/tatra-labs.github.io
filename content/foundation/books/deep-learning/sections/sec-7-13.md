# 7.13 Adversarial Training

A short section on a finding that was surprising in 2014 and is still not fully resolved: networks with
near-human accuracy can be fooled by input changes a human cannot see.

## The phenomenon

Starting from an example $x$ that a network classifies correctly, a search finds a nearby $x'$ — visually
indistinguishable, differing by an imperceptible perturbation — that the network classifies as something
else entirely, often with high confidence. These are **adversarial examples**, and the book notes they
transfer: an example crafted against one model often fools another trained on different data.

## The linear explanation

The book's preferred explanation is that the cause is **excessive linearity**, not excessive nonlinearity
as one might guess. Neural networks are built out of nearly linear components, deliberately, because that
makes them easy to optimise. A linear function of a high-dimensional input is extremely sensitive to
small coordinated changes: perturbing each of $n$ inputs by $\epsilon$ can move a linear output by as
much as $\epsilon$ times the sum of the absolute weights, which grows with dimension.

So the property that makes networks trainable is the property that makes them fragile. The book is
explicit that this is the same argument as Section 6.3's case for piecewise-linear units, seen from the
other side.

## Adversarial training

The response is to generate adversarial examples during training and include them in the training set.
The book reports that this reduces error on the original test set as well, which makes it a regulariser
rather than only a defence — it encodes the assumption that the function should be locally constant in a
small neighbourhood of every training point, which is a stronger and more targeted version of the
smoothness that dataset augmentation supplies.

**Virtual adversarial examples** extend the idea to unlabelled data. Rather than perturbing toward a
wrong label, perturb to maximise the change in the model's *own* predicted distribution, and train the
model to be insensitive to that perturbation. No labels are required, so this gives a semi-supervised
method along the lines of 7.6.

## My take

The linear explanation is elegant and the field has since concluded it is at best incomplete. The most
influential later account is that adversarial examples exploit **non-robust features** — patterns that
are genuinely predictive on the data distribution but imperceptible to humans. On that reading the model
is not malfunctioning; it is using real signal that we do not share, and the vulnerability is a
consequence of a training objective that never asked for human-aligned features.

Adversarial training is now understood to be more expensive and less free than this section suggests.
The consistent finding after publication is a **robustness-accuracy trade-off**: models trained to
resist perturbations lose clean accuracy, sometimes substantially, and the section's report that
adversarial training improves ordinary test error does not generally replicate at scale. It also costs
several times the compute, since generating each adversarial example requires its own gradient steps.

The virtual-adversarial idea aged much better than the defensive one. Its descendants — consistency
regularisation and the whole family of methods that train a model to agree with itself under
perturbation — are central to modern semi-supervised learning, and the perturbation there is usually
augmentation rather than an adversarial search. The mechanism from this section survived; the adversary
did not.
