# 3.11 Bayes' Rule

Half a page for the identity that lets you reverse the direction of a conditional. The derivation is two
lines from Section 3.5; the interest is entirely in what it is used for.

## The rule

$$
P(x \mid y) = \frac{P(x) P(y \mid x)}{P(y)}
$$

It follows from the definition of conditional probability applied in both directions. The denominator is
rarely known directly and is usually computed by marginalising the numerator:

$$
P(y) = \sum_x P(x) P(y \mid x)
$$

so no separate knowledge of $P(y)$ is required. In Bayesian vocabulary $P(x)$ is the **prior**,
$P(y \mid x)$ the **likelihood**, $P(x \mid y)$ the **posterior**, and $P(y)$ the **evidence** or
marginal likelihood.

## My take

Every hard problem in Part III is this formula with an intractable denominator. That is worth stating
plainly, because the rule itself is so simple that its cost is invisible.

The numerator is usually easy: a prior you chose and a likelihood you defined. The denominator requires
summing over every value $x$ can take, and in a model with high-dimensional latent variables there are
exponentially many. So the posterior is *defined* but not *computable*, and the three main responses to
that fact are three chapters of this book. Chapter 17 estimates the sum by sampling. Chapter 19 sidesteps
it by optimising a bound. Chapter 18 confronts the closely related problem of an intractable partition
function head on.

The other thing to carry forward is that Bayes' rule is what separates **discriminative** from
**generative** modelling. A discriminative model learns $P(y \mid x)$ directly. A generative model learns
$P(x \mid y)$ and $P(y)$, then uses this identity to get $P(y \mid x)$ when a decision is needed. The
generative route asks for strictly more — a full model of how the inputs are distributed — and the case
for paying that price is made in Section 5.7 and again, with a different answer, in Chapter 20.
