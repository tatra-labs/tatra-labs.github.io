# 20.1 Boltzmann Machines

The final chapter opens with the model that gave the field its energy-based tradition, and it is worth
understanding as the origin of everything in Chapters 17 and 18.

## The model

A Boltzmann machine is defined over $d$ binary random variables by an energy function

$$
E(x) = -x^{\top} U x - b^{\top} x
$$

with $U$ a matrix of pairwise weights and $b$ a vector of biases. The distribution is Boltzmann:
$P(x) = \exp(-E(x)) / Z$, with $Z$ the partition function of Chapter 18.

Introduced with all units visible, the model can only capture pairwise interactions among the observed
variables, which is limiting. Splitting the units into **visible** units $v$ and **hidden** units $h$
removes the restriction: the marginal distribution over the visible units, obtained by summing out the
hidden ones, can represent higher-order dependencies. With enough hidden units, the Boltzmann machine is a
**universal approximator of discrete distributions**.

## The learning rule

Maximum likelihood gives the positive and negative phases of 18.1, and for this model they take a
particularly simple form. The derivative of the log-probability with respect to a weight $U_{i,j}$ depends
only on the statistics of units $i$ and $j$ — the difference between the correlation of the two units
under the data and under the model.

The book emphasises why this is notable: the update for a connection depends only on the two units it
connects, so learning is **local**. A unit does not need to know anything about the rest of the network,
which the authors note gives the rule a biological plausibility that back-propagation lacks — and which
motivated much of the interest in these models.

The catch is where the two statistics come from. The data statistics are cheap; the model statistics
require samples from the model, which is Chapter 17's problem.

## My take

The locality property is the most interesting thing here, and its history is instructive. It was a major
part of the appeal of Boltzmann machines — a learning rule requiring no global coordination, resembling
Hebbian learning, plausibly implementable by neurons. It was also, in the end, worth nothing practically.
Back-propagation is not local and won anyway, because the criterion that mattered was whether the model
could be trained to do something useful, not whether the rule could run on wetware.

That is a recurring lesson in this book and it is worth taking seriously: biological plausibility has
predicted almost nothing about what works. Convolution's borrowing from V1, in Section 9.10, is the one
clear success, and it borrowed an architecture rather than a learning rule.

The universal-approximation result carries the same caveat as the one in 6.4. With enough hidden units the
model can represent any discrete distribution, and the number of units required and the difficulty of
finding the parameters are both unaddressed. In practice both were prohibitive, and the phrase "with
enough hidden units" is doing all the work.
