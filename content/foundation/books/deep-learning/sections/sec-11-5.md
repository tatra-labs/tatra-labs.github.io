# 11.5 Debugging Strategies

The problem the section opens with is real and specific: when a machine learning system performs badly,
it is usually impossible to tell whether the algorithm is wrong or the implementation is. The model is
adaptive, so a broken component is often **compensated for** by the rest of the system, and training
proceeds to a plausible-looking loss with a bug in it.

## Six tactics

**Visualise the model in action.** Look at the actual detections drawn on the image, listen to the
generated speech. Quantitative metrics on test error appear objective and can conceal that the system is
succeeding for the wrong reason, or that the evaluation itself is misconfigured.

**Visualise the worst mistakes.** Sort examples by the model's confidence in its wrong answer and inspect
the top of the list. This localises problems in preprocessing and labelling far faster than aggregate
metrics do.

**Reason about software using train and test error.** If training error is low and test error is high, it
is likely that training works and the model overfits — or that the test-time pipeline reloads the model
incorrectly, or preprocesses differently. If both are high, the fault could be anywhere.

**Fit a tiny dataset.** Train on a single example, or a handful. A model that cannot drive the training
error to zero on one example has a defect, not a learning problem — the book notes even a classifier with
only biases should be able to fit one example. This is the fastest test in the list and it isolates the
whole training loop.

**Compare back-propagated derivatives to numerical derivatives.** For a custom operation, check the
analytic gradient against a finite-difference estimate. The book recommends **centred differences**,
which are more accurate than the one-sided version, and warns that the test should use a small number of
inputs since finite differencing is expensive.

**Monitor histograms of activations and gradients.** Collected over a minibatch and plotted over training,
these reveal saturation — how often rectifiers are off, how often $\tanh$ units are near their limits —
and whether parameter updates are of a sensible size. The book gives a useful rule of thumb: the magnitude
of a parameter update over a minibatch should be around 1% of the parameter magnitude, not 50% and not
0.001%.

## My take

"Fit a tiny dataset" is the highest-value item and should be the first thing tried on any new training
setup. It takes minutes, it exercises the entire pipeline, and a failure is unambiguous — no model
architecture makes it impossible to memorise five examples. A large fraction of the time lost to
mysterious training behaviour is a bug that this test would have caught before the run started.

The 1% update rule is the second most useful item and the least known. It gives a concrete number for
whether a learning rate is roughly right, obtainable in a few steps rather than by watching a loss curve
for an hour, and it is worth logging permanently rather than only when something is wrong.

The point about adaptivity masking bugs is the deepest observation in the section, and it generalises
uncomfortably. A model with a broken data augmentation, a mislabelled class, or an off-by-one in the
positional encoding will still train, still converge, and still produce a number — just a slightly worse
one than it should have. Since nobody knows what the right number is, the bug is invisible. This is why
the visualisation advice matters more than it sounds: looking at outputs directly is often the only way
to notice that a system is quietly wrong rather than merely mediocre.
