# 10.2 Recurrent Neural Networks

The longest section in the chapter. It gives three design patterns, the forward equations, the cost of
training, and one shortcut that turns out to matter.

## Three patterns

**Hidden-to-hidden recurrence with an output at every step.** The most general form, and the one the rest
of the section develops.

**Output-to-hidden recurrence with an output at every step.** The recurrent connection carries the
*prediction* forward rather than the hidden state. Strictly less powerful — the output is trained to
match the target and so cannot carry arbitrary information about the past — but it has a compensating
advantage described below.

**Hidden-to-hidden recurrence with a single output at the end.** Reads the whole sequence, then emits one
value. This is the shape used for sequence classification.

## The forward pass

For the general pattern, with $U$, $W$ and $V$ the input, recurrent and output matrices:

$$
a^{(t)} = b + W h^{(t-1)} + U x^{(t)}
$$

$$
h^{(t)} = \tanh\left( a^{(t)} \right)
$$

$$
o^{(t)} = c + V h^{(t)}
$$

and the prediction is $\operatorname{softmax}(o^{(t)})$. The total loss is the sum of the per-step losses,
which for a probabilistic model is the negative log-likelihood of the sequence.

The book notes that this architecture is **universal**: any function computable by a Turing machine can be
computed by a recurrent network of finite size.

## The cost of training

The gradient is obtained by running back-propagation on the unfolded graph, which is called
**back-propagation through time**. The costs are the reason recurrent networks are expensive:

- Runtime is $O(\tau)$ and **cannot be parallelised** across time, because each state depends on the
  previous one. The forward pass is inherently sequential.
- Memory is $O(\tau)$, since every hidden state must be retained until its gradient is consumed.

## Teacher forcing

Models with output-to-hidden recurrence can be trained differently. Because the recurrent input is the
output, and the correct output is known during training, the *ground truth* can be fed in rather than the
model's own prediction. This is **teacher forcing**, and it removes the sequential dependency during
training — every step's target is available immediately, so the steps can be computed in parallel.

The drawback is a train/test mismatch. At generation time the true outputs are unavailable and the model
consumes its own predictions, which may lie far from anything it saw during training. The book's suggested
mitigations are to train on a mixture of teacher-forced and free-running inputs, or to use curriculum
learning to shift gradually from one to the other.

## RNNs as graphical models

The section also reads the architecture probabilistically. A recurrent network trained with
log-likelihood is applying the chain rule of Section 3.6, with the hidden state standing in for the full
conditioning history. Where a graphical model over a sequence would need edges from every past step to
the present one, the recurrent state factorises that dependence through a fixed-size bottleneck — the
parameter cost stops growing with sequence length.

## My take

The parallelism observation is the most consequential thing in the chapter, and it is stated here as a
cost rather than as a verdict. Runtime $O(\tau)$ with no parallelisation across time meant that a
recurrent model could not use hardware that had become overwhelmingly parallel, and that constraint —
not accuracy — is what ended the architecture's dominance. The transformer's central claim was
parallelism across positions, and the accuracy improvements followed from being able to train much larger
models rather than from any advantage per parameter.

Teacher forcing is worth reading closely because it survived the architecture. Every autoregressive
language model is trained with teacher forcing — conditioned on the true prefix, all positions in
parallel — and generates by consuming its own outputs. The mismatch described here as a drawback of one
recurrent variant is now a universal property of how these models are trained, and **exposure bias** is
the name it acquired. The mitigations the book suggests were tried and largely abandoned; what is used
instead is reinforcement learning from the model's own samples, which is a much heavier answer to the
same problem.
