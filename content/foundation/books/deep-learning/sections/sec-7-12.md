# 7.12 Dropout

The longest section in Chapter 7, and the one describing the technique that mattered most. Dropout is
presented as a way of getting the benefit of bagging over an exponential number of networks at the cost
of training one.

## The method

On each minibatch, sample a binary mask and multiply it into the units. A unit whose mask entry is zero
is removed for that step, along with all its connections. Typical probabilities of **keeping** a unit are
$0.5$ for hidden units and $0.8$ for input units.

Because each mask defines a different subnetwork, and a network with $n$ units admits $2^n$ masks,
dropout trains an ensemble of exponentially many models that **share parameters**. The relationship to
7.11 is close but not exact: in bagging the models are independent and each is trained to convergence on
its own dataset; in dropout the models share weights and the overwhelming majority of subnetworks are
never sampled at all.

## Inference

The ensemble prediction requires averaging over all masks, which is intractable. Dropout uses the
**weight scaling inference rule**: run the full network once with all units present, and multiply each
unit's outgoing weights by its keep probability, so that the expected input to each unit matches what it
saw during training. The book notes this is exact for a wide class of models without nonlinearities and
works well empirically otherwise.

Equivalently, and more commonly implemented, the scaling is done during training instead — divide by the
keep probability at training time so that inference needs no adjustment at all.

## Why it works, and its limits

Dropout is computationally cheap, requiring only mask generation and multiplication, and it imposes
almost no restriction on model type or training procedure. The book cites results showing it more
effective than other cheap regularisers such as weight decay.

Its limitation is stated plainly: dropout is **less effective when few labelled examples are available**.
Where the dataset is very small, other methods including Bayesian ones do better.

The section also gives an argument beyond ensembling — dropout forces units to be robust to the loss of
their neighbours, so features must be useful on their own rather than in fragile co-adapted groups. This
is the interpretation that survives best. And it points out that multiplicative noise is stronger than
additive noise, because a unit cannot compensate for it by growing its activation.

## My take

Dropout was arguably the most important regularisation result of the 2010s and its role has shrunk more
than most readers expect. In convolutional networks it was largely displaced by batch normalisation,
which regularises as a side effect of its normalising behaviour, and the two interact badly — the
variance shift between training and inference means using both is often worse than using either.

In transformers dropout persists but at low rates, and the largest language models frequently use none at
all. The reason follows directly from the book's own caveat: dropout helps when a model is large relative
to its data, and a model trained for a single pass over a corpus it never repeats is not overfitting in
the sense this chapter is about. When the data is effectively unlimited, the regulariser has nothing to
do.

The idea that outlasted the technique is the **co-adaptation** argument. Forcing a representation to work
under random ablation of its parts is a strong structural pressure, and it reappears in places that have
nothing to do with dropout — stochastic depth, masked language modelling, and the routing dropout used
in mixtures of experts. Monte Carlo dropout, keeping the masks on at inference to obtain an uncertainty
estimate, is the other survival, and it is a direct application of the ensemble reading given here.
