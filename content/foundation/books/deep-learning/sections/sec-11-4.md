# 11.4 Selecting Hyperparameters

Two approaches — do it by hand, or automate it — and a clear-eyed account of why the manual one was still
winning in 2016.

## Manual search

Tuning by hand requires understanding what each hyperparameter does. The book frames all of them through
one quantity: **effective capacity**, which is constrained by three things — the representational capacity
of the model, the ability of the learning algorithm to minimise the cost, and the degree of
regularisation.

Most hyperparameters have a U-shaped relationship with generalisation error. At one extreme capacity is
too low and the model underfits; at the other it is too high and the model overfits; the target sits
between. Not every hyperparameter can reach both ends of the curve — some, like the number of hidden
units, only increase capacity, and are bounded by memory rather than by the curve.

The **learning rate** is singled out as the most important hyperparameter of all, on the grounds that it
controls effective capacity in a more complicated way than the others: it is highest when the rate is
neither too large nor too small. Too large and training diverges, with the cost increasing sharply. Too
small and training is slow and may stall at a high value.

The book's practical procedure: monitor training and test error, and diagnose from the gap. Training error
higher than the target means increase capacity. Test error much higher than training error means increase
regularisation or gather more data. Its blunt summary is that the brute-force answer to most problems is
to increase model size and regularisation while collecting more data.

## Automatic search

**Grid search** enumerates a small set of values per hyperparameter and tries every combination. Values
should be selected on a **logarithmic scale**, and the cost grows exponentially with the number of
hyperparameters.

**Random search** samples each hyperparameter from a distribution instead. The book reports it converges
much faster than grid search, and explains why: when only some hyperparameters matter, a grid wastes
repeated trials on identical values of the important one while varying an unimportant one. Random search
gives a distinct value of every hyperparameter on every trial, so $n$ trials explore $n$ values of the
important one rather than the handful a grid affords.

**Model-based optimisation** — Bayesian methods fitting a surrogate to the validation-error surface — is
described honestly: promising, and not yet reliably better than a human expert, with the book noting it
sometimes performs comparably and sometimes worse.

## My take

The random-versus-grid result is the most actionable finding in the chapter and remains underused. The
argument is not that randomness is magic; it is that hyperparameter importance is very unequal, and a grid
allocates its budget as though it were uniform. Anyone still running grid searches is paying an
exponential cost for a benefit that does not exist.

The learning-rate emphasis has held up entirely. It is still the first thing to tune, still the one most
likely to be catastrophically wrong, and the failure signatures the book describes — a diverging loss for
too large, a flat one for too small — are still what to look for.

Two things have changed. Model-based optimisation matured, and the honest current assessment is that it
helps for expensive-to-evaluate problems with a moderate number of hyperparameters, which is not the
typical deep learning setting. And for very large models the entire approach became infeasible, since a
single run costs too much to repeat: the practice instead is to tune at small scale and **transfer** the
settings upward using known scaling relationships, which is a technique this chapter has no notion of.

The U-shaped framing also inherits the caveat from 5.2. The curve is a good picture for regularisation
strength and a poor one for parameter count, where double descent means more capacity often continues to
help past the point this section says it should stop.
