# 3.13 Information Theory

The section that gives the book its loss function. Everything trained with cross-entropy — which is
nearly everything — is an application of the four definitions here.

## From surprise to entropy

The organising intuition is that **an unlikely event is more informative than a likely one**. Three
requirements pin the measure down: likely events carry little information, certain events carry none, and
independent events carry additive information. The unique function satisfying all three is the
**self-information**:

$$
I(x) = -\log P(x)
$$

With the natural logarithm the unit is the **nat**; with base 2 it is the **bit** or shannon. One nat is
the information gained by observing an event of probability $1/e$.

Averaging self-information over the distribution gives the **Shannon entropy**:

$$
H(P) = \mathbb E_{x \sim P}[-\log P(x)]
$$

the expected number of nats needed to encode a sample. Near-deterministic distributions have low entropy;
uniform distributions have the highest. The convention $0 \log 0 = 0$ makes the formula well defined at
zero-probability states.

## Divergence and cross-entropy

For two distributions over the same variable, the **Kullback-Leibler divergence** measures the extra cost
of coding samples from $P$ using a code designed for $Q$:

$$
D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = \mathbb E_{x \sim P}\left[ \log P(x) - \log Q(x) \right]
$$

It is non-negative, and zero exactly when $P$ and $Q$ are the same distribution — the same
distribution almost everywhere, in the continuous case. It is **not symmetric**, so it is not a distance,
and the asymmetry is a real modelling choice rather than a technicality.

The **cross-entropy** is closely related:

$$
H(P, Q) = H(P) + D_{KL}(P \parallel Q) = -\mathbb E_{x \sim P}[\log Q(x)]
$$

Since $H(P)$ does not depend on $Q$, minimising cross-entropy with respect to $Q$ is identical to
minimising the KL divergence.

## The asymmetry, concretely

The book's illustration is fitting a single Gaussian $q$ to a bimodal $p$. Minimising
$D_{KL}(p \parallel q)$ puts high probability everywhere $p$ has mass, so $q$ smears across both modes
and covers the empty region between them. Minimising $D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$ puts low probability
everywhere $p$ has little mass, so $q$ picks one mode and ignores the other. Neither is correct; they
answer different questions.

## My take

Three things follow from this section that are worth holding together.

**Cross-entropy is not a heuristic.** It is the negative log-likelihood, and Section 5.5 shows that
maximum likelihood *is* minimisation of the KL divergence between the empirical distribution and the
model. The classification loss everyone uses by default has a derivation, and it is here.

**The direction of the KL determines the failure mode.** Maximum likelihood minimises
$D_{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \parallel p_{\text{model}})$ — the mode-covering direction — which is why
likelihood-trained generative models produce blurry averages rather than sharp samples. Variational
inference in Chapter 19 minimises the reverse direction and therefore under-estimates uncertainty
instead. Knowing which direction a method uses predicts how it will fail.

**Entropy is the bridge to the compression view of learning.** A model with low cross-entropy on held-out
data is literally a good compressor of that data, since the expected code length is the cross-entropy.
That equivalence is what makes bits-per-character a meaningful metric for language models, and it is the
reason the same number can be quoted as a loss and as a compression ratio.
