# 9.10 The Neuroscientific Basis for Convolutional Networks

The book calls convolutional networks the greatest success story of biologically inspired artificial
intelligence, and this section explains what was borrowed and — more usefully — what was not.

## What the biology says

The lineage begins with Hubel and Wiesel's recordings from cat visual cortex, which established that
neurons in the early visual system respond strongly to a small, specific pattern of light and hardly at
all to anything else.

**V1**, the first cortical area for vision, has three properties that convolutional networks copy. It is
arranged in a **spatial map**, mirroring the layout of the retina, which the two-dimensional feature map
reproduces. It contains **simple cells**, whose response is approximately a linear function of a small
local receptive field, which the convolution units imitate. And it contains **complex cells**, which
respond to the same features as simple cells but are **invariant to small shifts** in position, and often
to changes in contrast polarity — which is what pooling imitates.

The receptive fields of simple cells are well described by **Gabor functions**: a sinusoid multiplied by
a Gaussian envelope, giving an oriented, spatially localised, frequency-selective detector. Learned first
layers in convolutional networks reliably produce something close to this, whether trained supervised,
unsupervised, or — per 9.9 — not trained at all.

Beyond V1 the signal passes through V2 and V4 to the **inferotemporal cortex**, and the book notes that
IT behaves in some respects like the final feature layer of a convolutional network: the last stage of a
feedforward pipeline before recognition. Neurons responding to highly specific concepts — the medial
temporal lobe "Halle Berry neuron" — are mentioned as an approximate biological counterpart to the last
layer's units, with the caveat that they respond to the concept across modalities, including the written
name.

## What was not borrowed

The book is careful and the list is long. Human vision is **low resolution** except in the fovea, and the
impression of seeing a whole scene at high detail is constructed by memory across **saccades** — nothing
in a convolutional network resembles this. Biological vision is integrated with hearing, mood and
expectation. It has extensive **feedback** connections from higher areas to lower, which the feedforward
model has none of. And little is known about what V2 and V4 actually compute.

The book also notes that back-propagation is not known to be implemented by the brain, that the way
biological simple cells respond is captured by reverse correlation experiments rather than by anything
resembling gradient descent, and that neuroscience was the source of the architecture but has told us
almost nothing about how to train it.

## My take

The honest reading of this section is that neuroscience supplied one idea — local receptive fields with
pooled invariance, arranged in a hierarchy — and that everything else in a working system came from
elsewhere. That is a real contribution and a bounded one, and the field's habit of citing biological
plausibility as a general argument is not supported by the history here.

The feedback connections are the most interesting omission. Cortical vision has far more descending than
ascending connections, and a purely feedforward model discards the majority of the wiring. What arrived
later was not a return to biology but attention — a mechanism for letting later computation reweight
earlier representations — and it is at least suggestive that the architectural change with the largest
effect on vision since this book was written is the one that adds top-down influence.

Worth keeping the Gabor result specifically. That randomly initialised, unsupervised, and supervised
methods all converge on the same first-layer filters, and that those filters match a biological
measurement, is the strongest evidence in the chapter that the structure is a property of natural images
rather than of any particular learning algorithm.
