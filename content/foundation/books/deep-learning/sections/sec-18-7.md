# 18.7 Estimating the Partition Function

The chapter has spent six sections avoiding $Z$. This one asks what to do when it must actually be
computed, and the answer is that it can only be estimated.

## Why it is needed

Training can avoid the partition function; **evaluation** cannot. Comparing two undirected models by
likelihood requires normalised probabilities, and reporting a held-out likelihood requires knowing $Z$.
Without it, one can say a model assigns higher unnormalised score to the data than another does, which
means nothing, since the two models have different normalising constants.

## Importance sampling from a known model

The basic estimator is importance sampling from 17.2, applied to the ratio of two partition functions. If
a proposal model $p_0$ has a known $Z_0$ and samples can be drawn from it, then the ratio $Z_1 / Z_0$ can
be estimated by averaging the ratio of unnormalised densities over samples from $p_0$.

The book states the condition under which this fails, and it is the general failure of importance
sampling: if $p_0$ is not close to $p_1$, most samples land where $p_1$ has negligible mass, the weights
are dominated by rare samples, and the variance is enormous. For high-dimensional models, no simple $p_0$
is close enough.

## Annealed importance sampling

**AIS** bridges the gap with a sequence of intermediate distributions running from $p_0$ to $p_1$, usually
constructed as a geometric interpolation controlled by a temperature-like parameter. Each consecutive pair
is close enough for importance sampling to be well behaved, and the ratios multiply to give the overall
one. Transitions between the intermediate distributions are performed by MCMC.

The book describes AIS as the most common method for estimating the partition function of undirected
models, and notes it was introduced to the machine learning community for this purpose specifically.

**Bridge sampling** is the alternative, using a single well-chosen intermediate distribution rather than a
chain of them, and linked importance sampling combines the two ideas.

## My take

The most useful thing about this section is what its existence implies. An entire section of a textbook is
devoted to *estimating a number needed only to report a score*, and the estimate itself is stochastic with
no reliable error bar. That is a research field with a measurement problem, and measurement problems are
slow poison — progress requires comparing models, and comparisons here rest on an approximation whose
accuracy is unknown.

Later work bore this out. Careful studies found that AIS estimates of Boltzmann-machine likelihoods could
be substantially off, and that the direction of the error depended on the annealing schedule. Some
published comparisons between models were not reliable.

It is worth reading Chapter 18 as a whole with this in mind. A model family that cannot be trained without
approximation, cannot be sampled from reliably, and cannot be evaluated without a second layer of
approximation is carrying three separate sources of error, none of them bounded. That is not why
undirected models lost — the immediate cause was that directed models scaled better — but it is why the
loss was hard to notice while it was happening.

The contrast with an autoregressive model is total. Its likelihood is exact, computed in one forward pass,
and comparable across models with no estimation anywhere. That property is worth more than it sounds,
because it makes progress measurable.
