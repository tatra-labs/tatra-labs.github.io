# 2.11 The Determinant

The shortest section in the chapter, and it is here for one sentence: the determinant tells you what a
linear map does to volume.

## Definition and reading

The determinant $\det(A)$ maps a square matrix to a real number, and equals the product of the matrix's
eigenvalues:

$$
\det(A) = \prod_i \lambda_i
$$

The interpretation is geometric. The absolute value of the determinant is the factor by which the map
expands or contracts volume. If $\det(A) = 0$, space has been collapsed completely along at least one
direction — the map loses a dimension, and the matrix is singular. If $\lvert \det(A) \rvert = 1$, the
transformation preserves volume, which is the case for every orthogonal matrix.

## My take

The equation $\det(A) = \prod_i \lambda_i$ is the one to keep; the cofactor-expansion formula most
readers were taught is neither used in this book nor computed this way in practice, where the determinant
falls out of an LU factorisation.

Two places downstream depend on this section, and both are easy to miss.

The first is **volume in change of variables**. When a probability density is pushed through an
invertible map, its value must be corrected by the Jacobian determinant, because the map compressed or
stretched the volume element. Section 3.12 states the rule; normalizing flows are that rule turned into
an architecture, and the entire engineering problem of a flow model is designing a transformation
expressive enough to be interesting while keeping its Jacobian determinant cheap to compute.

The second is **the log-determinant in Gaussian densities**, which is where the term actually shows up
most often in practice — a term that a diagonal or low-rank covariance makes tractable and a full one
does not.

Neither use involves computing a determinant by hand. Both require knowing what it measures.
