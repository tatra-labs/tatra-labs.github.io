# 20.8 Other Boltzmann Machines

A brief catalogue of variants that do not fit the previous sections, and a closing note on what remains
unexplored.

## Discriminative training

Boltzmann machines are generative models, but nothing prevents training one on a **discriminative**
criterion instead. The **discriminative RBM** trains an RBM over the joint distribution of inputs and
labels by maximising $\log p(y \mid x)$ rather than $\log p(x, y)$, and the book reports this can
outperform the generative criterion when the goal is classification.

A hybrid objective — a weighted sum of the generative and discriminative log-likelihoods — is also
mentioned, which is the semi-supervised construction of Section 7.6 in energy-based form.

## Higher-order interactions

The standard Boltzmann machine has only **pairwise** terms in its energy function. The section notes that
higher-order interactions are possible: a three-way term coupling two hidden units and a visible unit
makes one hidden unit **gate** the interaction between the other two, which is a multiplicative rather
than additive combination.

Models of this kind appear in the mean-covariance RBM of 20.5 and in a family of gated models for
transformations between pairs of images, where one set of units encodes *how* the second image differs
from the first.

The book closes the section by observing that the space of possible Boltzmann machines is large and
largely unexplored, and that new variants continue to be introduced.

## My take

The multiplicative-interaction idea is the one that persisted, though not in this form. A unit that gates
the interaction between two others is exactly what an LSTM gate does in 10.10, and it is what attention
does at a larger scale: the attention weight is a product of a query and a key, so one representation
modulates how strongly another is read. Additive combinations of features can only sum evidence;
multiplicative ones can express conditionality, and that turns out to be the more useful primitive.

The discriminative-RBM finding is a small instance of a general point already made in 18.3 and worth
having met three times: **a model trained on a discriminative criterion is better at discrimination than
a model trained generatively, even when the generative model contains the discriminative one**. Training
on the quantity you will be evaluated on is nearly always better than training on a superset of it and
deriving the answer.

The closing observation — that the space is large and unexplored — reads differently in retrospect. The
space is still unexplored, and it was not abandoned because it was exhausted. It was abandoned because a
different family scaled, and there is no strong reason to believe every good idea in it was found. Energy-
based models have had a modest revival through their connection to score matching, and that revival came
from Chapter 18 rather than from anything in this chapter.
