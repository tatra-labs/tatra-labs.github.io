# 20.2 Restricted Boltzmann Machines

The workhorse of the deep learning revival, and a model whose design is a direct response to the sampling
problem of Chapter 17.

## The restriction

An RBM is a Boltzmann machine with a **bipartite** graph: a layer of visible units $v$, a layer of hidden
units $h$, connections between the layers, and **no connections within** either layer. The energy is

$$
E(v, h) = -b^{\top} v - c^{\top} h - v^{\top} W h
$$

and the distribution is $\exp(-E(v,h)) / Z$ as before. The partition function is still intractable.

## What the restriction buys

Everything, and it is worth being precise about why. Because there are no within-layer connections, the
hidden units are **conditionally independent given the visible units**, and vice versa. The conditionals
therefore factorise completely:

$$
P(h_i = 1 \mid v) = \sigma\left( c_i + v^{\top} W_{:,i} \right)
$$

and symmetrically for $P(v_j = 1 \mid h)$. Each is a sigmoid of an affine function — one matrix multiply
gives the parameters of every unit in a layer at once.

Three consequences follow:

- **Block Gibbs sampling.** Sample all hidden units simultaneously, then all visible units, alternating.
  Two matrix operations per sweep instead of thousands of sequential updates.
- **Tractable expectations.** The positive phase of the gradient can be computed exactly, since it
  requires only $\mathbb E[v h^{\top}]$ under the data-conditioned posterior, which factorises.
- **A simple gradient.** The derivative with respect to $W$ is a difference of two outer products, one
  from the data and one from the model.

Only the negative phase remains hard, and it is approximated with contrastive divergence or stochastic
maximum likelihood from Section 18.2.

## My take

The RBM is the clearest case in the book of an architecture chosen for the shape of its computation rather
than for anything about the data. The bipartite structure is not a claim that visible variables do not
interact — they obviously do — it is a claim that the interactions can be routed through hidden units, so
that the conditional distributions become a matrix multiply. It is a model designed around block Gibbs
sampling.

That design paid off historically and has not lasted. RBMs are what Hinton's 2006 result was built on, they
are the layers stacked in a deep belief network, and their training was what convinced the field that deep
models could be trained at all. They are not used now, for reasons that are all downstream of $Z$: the
gradient is approximate, sampling is unreliable, and likelihood cannot be evaluated without the estimation
machinery of 18.7.

Worth noticing the shape of the conditional above, though. A sigmoid of an affine function of the layer
below is exactly a feedforward layer, and the RBM's $P(h \mid v)$ is a one-layer neural network. The
connection is not a coincidence — it is why a trained RBM's weights could be used to initialise a
supervised network, which is what pretraining in Section 15.1 actually did. The generative model and the
feedforward layer were the same object all along, and the field eventually kept the layer and discarded
the generative story around it.
