# 17.2 Importance Sampling

A refinement of the basic estimator that changes which distribution the samples come from, either because
sampling from $p$ is impossible or because a different distribution gives a better estimate.

## The identity

The decomposition of an integrand into a distribution and a function is not unique. For any $q$ with
support covering $p$,

$$
p(x) f(x) = q(x) \frac{p(x) f(x)}{q(x)}
$$

so the same expectation can be estimated by sampling from $q$ and averaging the reweighted values:

$$
\hat s_q = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{p\left( x^{(i)} \right) f\left( x^{(i)} \right)}{q\left( x^{(i)} \right)}
$$

The ratio $p/q$ is the **importance weight**. The estimator is unbiased for any valid $q$, so the choice
of $q$ affects only the variance.

## The optimal proposal

The variance-minimising choice is

$$
q^{\ast}(x) \propto p(x) \left\lvert f(x) \right\rvert
$$

which concentrates samples where the integrand is large. The book notes that for positive $f$ the optimal
estimator has **zero variance** — a single sample gives the exact answer — and that this is unattainable,
since computing the normalising constant of $q^{\ast}$ is the original problem.

## The danger

A poor proposal makes things dramatically worse rather than slightly worse. If $q$ has little mass
somewhere $p(x) f(x)$ is large, the ratio at those rare samples is enormous, and the variance can be very
large or **infinite**. The book warns that the estimator may appear well-behaved for a long time and then
be dominated by a single sample.

**Biased importance sampling** avoids needing normalised $p$ or $q$ by dividing by the sum of the weights
rather than by $n$. It is biased, with the bias vanishing as $n$ grows, and it is what is used in practice
because the normalising constants usually are not available.

## My take

The failure mode is the thing to remember, because it is silent. An importance sampling estimate with an
inadequate proposal does not report an error — it reports a plausible number that is wrong, and the
diagnostic is not the estimate itself but the distribution of the weights. If a handful of samples carry
almost all the weight, the effective sample size is that handful regardless of how many were drawn.

This matters well beyond Monte Carlo integration. Off-policy reinforcement learning corrects for the
mismatch between the behaviour policy and the target policy with importance weights, and the variance
explosion described here is exactly why long-horizon off-policy estimates are unreliable and why the
weights are routinely clipped. PPO's clipped objective is a response to this section's problem.

The optimal-proposal result is also worth keeping for its structure, which recurs. The best possible
answer requires knowing the thing being computed; every practical method is an approximation to a
$q^{\ast}$ that cannot be constructed. Variational inference in Chapter 19 has the same shape — the
optimal $q$ is the true posterior, which is what was intractable.
