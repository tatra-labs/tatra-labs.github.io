# 13.4 Sparse Coding

The linear factor model with a sparsity-inducing prior, and the one member of the family with a genuinely
different computational structure — inference is an optimisation problem rather than a function
evaluation.

## The model

The generative side is the usual template, with Gaussian noise of fixed variance:

$$
p(x \mid h) = \mathcal{N}\left( x; W h + b, \frac{1}{\beta} I \right)
$$

What differs is the prior on $h$, chosen to concentrate mass near zero. The book names the Laplace prior
and the Student-$t$ and Cauchy alternatives; for the Laplace, the prior contributes an absolute-value
penalty to the log-density, so maximising the posterior means minimising

$$
\lambda \lVert h \rVert_1 + \beta \lVert x - W h \rVert_2^2
$$

reconstruction error plus an $L^1$ penalty on the code — the sparsity mechanism of Section 7.1, now
applied to the representation rather than the weights.

## Training

Because exact inference and exact maximum likelihood are intractable, sparse coding uses **MAP inference**
for $h$ and alternates: hold $W$ fixed and optimise $h$ for each example, then hold the codes fixed and
optimise $W$. Both halves are convex on their own; the joint problem is not.

The book is careful about what this costs. Because $h$ is obtained by running an optimisation, sparse
coding has **no parametric encoder** — computing the representation of a new example requires solving a
problem, not evaluating a function. That makes inference slow, and it means the model cannot be
back-propagated through as part of a larger network in the ordinary way.

The authors also note that the sparse-coding approach produces features that transfer well as inputs to
classifiers, and that samples from the model tend to look poor, because the model does not account for
the way its own reconstruction error accumulates.

## My take

The non-parametric encoder is the defining property and the reason sparse coding lost. Every deep
architecture that succeeded has a **feedforward** encoder: one pass, fixed cost, differentiable end to
end. An encoder that requires an inner optimisation loop cannot be composed with anything, cannot be
batched efficiently, and cannot be trained jointly with a downstream task. Section 14.8's predictive
sparse decomposition is the direct attempt to fix this by training a parametric encoder to *predict* the
optimisation's answer, and the variational autoencoder is the fully successful version of the same move —
replace inference-as-optimisation with a learned inference network.

That move — **amortised inference** — is one of the most important ideas in Part III, and it is easiest to
appreciate from here, where the un-amortised version is on display.

Sparse coding itself has had a striking second life in interpretability. Sparse autoencoders trained on
language model activations, which are the current standard tool for finding interpretable features, are
sparse coding applied to a representation rather than to raw data. The reason the $L^1$ penalty is used
there is the reason given in 7.10 and here: a sparse overcomplete basis tends to align its axes with
meaningful directions, and dense representations do not.
