# 20.4 Deep Boltzmann Machines

The fully undirected version of the deep belief network — cleaner in principle, harder in practice, and a
useful illustration of what the undirected formalism costs.

## The model

A deep Boltzmann machine is entirely undirected. Layers of binary hidden units are connected in a chain,
with connections between adjacent layers and none within a layer or between non-adjacent ones. The energy
is a sum of bilinear terms, one per pair of adjacent layers.

The layer structure preserves the property that made the RBM workable. Because there are no within-layer
connections, the units of any one layer are conditionally independent given the layers on either side. In
a DBM the layers can be split into **even** and **odd** groups, and within each group all units are
conditionally independent given the other group, so block Gibbs sampling updates half the network at a
time in two matrix operations.

## Where it differs from a DBN

The distinction the book draws is about inference. In a DBN the directed edges create explaining-away, so
the posterior over the latents is intractable and the standard treatment ignores it. In a DBM the
undirected structure means the posterior is also intractable, but it is **approximable by mean field**, and
that approximation is principled — Section 19.4's variational bound applies, and it is a genuine lower
bound rather than an ad hoc procedure.

The book presents this as the DBM's main theoretical advantage: it admits a variational lower bound on the
log-likelihood, which the DBN's mixed structure does not.

## The difficulties

They are substantial and the book does not hide them. Joint training of all layers at once is very hard,
so DBMs were usually trained by greedy layer-wise pretraining followed by joint refinement — the same
crutch as the DBN. The **centering trick**, reparameterising the units to have zero mean, is described as
improving joint trainability. **Multi-prediction DBMs** train the model to predict any subset of variables
from the rest, using back-propagation through the mean-field inference procedure, which gives a model good
at conditional prediction and not at density estimation.

Sampling requires MCMC at every gradient step, with all of Chapter 17's difficulties intact.

## My take

The multi-prediction variant is the item worth flagging, because of what it turned into. Training a model
to predict any subset of its variables from the rest, using an objective that does not attempt the joint
likelihood, is the masked-prediction objective — the same idea BERT is built on, arrived at here as a way
to escape the partition function rather than as a pretraining strategy. The book reports it produces good
conditional predictions and poor density estimates, which is an accurate description of a masked language
model.

The DBM as a whole is the clearest demonstration of the trade this part of the book describes. Its
undirected structure is theoretically tidier than the DBN's hybrid — it has a proper variational bound and
a symmetric interpretation — and it is harder to train, slower to sample, and impossible to evaluate
exactly. Every one of those costs traces to the partition function.

The centering trick is a small item with a familiar shape: reparameterise so the units have zero mean and
optimisation improves. That is what batch normalisation does, in Section 8.7, for entirely different
stated reasons. Zero-centring activations helps in both cases, and neither explanation is convincing.
