# 13.1 Probabilistic PCA and Factor Analysis

Part III opens with the simplest generative models there are: linear factor models, in which the data is
explained by a linear transformation of a low-dimensional latent variable plus noise.

## The template

Every model in this chapter has the same two lines. Sample an explanatory factor from a prior:

$$
h \sim p(h)
$$

and generate the observation by a linear map plus noise:

$$
x = W h + b + \text{noise}
$$

The prior must be factorial, $p(h) = \prod_i p(h_i)$, so the latent components are independent before the
data is seen. What distinguishes the members of the family is the choice of $p(h)$ and the form of the
noise.

## Factor analysis

The prior is a unit-variance Gaussian, $h \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and the noise is Gaussian with a
**diagonal** covariance $\psi$, one variance per observed variable. The latent variables therefore capture
all the dependencies *between* the observed variables, and everything left over is independent per
coordinate. That is the whole modelling assumption, stated cleanly.

The marginal distribution of $x$ is then Gaussian with covariance $W W^{\top} + \psi$.

## Probabilistic PCA

Constrain the noise variances to be **equal** across all coordinates, so $\psi = \sigma^2 I$. The
covariance becomes $W W^{\top} + \sigma^2 I$, and the model becomes probabilistic PCA.

The connection to Chapter 2 is exact and worth stating: as $\sigma \to 0$, probabilistic PCA converges to
conventional PCA, and the conditional expectation of $h$ given $x$ becomes an orthogonal projection onto
the space spanned by the columns of $W$. The algebraic derivation in 2.12 and this probabilistic one
describe the same object.

## My take

The template at the top of this section is the most useful thing to carry forward, because it is the
skeleton of every generative model in Part III and beyond. Sample a latent from a simple prior, push it
through a transformation, add noise. A variational autoencoder replaces the linear map with a neural
network and the exact posterior with a learned approximation. A GAN keeps the prior and the
transformation and discards the likelihood. A diffusion model runs the noise step many times. The
differences are large; the shape is the same.

The probabilistic reframing of PCA also settles something Chapter 2 left implicit. PCA was derived there
by minimising squared reconstruction error, which looked like a choice; here it is revealed as the
maximum-likelihood solution under isotropic Gaussian noise. Squared error was never arbitrary — it was a
noise model, exactly as Section 5.5 argued for supervised losses.

Factor analysis versus probabilistic PCA is a good miniature of a general trade. A diagonal covariance
with distinct entries lets the model say "this measurement is noisier than that one", which is often true
and costs $n$ extra parameters. Tying them all to a single $\sigma^2$ is a stronger assumption that buys
a cleaner solution. Almost every modelling decision in this part of the book has that shape.
