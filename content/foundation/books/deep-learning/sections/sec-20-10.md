# 20.10 Directed Generative Nets

The longest section of the chapter and the one containing everything that survived. It covers the directed
alternative to Boltzmann machines: models that generate by pushing noise through a network.

## Differentiable generator networks

The unifying idea. A **generator network** transforms samples from a simple distribution — usually a
standard Gaussian — into samples resembling the data:

$$
x = g(z; \theta), \qquad z \sim p(z)
$$

The network defines a distribution over $x$ implicitly, by transformation. When $g$ is invertible the
density can be computed with the change-of-variables formula from 3.12; more usually it cannot, and the
model is defined only by the samples it produces.

Sampling is trivial — one draw and one forward pass. There is no partition function anywhere, because the
model is normalised by construction. That single fact is what distinguishes this half of the chapter from
the first half.

## Variational autoencoders

The VAE pairs a generator network with the variational machinery of Chapter 19. An encoder produces the
parameters of an approximate posterior $q(z \mid x)$, a decoder produces $p(x \mid z)$, and the model is
trained by maximising the evidence lower bound of 19.1 — reconstruction accuracy plus an entropy term,
equivalently reconstruction minus a KL divergence to the prior. The reparameterisation trick of 20.9 is
what lets the gradient pass through the sampling step, so the whole thing trains by back-propagation.

The book identifies the characteristic weakness: samples tend to be **blurry**. The explanation given is
that the model tends to use only a subset of the latent dimensions, and that maximum likelihood with a
Gaussian decoder produces a conditional mean, which for a multimodal distribution is an average of
plausible outputs rather than any one of them.

## Generative adversarial networks

A generator competes with a **discriminator** trained to distinguish real data from generated samples. The
generator's objective is to fool the discriminator, giving the min-max game of Section 4.4's Lagrangian
structure. At equilibrium the generator's distribution matches the data and the discriminator is at chance.

The book credits GANs with producing **sharper** samples than VAEs — because no likelihood term forces the
model to cover the whole data distribution — and names the costs: training is unstable, convergence is not
guaranteed since the game may cycle rather than settle, and the model may suffer **mode collapse**,
generating only a few of the data's modes.

## The rest

**Sigmoid belief networks** are the directed binary predecessor. **Generative moment matching networks**
train a generator to match the moments of the data distribution rather than to fool a discriminator.
**Convolutional generative networks** use transposed convolutions to upsample, per 9.5. **Autoregressive
networks** — including NADE — decompose the joint by the chain rule and model each conditional with a
network, requiring no latent variables at all.

## My take

Everything in current use is in this section, and the ordering the book gives is almost the inverse of
their eventual importance.

**Autoregressive networks** get the briefest treatment and won the most. Exact likelihood, stable training,
no partition function, no adversary, no posterior — the chain rule of 3.6 and a good conditional model.
Every large language model is this.

**VAEs** persist not as generators but as **compressors**. The blurriness the book identifies made them
uncompetitive for direct image generation, and the encoder-decoder pair is now the standard way to map
images into a smaller latent space where a diffusion model does the generating. The weakness was fatal for
the intended use and irrelevant for the one that mattered.

**GANs** dominated image generation for about six years and were then displaced by diffusion, for exactly
the reasons the book names. Mode collapse and unstable training never got fully solved; diffusion has
neither problem because it has no adversary and a stable regression objective.

The sharp-versus-blurry contrast is worth understanding as a statement about objectives rather than
architectures. Maximum likelihood is mode-covering, per 3.13, so it hedges and produces averages.
Adversarial training has no such term, so it produces sharp samples and no pressure to cover everything —
which is mode collapse described from the other side. Diffusion is the resolution: it is trained by
maximum likelihood in effect, and it is sharp anyway, because generating iteratively means each step
predicts a *small* correction whose conditional distribution is nearly unimodal. The blur problem was
never inherent to likelihood; it was inherent to producing an entire image in one shot.
