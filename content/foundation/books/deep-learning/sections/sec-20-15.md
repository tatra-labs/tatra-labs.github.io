# 20.15 Conclusion

The book ends in a single short paragraph, and the modesty is deliberate. Training generative models with
hidden units is described as one way to make machine learning algorithms **understand the world** — the
representation of the data being valuable for many tasks beyond generation itself. The authors state that
research on generative models is in an early stage of development, that many of the techniques described
work poorly or not at all on realistic problems, and they invite the reader to improve on them.

## What the ending is actually saying

Two claims are being made, and they are worth separating.

The first is a bet: that **learning to generate is learning to understand**. A model that can produce data
must have captured the structure that generates it, so the representation acquired along the way should
transfer. This is the thesis of Chapters 13 through 15 restated as a closing argument, and the book leaves
it as a hope rather than a demonstration.

The second is an admission. Almost nothing in Chapter 20 worked well enough to deploy. Boltzmann machines
could not be sampled from reliably, deep belief networks were trained by a procedure that did not optimise
their objective, VAEs were blurry, GANs were unstable. The authors say so.

## My take

Both claims turned out to be right, and the way they were right is the most interesting thing about
reading this chapter a decade later.

The bet was won decisively and by a route the book barely mentions. **Autoregressive generative models —
next-token prediction — became the dominant paradigm in machine learning**, and the representation they
acquire in learning to generate is exactly the transferable one this paragraph hopes for. A language model
is trained to produce data and turns out to be able to classify, translate, summarise and reason, because
the structure needed to predict the next token includes most of the structure needed for everything else.
The claim in this paragraph is now the organising assumption of the entire field.

The admission was also accurate, and the models the chapter devotes the most space to are the ones that
did not survive. Fourteen sections on Boltzmann machines and their variants; one paragraph in 20.13 on
inverting a corruption process. The proportions are exactly inverted relative to what mattered.

That is not a criticism, and it is worth being clear why. The book reported the state of the field
accurately and said which parts were weak. Its errors of emphasis are errors that anyone writing in 2016
would have made, and it flagged its own uncertainty in the right places — the honesty of Section 15.1
about pretraining, of 18.7 about evaluation, of 20.14 about metrics. A textbook that tells you what it does
not know is more useful than one that does not.

**What to take from the book as a whole.** The mathematics of Part I has not aged at all, and it is the
part most readers skip. Part II is the working knowledge, still largely correct, with the transformer as
its one large omission. Part III is a historical record of a research programme that mostly failed, and it
is worth reading anyway, because the ideas that succeeded are scattered through it — score matching,
denoising, amortised inference, the reparameterisation trick, the positive and negative phases — attached
to models nobody uses.

The through-line of all twenty chapters, if there is one, is the argument from Section 5.11: the way to
learn from limited data is to build in the right assumptions, and depth plus distributed representations is
an assumption that matches the structure of the world unusually well. Everything else in the book is
mechanism. That claim has survived every architecture the field has since discarded, and it is the reason
this book is still worth reading nearly a decade after it stopped being current.
