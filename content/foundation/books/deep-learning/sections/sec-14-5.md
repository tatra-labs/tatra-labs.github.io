# 14.5 Denoising Autoencoders

The most consequential idea in Chapter 14, and the one whose descendants dominate generative modelling
today.

## The construction

A denoising autoencoder receives a **corrupted** input and is trained to reconstruct the original. A
corruption process $C(\tilde x \mid x)$ defines the distribution of corrupted versions given a clean
example, and training minimises the negative log-likelihood of the clean $x$ under the decoder applied to
the encoded corruption.

The procedure is: sample a training example $x$, sample $\tilde x$ from $C(\tilde x \mid x)$, and take a
gradient step on $-\log p_{\text{decoder}}(x \mid h)$ where $h = f(\tilde x)$.

The book makes the important point that this is just stochastic gradient descent on the negative
log-likelihood of a well-defined objective — the expected reconstruction under the corruption
distribution — and so it inherits all the ordinary guarantees, provided the model has enough capacity and
the examples are i.i.d.

Copying is now impossible. The identity function does not remove noise, so the model must learn something
about the structure of the data.

## The vector field

The geometric reading is what makes the section memorable. Corruption pushes a data point off the manifold
into the surrounding empty space; reconstruction pulls it back. A trained denoising autoencoder therefore
implicitly defines a **vector field** $g(f(x)) - x$ pointing from any point toward the data manifold.

The book connects this to **score matching**. The learned vector field approximates the score — the
gradient of the log-density — up to a constant depending on the noise level, so a denoising autoencoder is
estimating the derivative of the data distribution's log-density without ever computing the density
itself. For a Gaussian corruption this correspondence is exact in the small-noise limit.

That is a striking result. A model trained only to remove noise has learned the direction in which
probability density increases, everywhere in the space.

## My take

This section describes, in 2016, the foundation of diffusion models — and does not know it.

A diffusion model is a denoising autoencoder applied at **many noise levels simultaneously**, with a
single network conditioned on the level. Training is exactly the procedure above: corrupt, denoise,
gradient step. Sampling starts from pure noise and follows the learned vector field back toward the data
manifold, in many small steps rather than one. The score-matching connection stated here is the theory
that makes that sampling procedure correct: following the score is Langevin dynamics, and it converges to
the data distribution.

Everything needed is present — the corruption process, the reconstruction objective, the vector field, the
score interpretation. What is missing is the idea of training across a continuum of noise levels rather
than one, and using the resulting score estimate iteratively rather than in a single shot. That step took
another five years.

The general principle is worth extracting, because it explains more than diffusion. **Destroy information
and train a model to restore it.** Masked language modelling corrupts by deleting tokens. Diffusion
corrupts by adding Gaussian noise. Contrastive learning corrupts by augmentation. In each case the
corruption defines what the model must understand in order to undo it, and the choice of corruption is the
real design decision — which is the same point Section 7.4 made about augmentation, arrived at from the
other side.
