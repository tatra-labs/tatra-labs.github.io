# 20.9 Back-Propagation through Random Operations

A short, pivotal section. Everything before it in this chapter fights the partition function; this section
provides the tool that lets the rest of the chapter stop fighting it, by making sampling differentiable.

## The problem

A generative model contains a sampling step: draw $z$ from some distribution whose parameters the model
produces. Sampling is not a differentiable operation — the output is a random draw, not a function of the
parameters — so a gradient cannot flow back through it, and the parameters upstream cannot be trained.

## The reparameterisation trick

For continuous variables there is a clean solution. Rewrite the sample as a **deterministic function of
the parameters and an independent noise source**. For a Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation
$\sigma$:

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

The randomness now lives in $\epsilon$, which does not depend on any parameter, and $z$ is an ordinary
differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$. Back-propagation passes through it exactly as through any
other operation. The book describes this as extending back-propagation to stochastic computational graphs,
and notes it applies whenever the distribution can be expressed as a transformation of parameterless noise.

## Discrete variables

The trick does not work for discrete variables, since there is no continuous path from a parameter to a
discrete sample. The book gives **REINFORCE** as the alternative — an unbiased gradient estimator built
from the score-function identity, which requires only the ability to evaluate the log-probability of a
sample and the reward it obtains.

REINFORCE's problem is variance, which the book describes as very high. The standard remedy is a
**baseline**: subtract a value that does not depend on the sampled action, which leaves the estimator
unbiased and can reduce its variance substantially.

## My take

The reparameterisation trick is the most consequential single technique in Chapter 20 and it is presented
here as a piece of supporting machinery. It is what makes the variational autoencoder trainable, and by
extension it is what turned variational inference from a per-model derivation into a general procedure —
the gap Section 19.4 leaves open is filled here.

The contrast between the two estimators is worth internalising, because it explains a persistent asymmetry
in the field. Reparameterisation gives a **low-variance** gradient and requires **continuous** latent
variables. REINFORCE works for anything and gives a high-variance gradient. That is why continuous latents
dominate generative modelling and why discrete-latent models — VQ-VAE, hard attention, discrete
bottlenecks — need special machinery such as straight-through estimators or Gumbel-Softmax relaxations,
neither of which is here.

It is also why reinforcement learning is harder than supervised learning in a specific, quantifiable sense.
Actions are discrete or the environment is not differentiable, so the only available estimator is the
high-variance one, and everything from baselines to advantage estimation to PPO's clipping is variance
reduction on top of the estimator described in this section.

The general principle is worth stating: **move the randomness off the gradient path**. Where that is
possible, learning is easy; where it is not, expect to spend the difference on variance reduction.
