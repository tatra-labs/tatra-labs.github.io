# 17.5 The Challenge of Mixing between Separated Modes

The chapter's closing section, and the one that explains why Monte Carlo training of undirected models
never became reliable.

## The problem

An MCMC chain should produce independent samples from the target distribution. In practice successive
samples are highly correlated, and when the correlation persists the chain is said to **mix poorly**.

The mechanism is a random walk. Gibbs sampling changes one variable — or one layer — at a time, so the
chain wanders through state space in small steps. When probability mass is concentrated in several modes
separated by regions of low probability, moving from one mode to another requires passing through the
low-probability region, and each step of that journey is individually unlikely. The chain therefore stays
where it started, and a run of any realistic length explores a single mode.

The book gives a concrete case that makes the difficulty vivid. For a distribution over images with
distinct classes, a chain started on one class does not travel to another, because the intermediate states
are not valid images and have negligible probability. The samples are from the model *restricted to one
mode*, and there is no indication in the output that anything is wrong.

The problem is worse for models with latent variables and worse still for deep models, where the modes
correspond to configurations of many latent units.

## Tempering

The main remedy discussed is **tempering**. Introduce a temperature parameter $\beta$ and sample from a
distribution proportional to $\exp(-\beta E(x))$. At $\beta = 1$ this is the model; at lower $\beta$ the
distribution is flatter, the barriers between modes are lower, and the chain moves freely.

**Parallel tempering** runs several chains at different temperatures simultaneously and periodically
proposes swapping states between neighbouring temperatures, so a chain that explored widely at high
temperature can hand a distant state to a chain at $\beta = 1$.

The book reports that tempering helps and that it has not been enough to make sampling from these models
reliable on realistic problems.

The section also raises a more speculative idea: that a representation in which the modes are closer
together — a deep representation, where classes may occupy adjacent regions of latent space — might mix
better than one in the raw data space, because the barriers between modes are lower there.

## My take

This section is the honest ending to the chapter, and it explains the fate of an entire model family. The
gradient of an undirected model's log-likelihood requires an expectation under the model, per Section
18.1, so every training step needs samples, and if the sampler cannot leave one mode the gradient is
systematically wrong. Not noisy — wrong, in a way that more compute does not fix. Boltzmann machines and
deep belief networks were limited by this, and Chapter 18's methods are all attempts to work around it.

The deep-representation speculation at the end is the most interesting item, and it turned out to be
right in a way the book did not foresee. A latent diffusion model generates in a learned latent space
precisely because that space is better behaved than pixel space, and interpolating between two points in
it produces plausible intermediate images — which is to say the modes are connected there, exactly as this
paragraph suggests they might be.

Diffusion also resolved the mixing problem itself, by a route that sidesteps rather than solves it. Its
sampling chain does not need to travel between modes, because it starts from pure noise, where all modes
are equally reachable, and descends into one. There is no random walk between modes because there is never
a need to leave one. That is why an iterative sampler works for diffusion and did not work here.
