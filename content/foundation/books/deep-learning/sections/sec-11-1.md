# 11.1 Performance Metrics

Chapter 11 is about process rather than models, and it starts where a project should: deciding what
number you are trying to move.

## Setting a target

The first task is determining what error rate is **desired**, and the book insists this be an absolute
target rather than "as good as possible". The target comes from context — the error rate achievable by a
human, the rate at which the system becomes commercially viable, the rate at which it becomes safe — and
having one is what makes it possible to know when to stop.

The authors are direct that in an academic setting the target is usually a previously published number,
and in a real deployment it is a requirement.

## When accuracy is the wrong measure

For rare events, accuracy is uninformative. The book's example is a disease occurring in one person in a
million: a classifier that always answers "no" achieves 99.9999% accuracy and is worthless.

The alternative is the **precision/recall** pair. Precision is the fraction of reported detections that
are correct; recall is the fraction of true events that were detected. A detector that reports nothing has
undefined precision and zero recall; one that reports everything has perfect recall and precision equal
to the base rate.

Because a system can be tuned to trade one against the other, the pair is summarised either by the
**PR curve** — precision plotted against recall as the decision threshold varies — or by the **F-score**,
their harmonic mean:

$$
F = \frac{2pr}{p + r}
$$

## Coverage

The third metric the book introduces is the one most often forgotten. If a system is allowed to **decline
to answer** — deferring to a human — then its accuracy on the cases it does answer is only half the
picture. **Coverage** is the fraction of examples on which the system produces a response, and it trades
directly against accuracy: a model that answers only when confident can be made arbitrarily accurate by
answering almost nothing.

The right way to report such a system is accuracy at a stated coverage, and the book's Street View
example in 11.6 is exactly this.

## My take

The instruction to fix a target error rate before starting is the single most useful piece of advice in
Chapter 11, and it is routinely skipped. Without a target, a project has no stopping condition and no way
to decide whether the next improvement is worth its cost — which is how a team spends three months moving
a number that was already good enough.

The coverage discussion has become far more relevant than it was in 2016, and it is worth reading with
current systems in mind. Any deployment with a human in the loop, an abstention option, or a routing
decision between a small model and a large one is making a coverage trade, and reporting a single accuracy
figure for it is meaningless. A model that says "I don't know" appropriately is more useful than one with
higher raw accuracy that never does, and this section gives the vocabulary for saying so precisely.

Worth pairing with the standard-error point from 5.4. A metric is only as trustworthy as the size of the
set it was measured on, and the difference between two systems is frequently smaller than the noise in
the measurement.
