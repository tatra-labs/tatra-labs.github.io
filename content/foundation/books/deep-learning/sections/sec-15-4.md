# 15.4 Distributed Representation

The idea the book calls one of the most important tools in deep learning, and the one that separates it
most cleanly from everything that came before.

## The distinction

A **distributed representation** describes each input by many features, each of which can be active
independently. A **symbolic** or one-hot representation uses one active unit per concept.

The counting argument is the whole point. With $n$ binary features there are $2^n$ distinct
configurations, so a distributed representation over $n$ units can distinguish exponentially many
concepts. A one-hot representation over $n$ units distinguishes exactly $n$. The book's illustration is a
model describing objects by colour, shape and size separately: with $k$ values for each, $k^3$
combinations are describable, while a one-hot scheme needs $k^3$ units.

## The generalisation argument

The consequence that matters is not the counting but what it implies for generalisation. Because features
are shared across concepts, a distributed representation can describe a configuration **it has never
seen**. A model that has learned "red" from red cars and "truck" from blue trucks can represent a red
truck without having seen one, because the features are independent.

A one-hot representation cannot: every concept is a separate unit trained from its own examples, and
nothing is shared. This is the objection to k-means from Section 5.8, stated in general form.

The book supports this with a capacity result for linear threshold units, showing that the number of
regions a distributed representation can carve out of input space grows much faster than the number of
parameters — so the model's ability to distinguish configurations outpaces the data needed to fit it.

## My take

This is the argument to give someone who asks what deep learning actually contributes, because it is the
one that does not depend on any particular architecture. The exponential gap between $n$ and $2^n$ is not
a heuristic or an empirical finding; it is the reason a model with a fixed number of parameters can
distinguish more situations than it has training examples.

Word embeddings are the cleanest demonstration and were the book's contemporary example. A vocabulary of
50,000 words as one-hot vectors shares nothing between them; as 300-dimensional embeddings, every
dimension is shared by every word, and what is learned about one word constrains the estimate for
thousands of others. The whole advantage of neural language models over n-grams in Section 12.4 is this
single property.

The idea has since been sharpened in a way the book does not anticipate, and it is worth knowing about.
Real networks appear to use **superposition** — representing far more features than they have dimensions,
by placing them along non-orthogonal directions and tolerating a small amount of interference. That is a
stronger claim than the one here, since it means the features are not axis-aligned and cannot be read off
by inspecting individual units. It explains why individual neurons in a large model are usually
**polysemantic**, responding to several unrelated things, and it is why interpretability work needs sparse
dictionaries to recover the features rather than simply reading the coordinates.

So the counting argument in this section is correct and understates the case. Networks get more than
$2^n$ worth of use out of $n$ dimensions, and they pay for it in interpretability.
