# 19.2 Expectation Maximization

The classical algorithm for learning with latent variables, presented here as an instance of the
optimisation framework in 19.1.

## The algorithm

EM alternates two steps until convergence:

**The E-step.** Holding the parameters $\theta$ fixed, set $q(h \mid v) = p(h \mid v; \theta)$ for every
training example — the exact posterior under the current parameters. By 19.1 this makes the bound tight,
since the KL term becomes zero.

**The M-step.** Holding $q$ fixed, maximise the bound with respect to $\theta$, using any optimisation
algorithm. Because $q$ is fixed, the entropy term is constant and only the expected joint log-probability
matters.

The book emphasises a point that is easy to miss: EM is **not** an approximate inference algorithm. It is
a learning algorithm that uses exact inference in its E-step, and it applies only where that exact
posterior is available.

## Two observations

The book highlights two features of the procedure. The M-step can make **large** moves in parameter space
using a $q$ computed once, rather than recomputing the posterior after every small update — which is a
substantial efficiency gain when inference is expensive. And the value of $q$ becomes stale as $\theta$
moves, so the bound loosens during the M-step and is retightened at the next E-step.

The section also notes that the "expectation" and "maximisation" names are a poor fit for the general
case: the E-step does not always compute an expectation in closed form, and the M-step does not always
maximise.

## My take

EM is worth understanding for two reasons that have nothing to do with using it.

The first is that it makes the structure of the variational framework concrete. EM is the special case
where the posterior is available exactly, and everything in 19.3 through 19.5 is what happens when it is
not. MAP inference restricts $q$ to a point mass, mean-field restricts it to a factorised family, and
learned inference replaces the E-step with a network. Seeing EM first makes those look like relaxations of
one algorithm rather than three unrelated methods.

The second is the large-M-step observation, which is the part of this section with modern relevance. The
principle — do expensive inference once, then take many cheap parameter steps against its result — is
exactly the structure of policy iteration in reinforcement learning and of the sampling-then-training
loops in RLHF, where a batch of expensive generations is reused for multiple gradient updates. The same
staleness trade-off applies: reuse for too long and the fixed distribution no longer matches the model, at
which point the updates degrade.

EM itself is not used for deep models, because the E-step requires the exact posterior and there is no
interesting deep model where it is available. Gaussian mixtures and hidden Markov models are where it
still lives, and those are the models simple enough for the E-step to be a closed-form computation.
