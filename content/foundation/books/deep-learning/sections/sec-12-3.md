# 12.3 Speech Recognition

A short section, and the most concrete before-and-after story in the book: the domain where deep learning
displaced an entrenched, highly-refined pipeline first.

## The task and the old pipeline

Automatic speech recognition maps an acoustic sequence to the sequence of words that produced it. For
roughly thirty years the dominant system was a **GMM-HMM**: a hidden Markov model over phoneme sequences,
with Gaussian mixture models relating acoustic features to the HMM states. The input was typically
preprocessed into spectral features such as MFCCs.

The book is explicit that these systems were the state of the art until about 2009, and that they were
very good — the result of decades of refinement.

## What changed

Neural networks entered first as a **replacement for the GMM component**, keeping the HMM structure: the
network estimates the probability of an HMM state given the acoustics. The book dates the shift to
unsupervised pretraining of deep networks around 2009, and reports the result on the TIMIT phoneme
recognition benchmark — the error rate falling from about 26% to 20.7%, a drop large enough to reorient
the field.

Improvements followed quickly: larger and deeper models, distributed training, and the observation that
the pretraining step could be dropped once enough labelled data was available. Convolutional networks
applied across time and frequency helped, and the book notes the move away from hand-designed features
toward raw spectrograms and eventually raw waveforms.

The end state described is **end-to-end** recognition, dropping the HMM entirely, with connectionist
temporal classification and deep LSTMs mapping acoustics directly to character or phoneme sequences.

## My take

The value of this section is as a case study in how a mature engineering field gets replaced, and the
sequence is worth memorising because it has recurred in translation, vision and beyond.

The neural component does **not** arrive as a whole system. It replaces one module inside an existing
pipeline, competing on that module's own benchmark, where the comparison is unambiguous. Once it wins
there, the surrounding structure — chosen to compensate for the old module's weaknesses — starts to look
like overhead, and it is removed piece by piece. The HMM survived the first wave and did not survive the
second. The hand-designed features survived longer than the acoustic model and went the same way.

That pattern predicts where a field is in its transition better than any accuracy number does. A hybrid
system with a neural component wedged into a classical pipeline is a midpoint, not an endpoint.

What has happened since is a further step the book has no framework for: speech recognition became a
by-product of general sequence modelling rather than a discipline of its own. Whisper is an
encoder-decoder transformer trained on weakly-labelled audio scraped at scale, and it does transcription,
translation and language identification with no phonemes, no lexicon and no acoustic model anywhere in
it. The domain-specific structure that this section describes being dismantled was dismantled completely.
