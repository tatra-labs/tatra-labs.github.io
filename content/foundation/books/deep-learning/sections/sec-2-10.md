# 2.10 The Trace Operator

Two pages on a notational convenience. The trace exists in this chapter because it makes certain
expressions writable without summation signs, and because it has one property that gets used constantly.

## Definition and properties

The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries:

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(A) = \sum_i A_{i,i}
$$

Its immediate payoff is the Frobenius norm, which otherwise needs a double sum:

$$
\lVert A \rVert_F = \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(A A^{\top})}
$$

The trace is invariant to transpose, $\operatorname{Tr}(A) = \operatorname{Tr}(A^{\top})$, and linear.
The property that matters is **cyclic invariance**: the trace of a product is unchanged by rotating the
factors,

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{Tr}(CAB) = \operatorname{Tr}(BCA)
$$

This holds even when the shapes differ, so long as each product is defined — which means the intermediate
matrices in the two expressions may have completely different dimensions while the traces agree. A
scalar is its own trace, $a = \operatorname{Tr}(a)$, which lets a scalar expression be manipulated with
trace identities and then collapsed back.

## My take

Cyclic invariance is the reason this section exists, and its real use is deriving gradients. Any scalar
loss can be written as a trace, the trace can be rotated to put the parameter of interest on the outside,
and the derivative then reads off directly. Most compact derivations of the least-squares normal
equations, of the PCA objective in 2.12, and of matrix-calculus identities generally, are cyclic
invariance applied two or three times.

For a reader who only intends to *use* deep learning, this is the most skippable section in Chapter 2 —
autodiff has made hand-derived matrix gradients an unusual skill. For a reader who intends to read
papers, it is not skippable at all, because the derivations in appendices are written in exactly this
style and are unreadable without it.
