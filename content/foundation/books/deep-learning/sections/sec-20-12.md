# 20.12 Generative Stochastic Networks

A generalisation of the previous section, and one of the more interesting failed ideas in the book:
give up on modelling the distribution and model the sampler instead.

## The idea

A generative stochastic network does not parameterise $p(x)$. It parameterises the **transition operator**
of a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the data distribution — the model is a procedure for
taking one step, and the distribution is whatever that procedure converges to.

The formulation includes latent variables $h$, and the chain alternates between sampling
$h$ given the current $x$ and sampling a new $x$ given $h$. Training uses the same reconstruction
objective as a denoising autoencoder: maximise the log-probability of the true $x$ under the transition
starting from a corrupted version of it.

The book notes that a denoising autoencoder is a special case with no latent variables, and that the
addition of latents is what makes the family more general.

The theoretical justification is the same as 20.11's: if each transition is learned correctly, the chain's
stationary distribution is correct, so a good one-step model gives a good generative model without ever
writing down a density.

## My take

The reframing is genuinely elegant and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the interesting part.
A distribution is a hard object to represent — it needs normalising, and normalising is what Chapter 18
was about. A **sampler** is an easy object to represent: it is just a function from a state and some noise
to a new state. So parameterise the sampler and let the distribution be implicit.

The idea failed for the reason it was always going to. Defining a distribution as the fixed point of a
chain means every use of the model requires running the chain to convergence, which is Chapter 17's
problem in full — unknown mixing time, no convergence test, and slow mixing between modes. A model whose
every query costs an unbounded number of steps is not a practical model, however clean its definition.

What is worth noticing is that the underlying insight was correct and was vindicated by a design that
avoids the fixed point. Diffusion also parameterises a transition operator rather than a density; the
difference is that its chain has a **fixed length and a fixed schedule**, so it terminates, and it is
trained to invert a known corruption process rather than to have the right stationary distribution. Both
are "model the sampler, not the distribution". One requires convergence and one does not, and that is the
entire difference between an abandoned idea and the dominant one.

Reading 20.11 and 20.12 together is the most useful thing to do with the end of this chapter. They contain
the right idea twice, both times attached to the requirement that made it useless.
