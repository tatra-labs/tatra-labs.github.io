# 18.4 Score Matching and Ratio Matching

The second family of methods that avoid the partition function, and the one whose descendants matter most
today.

## Score matching

The **score** of a density is the gradient of its log with respect to the data:

$$
\nabla_x \log p(x)
$$

The key observation is that $Z$ depends on the parameters and not on $x$, so $\nabla_x \log Z = 0$ and
the score of $p$ equals the score of the unnormalised $\tilde p$. **The partition function disappears
under differentiation with respect to $x$.**

Score matching minimises the expected squared difference between the model's score and the data
distribution's score:

$$
L = \frac{1}{2} \mathbb E_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}} \left\lVert \nabla_x \log p_{\text{model}}(x) - \nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x) \right\rVert_2^2
$$

The data score is unknown, which appears to be fatal — but integration by parts converts the objective
into one requiring only the model's first and second derivatives with respect to $x$, evaluated at the
data points. No samples from the model, no $Z$, no MCMC.

The book records the limitations. Score matching requires **continuous** data, since it differentiates
with respect to $x$. It requires the model's log-density and its derivatives to be tractable. And it does
not work with models that provide only a lower bound on the log-density, so it cannot be combined with
variational methods.

## Ratio matching

For **binary** data, where derivatives with respect to $x$ do not exist, ratio matching applies the same
strategy to a ratio: compare the model's probability of a configuration with that of the configuration
with one bit flipped. The partition function cancels in the ratio, as it did in pseudolikelihood. The book
reports it works better than pseudolikelihood for training models on binary image data.

## My take

Score matching is the most important idea in Chapter 18 by a wide margin, and the book gives no indication
of that — it appears as one option among six for training Boltzmann machines.

The reason it matters is stated in the first line above and is worth restating: **the score does not
depend on the normalising constant**. A model that learns $\nabla_x \log p(x)$ has learned everything
needed to sample by following the gradient uphill, via Langevin dynamics, without ever knowing $p$ itself.
That is the foundation of score-based generative modelling, and diffusion models are its practical form.

Two obstacles stood between this section and that outcome, and both were removed after publication. The
second-derivative requirement makes the objective expensive for a neural network — solved by **denoising**
score matching in 18.5, which removes it entirely. And the estimate is poor in low-density regions, which
is exactly where a sampler starting from noise begins — solved by training across **multiple noise
levels** so that there is signal everywhere.

Read 18.4 and 18.5 together and the whole of modern generative modelling is one step away. The book has
the objective, the reason it avoids $Z$, and the denoising connection. What it lacks is the idea of
conditioning one network on a continuum of noise scales, and the recognition that this method was worth
pursuing for its own sake rather than as a way to train a Boltzmann machine.
