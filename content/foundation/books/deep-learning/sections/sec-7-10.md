# 7.10 Sparse Representations

Weight decay penalises the parameters. This section penalises the **activations** instead, and the
distinction is the whole content.

## Representational sparsity

Section 7.1 produced sparse *parameters* with an L1 penalty. Here the same idea is applied one level
along: penalise the representation $h$ so that most of its entries are zero for any given input.

The regularised objective takes the familiar shape, with the penalty applied to the hidden units rather
than the weights:

$$
\tilde J(\theta; X, y) = J(\theta; X, y) + \alpha \Omega(h)
$$

With $\Omega(h) = \lVert h \rVert_1$ this induces the same thresholding behaviour as before, now on the
activations. Other choices are possible — the book mentions penalties derived from a Student-$t$ prior,
and KL-divergence penalties that push the average activation of each unit toward a small target value.

The book contrasts the two kinds of sparsity directly. Sparse parameters mean many weights are zero, so
many connections are absent. Sparse representations mean many *activations* are zero, so a given input
excites only a few units, and different inputs excite different ones.

**Orthogonal matching pursuit** is given as an alternative route: constrain the representation to have at
most $k$ nonzero entries, and solve for the best such representation directly rather than encouraging
sparsity with a penalty. The book notes that this hard-constraint approach can be very effective when the
representation is used as a feature extractor.

## My take

The most important thing about this section is easy to overlook: **the rectifier already does this**. A
ReLU network produces representations in which a substantial fraction of units are exactly zero for any
given input, with no penalty required, because the activation function has a hard zero region. Section
6.3 introduces the unit and does not connect it to sparsity; this section describes the property and does
not connect it to the unit. Read together, the point is that the default hidden unit of modern deep
learning is a sparsity-inducing one by construction.

Explicit activation penalties have largely disappeared as a result. They persist in sparse autoencoders,
which Chapter 14 covers, and they have had a genuine second life in **mechanistic interpretability** —
sparse autoencoders trained on the activations of a large language model, in the hope that a sparse
overcomplete basis recovers human-interpretable features. That application depends on exactly the
argument for representational sparsity given here: an input that activates few units activates
*meaningful* ones, and the axes acquire individual interpretations they do not otherwise have.

The hard-constraint route is also worth noting for what it foreshadows. Fixing the number of active units
in advance, rather than penalising and hoping, is the same design decision as top-$k$ routing in a sparse
mixture of experts — where sparsity is imposed for computational rather than statistical reasons, and
where it is the whole point of the architecture.
