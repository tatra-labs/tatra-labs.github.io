# 14.8 Predictive Sparse Decomposition

A hybrid of sparse coding and the parametric autoencoder, designed to fix the specific defect identified
in Section 13.4: that sparse coding has no encoder.

## The construction

The model has a parametric encoder $f(x)$, a decoder $g(h)$, and a training objective minimising three
terms at once:

$$
\lVert x - g(h) \rVert_2^2 + \lambda \lvert h \rvert_1 + \gamma \lVert h - f(x) \rVert_2^2
$$

Reconstruction error, an $L^1$ sparsity penalty on the code, and a term forcing the encoder's output to
**agree with the code**.

Training alternates in the manner of sparse coding: minimise over $h$ for each example, then take a
gradient step on the parameters of $f$ and $g$. The optimisation over $h$ is fast, because the encoder
supplies a good initial value — it is being trained to predict the answer.

After training, computing features is a single forward pass through $f$. The expensive inner optimisation
is used during training and discarded at deployment.

The book notes that PSD is an example of **learned approximate inference**, connecting it to Section 19.5,
and that after training the encoder alone is used, which makes the model cheap to apply.

## My take

This is the clearest early example of **amortised inference** in the book, and it is worth naming as such
because the pattern is everywhere in modern machine learning.

The structure is: an expensive procedure computes a good answer; a network is trained to predict that
answer directly; at test time the network replaces the procedure. The cost of inference is paid once
during training and amortised over every subsequent use. PSD does this for sparse coding. The variational
autoencoder does it for posterior inference. Learned optimisers, learned samplers, distillation of
multi-step diffusion samplers into few-step ones, and the whole practice of training a fast model to
imitate a slow one are the same move.

Reading it here, in its simplest form, makes the later versions much easier to recognise: the third term
in that objective is the entire idea.

PSD itself did not survive. It was superseded within a few years by the variational autoencoder, which
achieves the same amortisation with a cleaner justification — the encoder is not merely trained to imitate
an optimisation, it is derived as the approximate posterior of a proper probabilistic model, and the whole
objective is a bound on the likelihood rather than a sum of three heuristically weighted terms. The two
extra hyperparameters $\lambda$ and $\gamma$ in the expression above are the visible symptom of the
missing derivation.

Worth noting the sequencing, though. Amortisation arrived before the probabilistic framework that
justified it, as an engineering fix for something too slow. That is a fairly common order of events, and
this section is a good record of it.
