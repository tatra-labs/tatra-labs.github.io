# 15.1 Greedy Layer-Wise Unsupervised Pretraining

The technique that started the third wave, assessed by the authors at the moment it stopped being useful.
The section is unusually candid and is worth reading for the honesty as much as the content.

## The procedure

**Greedy** — each layer is optimised in isolation. **Layer-wise** — layers are trained one at a time, in
order, each on the output of the one below. **Unsupervised** — each layer is trained with an unsupervised
objective, typically as a single-layer autoencoder or an RBM. **Pretraining** — the result is only a
starting point, followed by joint supervised fine-tuning of the whole network.

Historically this is what made deep networks trainable in 2006, and the book credits it with the
observation that deep architectures could be optimised at all — a belief that had not previously been
supported by evidence.

## Why it might work

Two explanations are offered, and the book treats both as partial.

**An optimisation effect.** The initialisation places the parameters in a region from which supervised
training proceeds more easily, which matters when the optimiser is weak.

**A regularisation effect.** The initial parameters encode information about the input distribution that
supervised training alone would not discover, so the model is biased toward functions consistent with the
structure of $p(x)$. This is the stronger hypothesis, and the argument for it is that pretraining helps
most when labelled data is scarce and unlabelled data is plentiful.

## Why it stopped being used

The book's assessment is direct. Unsupervised pretraining has largely been abandoned except in natural
language processing, where word embeddings remain useful. For image classification with large labelled
datasets it does not help, and may hurt. The authors attribute this to improvements elsewhere — better
initialisation, rectifiers, batch normalisation, and better optimisers — which removed the optimisation
problem pretraining was compensating for.

They also make a methodological criticism of the technique itself: it has **two separate phases with
separate hyperparameters**, and the effect of the first cannot be measured until the second is complete.
That makes tuning expensive and makes it hard to know what is responsible for a result. Methods with a
single training phase and an unsupervised term in the objective avoid this.

## My take

This section is the most interesting wrong call in the book, and the interesting part is that the
reasoning was correct.

Everything the authors say about 2016 was true. Pretraining had stopped helping in vision, the
optimisation problem it solved had been solved better elsewhere, and the two-phase structure was a real
methodological cost. What they could not see is that the regularisation hypothesis — their own stronger
explanation — scales, and that the regime where it pays is not "scarce labels, moderate unlabelled data"
but "scarce labels, *enormous* unlabelled data".

The technique returned in a different shape. Not layer-wise, not greedy, and not with an autoencoder
objective: the whole network trained at once on next-token prediction over a corpus large enough that the
pretraining stage is the main event and the supervised stage is an afterthought. The word-embedding
exception the authors carve out for NLP is precisely the thread that led there.

The criticism of two-phase training also aged in an unexpected direction. It is entirely valid, and the
field accepted the cost anyway, because the first phase became so much more valuable than the second that
the inconvenience stopped mattering. Pretrain-then-fine-tune is now the default pipeline in every
modality, with all the hyperparameter difficulties this section identifies, and nobody minds.
