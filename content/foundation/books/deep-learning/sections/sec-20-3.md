# 20.3 Deep Belief Networks

The model that started the third wave in 2006, and a strange hybrid: partly directed, partly undirected,
with a training procedure that has almost nothing to do with its definition.

## The structure

A deep belief network has several layers of latent variables, typically binary, and a visible layer. Its
distinguishing feature is that the connections are **mixed**. The top two layers form an undirected RBM;
every layer below is connected to the one above by **directed** edges pointing downward.

So the model is a directed sigmoid belief network with an RBM sitting on top as its prior. The book notes
that the term "deep belief network" now refers specifically to this hybrid, and that the historical use of
"belief network" for general directed models caused persistent confusion.

## Training and sampling

A DBN is trained **greedily**, layer by layer, exactly as Section 15.1 describes. Train an RBM on the
data; use its hidden activations as data for a second RBM; repeat. The stack of RBMs is then reinterpreted
as a DBN — a reinterpretation the book is careful to note is not exact, since the training procedure does
not optimise the DBN's own likelihood.

Sampling requires both machineries. Run Gibbs sampling in the top-level RBM until it mixes, then sample
downward through the directed layers by ancestral sampling. Inference in the lower layers is intractable
because of the explaining-away effect from 3.7 — the directed edges make the latent variables dependent
given the observations — so the approximate inference used in practice ignores it and treats the network
as though the edges pointed the other way.

After the generative pretraining, the weights are typically used to **initialise a supervised network**,
which is fine-tuned by back-propagation.

## My take

The DBN is best understood as a historical object rather than a model anyone should build, and the reason
is contained in the paragraph above: it is trained by a procedure that does not optimise its objective,
and its inference is an approximation that ignores the structure that makes it a DBN in the first place.
Almost every practical statement about it is a departure from its definition.

Its importance was empirical and it was enormous. It was the first demonstration that a deep architecture
could be trained to do something useful, at a time when the consensus was that deep networks were not
trainable, and it restarted a field that had spent a decade out of favour. The book's own account in
Section 1.2 credits it with the third wave.

Two things are worth extracting. The first is that the mechanism that mattered — layer-wise initialisation
followed by supervised fine-tuning — turned out to be separable from the probabilistic model that
motivated it, and it was the initialisation that did the work. Once better initialisation, rectifiers and
Adam arrived, the scaffolding was removed and nothing was lost.

The second is that this is a good example of a result being right for the wrong reason. The 2006 argument
was about generative pretraining capturing the structure of $p(x)$; the operative effect was that the
optimiser could now reach a good solution. The field spent several years pursuing the generative story
before Section 15.1's assessment settled it.
