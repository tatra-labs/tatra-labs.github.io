# 15.5 Exponential Gains from Depth

A short section collecting the theoretical arguments that depth buys exponential efficiency, most of
which have appeared earlier in the book.

## The results

**Piecewise-linear networks.** A deep rectifier network divides input space into a number of linear
regions that is exponential in the depth. The book gives the mechanism: each layer can fold the space,
and folds compose multiplicatively, so a function with exponentially many pieces is representable with a
number of parameters linear in depth. A shallow network must describe each region separately.

**Sum-product networks and other families.** Results exist showing families of functions requiring
exponentially many units in a shallow network and polynomially many in a deep one, so the advantage is not
an artefact of rectifiers.

**Statistical efficiency, not only representational.** The book emphasises that depth reduces the number of
*examples* needed as well as the number of parameters, because the composition expresses an assumption
about the data — that it was generated by a composition of simpler factors — and a correct assumption
substitutes for data.

The section connects these back to 6.4, and states the caveat with them: the theorems show that some
functions have this property, not that the functions of practical interest do. The justification for
believing that real problems are compositional is empirical and philosophical rather than proven.

## My take

The honest summary is that these results are suggestive and are not the reason anyone uses deep networks.
The reason is that deep networks work, and the theorems provide a story for why that might be. The
distinction matters because the theorems are frequently cited as though they establish something about
practical performance, and they do not — they identify function families with an exponential separation
and say nothing about whether image classification is in one.

Two updates from the years since are worth attaching.

The **linear-region counting** result turned out to overstate the case in a specific way: trained networks
use far fewer distinct regions than the bound allows, and the number in use grows much more slowly than
the theoretical maximum. The capacity is there and is not exercised, which means region-counting is a poor
proxy for what the network is doing.

And the **depth-versus-width** balance moved. The theorems favour depth without limit; practice found
diminishing returns past a moderate depth, and current large models are far wider than they are deep. Part
of that is optimisation and part of it is hardware — width parallelises across devices and depth is
inherently sequential — but the outcome is that the architecture the theory most strongly recommends is
not the one that gets built.

What survives is the compositional argument in its statistical form, which is the least formal item in
the section and the most durable. Deep models work on data produced by processes with hierarchical
structure — pixels to edges to parts to objects, characters to words to phrases to meaning — and they work
less well on tabular data, which has no such structure. That empirical pattern is the real support for
depth, and it does not need a theorem.
