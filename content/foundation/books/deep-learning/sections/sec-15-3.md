# 15.3 Semi-Supervised Disentangling of Causal Factors

The most philosophical section in Chapter 15, and the one that asks the sharpest question: what makes one
representation better than another?

## The answer proposed

The book's candidate is that a good representation has features corresponding to the **underlying causes**
of the observed data, with separate features for separate causes. Ideally the representation
**disentangles** them, so that changing one factor of variation changes one part of the representation.

This gives a criterion that is about the world rather than about the task, which is what makes it
attractive for unsupervised learning — there is no label to define what is useful, so the structure of the
data has to do it.

## When unsupervised learning helps a supervised task

The section then addresses the question Section 7.6 raised. Learning $p(x)$ helps in learning
$p(y \mid x)$ only if the two are connected. The book states the condition: if $y$ is one of the causal
factors of $x$, then a model of $p(x)$ that recovers the causal factors will contain $y$ among them, and
a simple classifier over that representation will succeed.

The counter-case is given too. If the model of $p(x)$ spends its capacity on the dominant sources of
variation — the book's example is a dataset of images where brightness varies enormously and the object
identity is a small effect — then the representation will capture brightness and neglect the thing wanted.

Two responses are discussed: use supervision to indicate which factors matter, and use a much larger
representation, on the grounds that a model with enough capacity will capture the salient factor
eventually even if it is not the largest.

The book also connects the framing to robustness. A model that has learned the causal structure should be
more robust to distribution shift, because the causal mechanism $p(\text{effect} \mid \text{cause})$ is
expected to remain stable when the distribution over causes changes.

## My take

The condition for when unsupervised learning helps is the most useful sentence in the section, and it is
falsifiable: it helps when the label is among the causes of the input, and not otherwise. That predicts
correctly in both directions — it is why pretraining on unlabelled images helps object recognition, since
object identity causes the pixels, and why it does not help predict something merely correlated with the
image, such as which photographer took it.

Disentanglement itself has been the least successful research programme in this chapter. A great deal of
work followed on VAE variants designed to disentangle, and the decisive result, established afterwards, is
negative: **unsupervised disentanglement is not identifiable** without inductive biases or supervision,
for essentially the reason Section 13.2 gives about ICA. Many representations explain the data equally
well and only some are disentangled; nothing in the objective distinguishes them. Large-scale evaluations
found that reported successes depended more on random seed and on unprincipled model selection than on the
method.

What did happen instead is worth noting because it does not fit the framing. Very large models trained
without any disentangling objective develop representations in which many concepts are **linearly
decodable** — recoverable by a linear probe — which is a weaker property than disentanglement and turns
out to be enough for most purposes. The factors are not on separate axes; they are on separate
*directions*, in superposition, and finding them is the business of the sparse autoencoders mentioned in
13.4.

The causal-robustness claim, meanwhile, remains a good idea with little empirical support at scale.
Distribution shift is still largely unsolved, and no model has demonstrably solved it by recovering causal
structure.
