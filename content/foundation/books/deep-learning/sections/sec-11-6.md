# 11.6 Example: Multi-Digit Number Recognition

Chapter 11 closes by walking through a real project end to end — transcribing address numbers from Street
View imagery — and applying each of the previous sections in order.

## The process

**The metric came first, from the product requirement.** The target was human-level accuracy of 98%, and
the design decision that made it achievable was to allow the system to **decline**. Accuracy was fixed at
98% and the quantity optimised was **coverage** — the fraction of inputs the system was willing to
transcribe. The project reached 95% coverage at that accuracy.

**The baseline was conventional.** A convolutional network with rectified linear units, chosen because the
task is vision with known spatial structure. The output was structured: the model predicted a sequence of
digits, and the book notes the naive approach of an independent classifier per digit was replaced by one
optimising the log-likelihood of the whole sequence.

**Training and test error were compared to decide what to do next.** The observation was that training
error and test error were both high and close together, which — by the reasoning of 11.3 — indicated
underfitting or a software defect rather than a data shortage.

**Visualising the worst mistakes found the bug.** Inspecting the examples the model was most confident and
most wrong about revealed that the input cropping was too tight: the preprocessing was cutting digits off
the edge of the number. Widening the crop region gained several percentage points of coverage.

The remaining improvements came from hyperparameter tuning — mostly making the model larger while
controlling overfitting.

## My take

The cropping bug is the point of the whole chapter, and it is worth dwelling on. The problem was not the
architecture, the optimiser, the loss, or the amount of data. It was that the preprocessing threw away the
information the model needed, and no amount of work on the model would have recovered it. It was found by
looking at pictures, and it would not have been found by looking at a loss curve.

That is the argument for Section 11.5's insistence on visualisation, and the reason this chapter exists at
all in a book otherwise about mathematics. The failure modes that dominate real projects are not the ones
the theory describes.

The coverage framing is the other durable lesson. Turning "be accurate" into "be accurate on as much as
possible, and abstain otherwise" changed the problem into one that could be shipped, and it is a
reframing available far more often than it is used. Any system with a fallback — a human reviewer, a
larger model, a default answer — can trade coverage for accuracy, and the trade is usually a much better
deal than the equivalent effort spent on the model.

What is dated is only the scale. A team today would start from a pretrained backbone and reach the same
place faster. The process — fix the metric, build a baseline, diagnose from the train/test gap, look at
the failures — is unchanged, and it is the part of this book that generalises past deep learning entirely.
