# 19.4 Variational Inference and Learning

The chapter's main event: restrict $q$ to a tractable family and optimise the bound within it.

## The strategy

The ELBO of 19.1 is exact when $q$ is the true posterior and computable when $q$ is simple. Variational
inference takes the second option: choose a restricted family $\mathcal{Q}$ and find the best member of it.

The most common restriction is the **mean-field** approximation, in which the latent variables are assumed
independent given the observations:

$$
q(h \mid v) = \prod_i q\left( h_i \mid v \right)
$$

The posterior may have arbitrary dependencies among the latents; the approximation asserts it does not.
More generally, **structured variational inference** allows some dependencies by imposing a graphical
structure on $q$.

The optimisation itself typically has a fixed-point character: because the bound is a functional of $q$,
the calculus of variations gives, for each factor, an update that maximises the bound with the others held
fixed. For discrete latent variables the parameters of $q$ are ordinary numbers and gradient methods apply
directly; for continuous ones the optimisation is over functions, and the calculus of variations supplies
the stationary condition.

The book notes that in some cases the functional form of the optimal $q$ need not be specified in advance —
it falls out of the optimisation.

## The direction of the KL

The book gives sustained attention to a consequence of the objective. Variational inference minimises
$D_{KL}(q \parallel p)$, not $D_{KL}(p \parallel q)$. As Section 3.13 established, this direction is
**mode-seeking**: $q$ is heavily penalised for placing mass where $p$ has little, and barely penalised for
missing mass that $p$ has.

So variational inference produces an approximation that fits **one mode** of the posterior and ignores the
others, and that is **narrower** than the true posterior. The book is explicit that this leads to
systematic underestimation of uncertainty.

## My take

The mode-seeking bias is the most practically important thing in Chapter 19, and it is worth carrying as
a prediction rather than a caveat. Any system whose uncertainty estimate comes from a variational
approximation is reporting less uncertainty than it has, in a direction that is known in advance.

Mean field itself is a strong assumption that is usually false — latent variables in a real model are
correlated, often strongly — and its practical effect compounds the narrowing, since a factorised $q$
cannot represent the correlations that make the true posterior broad in some directions.

What the section does not have, and what made variational inference into a general tool, is the
**reparameterisation trick**. The obstacle to using gradient descent on a continuous $q$ is that the
objective is an expectation under $q$, whose parameters are what is being optimised — so the gradient does
not pass through the sampling step. Writing the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and
an independent noise variable moves the randomness out of the path, and the gradient flows. That single
change, published the year before this book and not covered here, is what turned variational inference from
a model-by-model derivation into a procedure that works for any differentiable model, and it is what makes
the variational autoencoder trainable by ordinary back-propagation.
