**Lilian Weng · 2021 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-01-02-controllable-text-generation/)**

This post is a photograph of the field taken eleven months before ChatGPT, and it is worth reading mostly for what it does not know. The question it addresses — how do you make a language model produce text with a property you want, when it was only ever trained to continue documents — was in 2021 a genuinely open research area with a dozen live answers. The post catalogues them carefully. Within two years, one answer had absorbed almost all the others.

**It opens with decoding, and this part has aged best.** Decoding is how you turn the model's probabilities into actual text, and the surprising finding is that always picking the most probable continuation produces *bad* writing — repetitive, bland and self-similar. The diagnosis is worth remembering: human writing is not the most probable continuation. Real text keeps stepping off the high-probability path in ways a likelihood maximiser never will.

**Nucleus sampling** is the fix that stuck. Sample from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to `p`, so the pool of candidates widens automatically where the model is genuinely uncertain and narrows where it is confident. That is still the default decoder five years later.

Then the control methods, roughly in order of how much of the model they touch. **Guided decoding** steers at generation time: PPLM nudges the model's own internal activations toward whatever an attribute classifier likes; GeDi uses a small specialised language model as a cheaper judge; FUDGE scores partial sentences by predicting whether the *finished* text will have the property you want. **CTRL** goes the other way and bakes control codes into pretraining, so a single leading token selects domain and style. **Soft prompts** — prefix tuning, prompt tuning, AutoPrompt — learn a few vectors to prepend to the input, tuning a handful of parameters instead of the whole model. **Unlikelihood training** adds an explicit penalty on tokens you do not want, rather than only rewarding those you do.

And, in a section no longer than the others, reinforcement learning from human preferences.

> The 2021 problem was that a language model had no notion of a *good* continuation, only a likely one, and every method here is a different place to inject that missing preference: the decoder, the prompt, the objective, or the weights.

**What did not survive: nearly all of it, and the manner of death is the lesson.** Instruction tuning followed by RLHF did not out-compete these methods on their own terms. It made the question different. Once a model has been trained to follow instructions, controlling its topic, sentiment, style or safety is done by *asking* — and an inference-time discriminator steering activations toward a sentiment classifier is a great deal of machinery for something a sentence now accomplishes. CTRL's control codes became the system prompt. PPLM, GeDi and FUDGE are cited today mostly by each other. Soft prompts survived in mutated form as parameter-efficient fine-tuning, where LoRA won by adapting weights rather than inputs. Unlikelihood training's descendant is the negative half of a [DPO](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-3) pair.

The pattern worth carrying forward: **a research area with a dozen competing partial solutions is often a sign that the framing is wrong**, and the resolution arrives from a direction that dissolves the question rather than answering it. Read the post for nucleus sampling and the degeneration argument, both permanent, and read the rest as an unusually well-documented account of a field a year before it was reorganised.
