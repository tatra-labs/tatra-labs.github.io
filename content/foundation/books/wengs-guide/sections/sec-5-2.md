**Lilian Weng · 2025 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2025-05-01-thinking/)**

The framing that makes this post more than a survey arrives early: **treat the chain of thought as a latent variable.** The probability of an answer given a question is a sum over reasoning traces `z` of `P(z | question) * P(answer | z, question)`. Once written that way, a great deal of scattered work snaps into place. Chain-of-thought prompting is conditioning on a plausible `z`. Self-consistency is a Monte Carlo estimate of the marginal. STaR — sample traces, keep the ones that reach the right answer, fine-tune on them — is hard expectation-maximisation, with the sampling as the E step and the fine-tuning as the M step. Nobody had to invent a new theory of reasoning; this is latent-variable modelling, and the latent happens to be readable.

The second organising idea is **computation as a resource**. A transformer spends fixed compute per token, so the only way to spend more on a harder problem is to emit more tokens. Thinking in tokens is thinking in FLOPs, and the post is clear that this is the mechanism rather than a metaphor.

From there the survey covers the ways of spending them. **Sequential revision** — generate, critique, correct — and its documented hazard: without external feedback, models revise correct answers into incorrect ones, because the critique step has nothing to check against and self-doubt is not calibrated. **Parallel sampling** with a verifier or process reward model scoring intermediate steps, then beam search over partial reasoning. **Tool use**, offloading the parts a model is bad at, which is the reliable version of the same idea. **Reinforcement learning for reasoning**, where the striking result is DeepSeek-R1's demonstration that **pure RL against verifiable answers, with no supervised reasoning data at all**, produces reflection, backtracking and self-correction as emergent behaviours — the model discovers deliberation rather than imitating it. And **thinking in continuous space**: recurrent depth, filler or pause tokens that buy computation without committing to words, which sacrifices the one genuinely valuable property of the token-based approach.

That property is **faithfulness**, and the post treats it as a first-class concern rather than a footnote. A readable chain of thought is only useful as an observation channel if it reflects the computation. The encouraging finding is that reasoning models trained with RL appear more faithful than earlier models under misleading prompts — more likely to acknowledge a hint they used. The discouraging one is [what happens when you optimise against a monitor](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1).

> The reasoning trace is a latent variable that happens to be legible, and its legibility is a property we can lose — it is not guaranteed by the architecture and it does not survive being optimised.

**What the post is careful about, and most coverage is not.** Test-time compute is not a substitute for capability. The finding it reports is that extra thinking helps most on problems of *moderate* difficulty relative to the model — where the answer is within reach and the model needs room to find it — and does not bridge large gaps. A model that cannot do a thing does not become able to do it by thinking longer; it produces a longer wrong answer, at higher cost.

Its six open problems are the best short statement of the current research frontier, and the first is the one that matters: can a model be incentivised to produce **human-readable, faithful** reasoning without that incentive teaching it to perform readability instead? The rest — defining reward hacking without playing whack-a-mole, self-correction that does not hallucinate, extending RL to tasks that are hard to grade, distilling test-time gains back into the weights, and allocating thinking time by difficulty — are all live, and all of them are versions of the measurement problem this guide keeps returning to.
