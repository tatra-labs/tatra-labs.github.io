**Lilian Weng · 2025 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2025-05-01-thinking/)**

The framing that makes this post more than a survey arrives early: **treat the chain of thought as a hidden variable.**

Here is what that means. The probability the model gets the right answer is not one quantity but a sum: over all the reasoning traces it might produce, the chance of producing that trace times the chance the trace leads to the right answer. Write it that way and a great deal of scattered work snaps into place at once. Chain-of-thought prompting is choosing a plausible trace to condition on. Self-consistency — sampling many traces and voting — is estimating that sum by sampling. STaR, which samples traces, keeps the ones that reached the right answer, and fine-tunes on those, is the classical expectation-maximisation algorithm: sampling as one step, fine-tuning as the other. **Nobody had to invent a new theory of reasoning.** This is ordinary latent-variable modelling, and the latent variable happens to be readable English.

The second organising idea is **computation as a resource**. A transformer spends a fixed amount of compute per token, so the only way to spend more of it on a harder problem is to emit more tokens. Thinking in tokens is thinking in FLOPs, and the post is clear this is the mechanism rather than a metaphor.

From there the survey covers the ways of spending them. **Sequential revision** — generate, critique, correct — along with its documented hazard: without external feedback, models revise *correct* answers into incorrect ones, because the critique step has nothing to check against and a model's self-doubt is not calibrated. **Parallel sampling**, with a verifier or a process reward model scoring intermediate steps and a search over partial reasoning. **Tool use**, offloading the parts the model is bad at, which is the reliable version of the same idea.

**Reinforcement learning for reasoning** is where the striking result sits. DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated that pure RL against checkable answers — **with no supervised examples of reasoning at all** — produces reflection, backtracking and self-correction as emergent behaviours. The model discovers deliberation rather than imitating it.

And **thinking in continuous space**: recurrent depth, or filler tokens that buy computation without committing to particular words. That last one sacrifices the single most valuable property of the token-based approach.

That property is **faithfulness**, and the post treats it as a first-class concern rather than a footnote. A readable chain of thought is only useful as a window onto the model if it actually reflects the computation. The encouraging finding is that reasoning models trained with RL appear more faithful than earlier models when given misleading prompts — more likely to admit a hint they used. The discouraging one is [what happens when you optimise against a monitor](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1).

> The reasoning trace is a latent variable that happens to be legible, and its legibility is a property we can lose — it is not guaranteed by the architecture and it does not survive being optimised.

**What the post is careful about, and most coverage is not.** Test-time compute is not a substitute for capability. The finding it reports is that extra thinking helps most on problems of *moderate* difficulty relative to the model — where the answer is within reach and the model just needs room to find it — and does not bridge large gaps. **A model that cannot do a thing does not become able to do it by thinking longer.** It produces a longer wrong answer, at higher cost.

Its six open problems are the best short statement of the current research frontier, and the first is the one that matters: can a model be incentivised to produce **human-readable, faithful** reasoning without that incentive teaching it to merely perform readability? The rest — defining reward hacking without playing whack-a-mole, self-correction that does not hallucinate, extending RL to tasks that are hard to grade, folding test-time gains back into the weights, and allocating thinking time according to difficulty — are all live, and all of them are versions of the measurement problem this guide keeps returning to.
