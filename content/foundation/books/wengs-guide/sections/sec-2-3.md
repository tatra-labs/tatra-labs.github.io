**Lilian Weng · 2019 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2019-05-05-domain-randomization/)**

Training a robot in the real world is slow, expensive and destructive. Training it in simulation is fast, free and safe, and produces a policy that fails the moment it meets a real actuator. The gap between the two is not a small calibration error — friction is wrong, latency is wrong, the camera's colour response is wrong, and a policy trained against a specific set of wrong physics has learned to exploit them.

**Domain randomization's answer is to stop trying to make the simulator correct.** Randomise it instead: textures, lighting, object colours and positions, camera pose, friction coefficients, masses, damping, actuator delay. Sample new values every episode. A policy that must succeed across that entire distribution cannot rely on any particular value being true, and if the randomisation range is wide enough to contain reality, **the real world becomes just another sample** — not a distribution shift the policy has to survive, but one more draw it was trained on.

The post traces the refinements. Uniform randomisation over hand-chosen ranges is the baseline and requires someone to pick the ranges, which is the same tuning problem in different clothes. Guided approaches use a small amount of real-world data to fit the randomisation distribution, closing the loop rather than guessing. **Automatic domain randomization** starts narrow and widens each parameter's range as the policy's performance permits, which makes it simultaneously a randomisation scheme and a [curriculum](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-2-1).

The most interesting reported effect is emergent. A recurrent policy trained under randomised dynamics often develops **implicit system identification**: over the first few steps in a new environment it infers the physical parameters it is facing from how the system responds, and adjusts. Nobody asked for that. It appears because inferring the dynamics is the best available strategy when the dynamics vary and you are scored across all of them — which makes this, again, [meta-learning](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-2-2) arriving as a side effect of the training distribution rather than as a design.

> Do not try to make the simulator match reality; make it vary so much that reality is inside the range, and let the policy work out where it landed.

**What to be careful about:** the flagship demonstration. OpenAI's robot hand solving a Rubik's cube, trained with automatic domain randomization, was widely reported as a robot learning to solve a Rubik's cube, and that is not what happened. **The solution sequence was computed by a classical solver**; the learned component was the manipulation — rotating faces and reorienting the cube in-hand — which is genuinely hard and genuinely impressive and is not the same claim. Success rates on the full scramble were modest, and the framing generated more excitement than the result supported. It is a useful case to keep in mind whenever a robotics demonstration is described in terms of the task rather than the controller.

**Where it applies now.** For anyone training language models, the simulator is a synthetic data generator and the lesson carries over intact: if the surface form of generated data is not varied aggressively, the model learns the generator's format rather than the underlying task. The same logic underwrites augmentation with distractors and paraphrases against the context-sensitivity that [System 2 Attention](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-5-4) diagnoses. And it is a standing argument about evaluation — an agent measured in a single environment configuration has not been shown to be robust, only to have been measured once. The failure mode domain randomization was invented for, a policy quietly exploiting a fixed quirk of its training environment, is the same one that appears as [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1) when the environment is a reward model.
