**Lilian Weng · 2018 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2018-01-23-multi-armed-bandit/)**

Strip everything from reinforcement learning except the one dilemma at its centre and you get the bandit. `K` slot machines, each paying out with an unknown probability, one pull per round. No states, no transitions, no future to plan for. All that remains is the question of whether to pull the arm that has looked best so far or to gather more evidence about the others, and it turns out this is where most of the conceptual difficulty lived.

The measure is **regret**: the reward you would have accumulated always pulling the genuinely best arm, minus what you actually got. The goal is not to find the best arm — that is easy and expensive — but to lose as little as possible while finding it.

The post works through the solutions in order of sophistication. **Epsilon-greedy** explores at random a fixed fraction of the time. It works, and its flaw is that it keeps exploring arms it has already established are bad, at the same rate, forever.

**Upper confidence bounds** fix that with the principle of *optimism in the face of uncertainty*. Do not act on the estimated value of an arm; act on the upper end of its confidence interval. UCB1 takes `Q(a) + sqrt(2*log(t) / N(a))`, where the bonus shrinks as an arm is pulled more often — a direct application of Hoeffding's inequality. An arm is tried either because it looks good or because you do not yet know that it is not, and the second reason expires on its own as evidence accumulates. No exploration parameter has to be tuned or annealed.

**Thompson sampling** is the most elegant and the most practical. Maintain a posterior over each arm's payout probability — a Beta distribution, for Bernoulli rewards, updated by counting successes and failures. Each round, **draw one sample from each posterior and pull the argmax.** An arm is chosen with exactly the probability that it is the best one given current belief. Exploration is not a rule bolted onto the algorithm; it falls out of the uncertainty in the posterior and disappears as the posterior sharpens.

> Optimism and posterior sampling both exploit the same fact: the right amount of exploration is a function of how uncertain you are, so any procedure that represents uncertainty gets exploration for free.

**Why this is the most relevant post in the chapter, despite being the simplest.** As [1.1](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-1-1) argues, RLHF is structurally a bandit problem, not a sequential one — a prompt arrives, the model emits one response, a reward model scores it, and nothing carries over. If you read only one post here before working on post-training, this is a better use of an hour than the policy-gradient survey.

**What the post does not cover, and it is the half you need.** These are *context-free* bandits: `K` fixed arms with fixed payout rates. The LLM case is a **contextual** bandit, where the prompt is the context and the arm's value depends on it, and the post gives that setting only a passing mention. LinUCB and contextual Thompson sampling are the classical answers, and they assume a small, enumerable action set — whereas the action space of a language model is every string it could produce. The regret bounds do not transfer, and it is worth being clear that they cannot: the guarantees rest on being able to pull each arm often enough to bound its confidence interval, and there is no sense in which you can sample the space of possible responses to sufficiency.

What transfers is the intuition, and it transfers well. Best-of-`n` sampling is a crude exploration budget. The entropy and KL terms in every RLHF objective are exploration controls under different names. And the current problem of **entropy collapse** in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards — models becoming deterministic during training, improving on the first attempt while getting worse across many — is a bandit failure exactly: an agent that has stopped exploring, converging early on an arm that merely looked best.
