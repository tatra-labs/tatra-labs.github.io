**Lilian Weng · 2018, revised through 2019 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2018-04-08-policy-gradient/)**

The most useful single document on this list. Roughly twenty algorithms — REINFORCE, actor-critic, A3C, A2C, DPG, DDPG, D4PG, MADDPG, TRPO, PPO, ACER, ACKTR, SAC, TD3, SVPG, IMPALA — derived in **one consistent notation**, each presented as a fix for a specific defect in the one before it. The original papers do not agree on symbols and mostly do not acknowledge each other's framing; this post makes them a single argument, and doing that is the expensive part nobody else did.

**The spine is the policy gradient theorem, and its content is a deletion.**

Here is the problem it solves. You want to improve your policy, so you want to know how the total reward changes as you adjust its parameters. But changing the policy changes *which situations the agent ends up in*, and how often. That distribution over situations depends on the policy in a way you cannot possibly compute without knowing the rules of the world — which is exactly what you do not have.

The theorem says that term **vanishes**. The gradient works out to a sum over situations, weighted by how often you visit them, of the action values times the gradient of the policy — with no derivative of the visiting distribution anywhere in it. **So you can sample situations by simply acting in the world, and ignore the fact that your policy shaped which ones you saw.** Without this, model-free policy optimisation does not exist.

Then the sequence of repairs, each addressing the previous method's failure.

**REINFORCE** plugs in the actual observed return. Correct on average, and so noisy it is often unusable — the same action can look brilliant or terrible depending on what else happened in that episode. **Subtract a baseline** — any function of the situation, such as "how well do I usually do from here" — and the estimate stays correct on average while the noise drops sharply, because you are now measuring whether an action beat expectations rather than its raw outcome. **Actor-critic** makes that baseline a learned value function, which is where the trade between noise and accuracy begins in earnest. **A3C** runs many workers in parallel so consecutive samples are not all from the same situation; **A2C** observes that the asynchrony was incidental and batches them synchronously instead.

**TRPO** addresses the step-size problem, which is peculiar to this setting and worth understanding. A small change in parameters can produce a large change in *behaviour*. The agent then collects all its subsequent data under a much worse policy, and the run never recovers — unlike supervised learning, a bad step here poisons the data that comes next. TRPO constrains how far the new policy's behaviour may move from the old one, buying a guarantee of improvement at the cost of a constrained optimisation problem with heavy machinery in it. **PPO** replaces the hard constraint with a **clipped objective**: form the ratio between the new policy's probability for an action and the old one's, and clip it, so the objective simply flattens out once the policy has moved far enough and there is no longer any gradient pulling it further. First-order, a few lines of code, most of the benefit. That is why PPO won, and why it was sitting on the shelf when [InstructGPT](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) needed an optimiser.

> Every algorithm after REINFORCE is buying variance reduction or step-size safety, and each purchase is paid for in bias, complexity, or both.

**What did not survive, and it is a satisfying reversal.** These algorithms were designed for continuous control — a robot arm, a walking gait — where episodes are long, rewards are sparse and delayed, and assigning credit across hundreds of timesteps is the entire difficulty. Transplanted into RLHF, PPO carries machinery for a problem that is not there. As [1.1](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-1-1) notes, scoring one response is a bandit problem: no state transitions, no bootstrapping, nothing for the advantage estimator to estimate across.

The field spent several years discovering this. **GRPO** (2024) dropped the learned value network entirely, replacing the baseline with the average reward of a group of responses sampled for the same prompt — a baseline computed straight from data rather than fitted, which is closer to REINFORCE-with-a-baseline than to actor-critic. Ahmadian et al.'s "Back to Basics" (2024) went further, showing plain REINFORCE-style estimators match or beat PPO for RLHF, because the noise PPO's critic was built to suppress is mostly absent here. **The first algorithm in the post turned out to be the right one for its largest application**, after six years of increasingly elaborate alternatives. That is worth knowing before you spend a week tuning a critic that has nothing to do.
