Lilian Weng has been writing [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/) since 2017. There are around sixty posts. Several of them are the best available treatment of their subject in any format — better than the survey papers, and free — and a few have quietly become the thing people cite when they cannot be bothered to cite fourteen primary sources.

**There is no guide.** She has never published a curriculum, a syllabus or a recommended order. Lil'Log is a blog: reverse-chronological, tagged, and otherwise unsorted. The front page in 2026 opens on recursive self-improvement and, ten entries later, is explaining contrastive learning. That is the correct way to run a blog and a poor way to learn a subject.

**So the curation here is mine and the writing is hers**, and it matters that those two things stay separate. I have chosen twenty posts, dropped roughly forty, and put the twenty in an order that goes somewhere. Nothing in the selection came from her. Where I disagree with a post I say so in that entry rather than quietly leaving it out.

**Why her posts in particular, when the primary literature is right there.** Two reasons, and the second is the real one.

The first is coverage. A Lil'Log post is typically thirty to eighty references worked into a single argument, with consistent notation across all of them — which is the expensive part, and the part survey papers usually skip. *Policy Gradient Algorithms* puts REINFORCE, A3C, DPG, DDPG, TRPO, PPO, ACER, SAC and a dozen more into one set of symbols. Reading it is faster than reading four of those papers, and you come out able to read the rest.

The second is that **the sequence is a career, not a syllabus**, which makes it unusually honest about which problems actually bite. She joined OpenAI in 2018 and the 2018–2020 posts are robotics-era reinforcement learning: policy gradients, exploration, curricula, sim-to-real. She ran Safety Systems and later became VP of Research and Safety, and the 2021–2024 posts turn to toxicity, controllable generation, human data quality and reward hacking. She left in November 2024, co-founded Thinking Machines Lab, [stepped down in July 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/thinking-machines-lab-cofounder-lilian-103336583.html) citing illness, and returned to OpenAI days later to lead work on recursive self-improvement — the subject of the post she had published that same month. You are reading someone's problems in the order they became her problems. That is worth more than a well-balanced curriculum.

**How these notes are organised.** Five chapters:

- **1. The Reinforcement Learning Spine.** The four posts that are effectively a textbook — the RL overview, policy gradients, bandits, exploration — plus the implementation post, because none of it is real until something trains.
- **2. When the Environment Is the Problem.** Curriculum, meta-RL, domain randomisation, evolution strategies. Everything here is an admission that the hard part of RL is usually not the algorithm.
- **3. Steering a Language Model.** Where the blog turns. Controllable generation, toxicity, prompting, and the human data underneath all of it.
- **4. Where Alignment Breaks.** Reward hacking, hallucination, adversarial attacks. Three failure modes, one per post, each treated as a measurement problem rather than a moral one.
- **5. The Current Frontier.** Agents, test-time thinking, harness engineering, and scaling laws done carefully.

**What I have left out**, so the gaps are stated rather than implied. The generative-modelling line — GANs, VAEs, flows, diffusion, video diffusion — is excellent and belongs in a different guide. So do the systems posts (*How to Train Really Large Models on Many GPUs*, *Large Transformer Model Inference Optimization*), the vision work (the four-part object-detection series, visual language models), the *Learning with not Enough Data* trilogy, *Some Math behind Neural Tangent Kernel*, and the two Transformer Family surveys, which the [Sutskever notes](/foundation/book/sutskevers-list?section=sec-2-5) already cover from the primary sources. Dropping about forty posts to keep twenty is the only way this stays a route rather than an index.

**One thing to know before you start.** These notes are not summaries and they are not a substitute. Her posts are long, and the value is in the equations and the reference trail, both of which I have deliberately not reproduced. What each entry gives you is the mechanism in enough detail to know whether you need the post, the one claim the post exists to make, and an honest note on what has not held up. A blog written between 2017 and 2026 has plenty that has not held up; the RL chapters in particular describe an approach to sequential decision-making that language models have largely absorbed and replaced. Saying so is the point.

**If five chapters is too much:** read *A (Long) Peek into Reinforcement Learning*, then *Policy Gradient Algorithms*, then jump straight to *Reward Hacking in Reinforcement Learning*. That is the shortest path from "what is a value function" to understanding why the current generation of models behaves the way it does.
