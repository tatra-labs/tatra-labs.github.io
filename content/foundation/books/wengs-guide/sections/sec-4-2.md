**Lilian Weng · 2024 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2024-07-07-hallucination/)**

The post begins by narrowing the word, which is the first useful thing anyone can do with it. "Hallucination" is used for two distinct failures that have different causes and different fixes. **In-context hallucination** is output that contradicts material supplied in the prompt — a summary that misstates the document it was given. **Extrinsic hallucination** is output unsupported by anything the model reliably knows: a fabricated citation, a plausible and non-existent API, a confident biography of someone who does not exist. The post is about the second, and treats it as a factuality-and-abstention problem rather than a mystery.

**On causes, the finding that changed practice is Gekhman et al.'s.** Fine-tuning a model on facts it does not already know *increases* hallucination. The mechanism is straightforward once stated: supervised fine-tuning teaches the model to produce confident answers in the demonstrated format, and if the demonstrated answers concern facts absent from pretraining, what the model learns is not those facts but **the behaviour of answering confidently about things it does not know**. The examples generalise as a style. This inverts the naive instinct to fix knowledge gaps with more fine-tuning data, and it is the single most actionable result in the post.

The evaluation section is the most useful catalogue available. FActScore decomposes a generation into atomic facts and checks each against a source, which is the right granularity — a paragraph is not true or false, its claims are. SAFE has a model issue search queries per atomic fact and reason over the results. SelfCheckGPT needs no external source at all: sample several generations at temperature and check them against each other, on the logic that **a fact the model knows is stable across samples while a fabricated one varies**, which makes inconsistency a cheap proxy for unreliability.

Mitigations divide into grounding and calibration. Retrieval-based methods attribute or repair claims post hoc; chain-of-verification has the model draft, generate questions against its own draft, answer them independently, and revise. The calibration line asks whether models know what they do not know, and finds they partly do — internal signals correlate with correctness well enough to be usable, which means the failure is often not ignorance but the absence of any incentive to admit it.

> A model fine-tuned on facts it never learned does not acquire the facts; it acquires the habit of answering as though it had.

**What has sharpened since.** In 2025 Kalai and colleagues at OpenAI made the incentive argument explicit and formal: **standard benchmarks reward guessing.** Under binary scoring, an abstention scores zero with certainty while a guess has some chance of scoring one, so a model optimised against such benchmarks is being trained to bluff whenever its confidence exceeds zero. Hallucination on this account is not primarily a knowledge defect or a decoding artifact but the rational strategy under the scoring rule the field chose, and the remedy is at the level of evaluation design — scoring rules that give partial credit for calibrated abstention, and confidence thresholds stated in the task.

That is a satisfying convergence with the rest of this guide. It is the same shape as [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1): a measure that does not penalise the failure mode, optimised hard, producing a model that exploits the gap. And it is the reason [retrieval](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-6-1) reduces hallucination without solving it — grounding supplies evidence, and does nothing about a system whose training taught it that an unsupported answer beats no answer.
