The attacks themselves are covered from the attacker's side in Karpathy's list — [the taxonomy of why jailbreaks work](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-7-1) and [the optimisation method that automates them](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-7-2). This post is the same material written from the other chair, by someone then running safety systems for a deployed model, and the difference in emphasis is the reason to read both.

The survey is thorough — token manipulation, gradient-guided search over discrete inputs, jailbreak prompting, human red teaming, and using one model to generate attacks against another — and it includes the line that gives the post its character: "We (including my team at OpenAI, shoutout to them) have invested a lot of effort..." **This is a defender's document.** It is organised around what you can actually deploy, and it is candid that the answer is *not much, with any guarantee*.

**The structural argument is the part worth extracting**, and it explains why this problem is harder than it looks rather than merely unsolved.

Adversarial robustness in vision has a decade of work behind it and one durable result: certified defences — ones that come with a mathematical guarantee — exist for small perturbations and do not scale to realistic threats, while everything else is an arms race. Now ask why even those limited guarantees exist. They lean on **continuity**. An image perturbation is small in a measurable sense, so you can reason about a ball of nearby images around the input and prove things about all of them at once.

**Language has no such measure, and this is not a gap waiting to be filled.** There is no meaningful ball around a sentence. A one-token change can invert its meaning; a paraphrase that replaces every token can leave it identical. The mathematical structure the certified-defence literature depends on is simply absent, so those guarantees do not transfer even in principle.

What remains is heuristic, and each piece has a cost the post is honest about. Filters that catch gibberish suffixes miss fluent attacks. Paraphrasing the input breaks an optimised token sequence and degrades legitimate prompts. Adversarial training helps against the distribution of attacks you trained on. Guardrail classifiers add latency and are themselves attackable. Red teaming, human and automated, finds instances — and finding instances is not the same as bounding the space of them.

> Certified robustness in vision rests on inputs living in a continuous space with a distance function; language has neither, so there is no version of that guarantee to port.

**What has and has not changed.** The specific attacks are patched, as they always are, and defence in depth has become the standard posture: classifiers at input and output, constitutional and deliberative methods where the model reasons about policy before answering, and adversarial training against automatically generated attacks. Measured jailbreak rates on frontier models are far below 2023 levels. None of that is a guarantee, and the post's central claim — that we lack a principled defence and are managing a risk rather than eliminating it — has held.

The threat model has also moved, in a direction that makes this post's framing incomplete. Nearly everything here assumes **the attacker is the user**. The dominant practical risk in 2026 is [indirect prompt injection](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-7-3), where the attacker is a web page an agent reads on somebody else's behalf, and the defences catalogued here barely address it. A guardrail classifier tuned to spot a user trying to extract dangerous instructions is not looking for a sentence buried in a retrieved document that tells the agent to email its context somewhere. Read this alongside Weng's [harness](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-3) post, which is where the agentic surface she was describing at OpenAI in 2023 actually ended up.
