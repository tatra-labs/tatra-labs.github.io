**Lilian Weng · 2021 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-03-21-lm-toxicity/)**

The post spends its first section refusing to get to the methods, and that is exactly why it is in this guide. Before anything can be reduced, it has to be measured — and **toxicity does not have a definition that survives contact with a measurement instrument.**

Look at what the standard instrument actually reports. Perspective API, the classifier the entire literature scores against, returns the probability that *a rater would find a comment rude or disrespectful*. So the target is not a property of the text at all. It is a prediction about a population of annotators, and it inherits whatever that population happened to think.

The consequences are documented and severe. Toxicity classifiers systematically over-flag African-American English. They flag neutral sentences that merely *mention* an identity group — "I am a gay man" scores as toxic in a way "I am a man" does not — because the training data associates those terms with the abuse directed at the people they describe. So a benchmark built on such a classifier will reward a model for avoiding entire subjects, and will score that avoidance as safety.

With that established, the mitigations. **Data-based**: filter the pretraining corpus, or continue training on a clean subset. **Decoding-time**: blocklists and vocabulary shifting, or the guided methods from [the previous entry](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-1). DExperts is the neatest — run a deliberately toxic model and a deliberately non-toxic one alongside the base model and use the *difference* between their predictions to reshape the output, so the toxic model contributes by being subtracted. **Fine-tuning**: condition on the attribute, or continue pretraining on non-toxic text.

**The finding that matters most is that filtering has a cost, and the cost is not small.** Aggressively removing toxic pretraining data damages the model in two distinct ways.

First, it reduces the model's ability to *recognise* toxicity at all. A system that has never seen an insult cannot reliably detect, classify or refuse one — so the moderation capability everyone wants turns out to depend on exposure to the thing being moderated. Second, the filters inherit the classifier biases described above: work on the C4 corpus found that blocklist-based filtering disproportionately removed text about and by minority groups, along with a great deal of ordinary discussion of sexuality and disability. **The filter does not remove toxicity evenly. It removes the topics near it.**

> The instrument that defines toxicity is a model of annotator reactions, so every intervention optimises against those annotators' judgements — including their blind spots, which the pipeline then encodes as safety.

**What this became.** As a technique catalogue the post is largely obsolete: nobody runs DExperts, and toxicity control now happens through the safety half of preference training plus classifiers at the API boundary. The measurement problem went nowhere at all. Every safety reward model is a descendant of the classifier described here, trained on aggregated human labels with the same subjectivity underneath — and it is then **optimised against hard**, which is precisely the setup for [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1): a model that learns the shape of what raters penalise rather than the substance of it.

The over-refusal behaviour of [Llama 2-Chat](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-2) is this post's prediction arriving on schedule — a model declining to discuss medicine, security or history because those subjects sit near the region its safety signal marked dangerous. Weng returns to the underlying issue, that annotator disagreement carries information a single aggregated label destroys, in [Thinking about High-Quality Human Data](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-4), which is the mature statement of the same argument and should be read as the sequel to this one.
