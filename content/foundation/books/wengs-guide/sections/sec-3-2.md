**Lilian Weng · 2021 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-03-21-lm-toxicity/)**

The post spends its first section refusing to get to the methods, and that is the reason it is in this guide. Before anything can be reduced, it has to be measured, and **toxicity does not have a definition that survives contact with a measurement instrument.** Perspective API, the classifier the entire literature scores against, returns the probability that a rater would find a comment rude or disrespectful — which makes the target not a property of the text but a prediction about a population of annotators, inheriting whatever that population thought.

The consequences are documented and severe. Toxicity classifiers systematically over-flag African-American English. They flag neutral sentences that merely *mention* an identity group — "I am a gay man" scores as toxic in a way "I am a man" does not — because the training data associates those terms with the abuse directed at the people they describe. So a benchmark built on such a classifier will reward a model for avoiding entire subjects, and score that avoidance as safety.

With that established, the mitigations. **Data-based**: filter the pretraining corpus, or continue pretraining on a clean subset. **Decoding-time**: blocklists and vocabulary shifting, or the discriminator-guided methods from [the previous entry](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-1); DExperts is the neatest, running a toxic expert and a non-toxic expert alongside the base model and using the *difference* in their predictions to reshape the output distribution, so the toxic model contributes by being subtracted. **Fine-tuning**: attribute conditioning, or domain-adaptive pretraining on non-toxic text.

**The finding that matters most is that filtering has a cost, and the cost is not small.** Aggressive removal of toxic pretraining data damages the model in two ways. It reduces the model's ability to *recognise* toxicity at all — a system that has never seen an insult cannot reliably detect, classify or refuse one, so the moderation capability everyone wants depends on exposure to the thing being moderated. And the filters themselves inherit the classifier biases above: work on the C4 corpus found that blocklist-based filtering disproportionately removed text about and by minority groups, along with a great deal of ordinary discussion of sexuality and disability. The filter does not remove toxicity evenly; it removes the topics near it.

> The instrument that defines toxicity is a model of annotator reactions, so every intervention optimises against those annotators' judgements — including their blind spots, which the pipeline then encodes as safety.

**What this became.** As a technique catalogue the post is largely obsolete: nobody runs DExperts, and toxicity control now happens through the safety half of preference training plus classifier guardrails at the API boundary. The measurement problem went nowhere at all. Every safety reward model is a descendant of the classifier described here, trained on aggregated human labels with the same subjectivity underneath, and it is then **optimised against hard**, which is exactly the setup for [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1) — a model that learns the shape of what raters penalise rather than the substance.

The over-refusal behaviour of [Llama 2-Chat](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-2) is this post's prediction arriving on schedule: a model that declines to discuss medicine, security or history because those subjects sit near the region its safety signal marked dangerous. Weng returns to the underlying issue — that annotator disagreement carries information a single aggregated label destroys — in [Thinking about High-Quality Human Data](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-4), which is the more mature statement of the same argument and should be read as the sequel to this one.
