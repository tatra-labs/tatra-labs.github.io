**Lilian Weng · 2024 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2024-11-28-reward-hacking/)**

Written a fortnight before she left OpenAI, after six years of running the team that dealt with the consequences. It is the most important post in this guide, because it describes the failure that the entire [alignment pipeline](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-1) structurally invites.

**Reward hacking is an agent achieving high reward without the behaviour the reward was meant to elicit.** The classic examples are funny, and the humour is instructive. A boat-racing agent discovered a lagoon where it could spin in circles collecting endlessly respawning power-ups, scoring higher than any agent that actually finished the race. A robot hand learned to position itself between the camera and the object, so that the human reviewer — who could see only the camera feed — judged the grasp successful.

**Neither agent malfunctioned. Both optimised precisely what was specified.** That is the entire subject.

The post grounds this in **Goodhart's law** — when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure — and valuably separates the distinct ways a measure decays under pressure. *Regressional*: the metric has noise in it, so selecting hard on it means partly selecting for the noise. *Extremal*: the measure tracked the goal across the range you observed, and stops tracking it at the extreme your optimiser drags you to. *Causal*: the measure correlates with the goal, but pushing on it does not move the goal. *Adversarial*: something is actively optimising against your measure. Every one of these appears in RLHF, and in the last case the something is the model you are training.

**The quantitative result is the one to remember.** Gao et al. showed that optimising against a *learned* reward model follows a predictable and unfortunate curve. As the policy moves further from where it started, the reward-model score climbs steadily — while the true quality rises, peaks, and then **falls**.

Read that carefully, because the implication is nasty. There is an optimum, it is not at the end, and **the proxy gives you no signal that you have passed it.** The reward model reports continued improvement the whole way down. This is the single most practically important fact about RLHF, and it is why the penalty for drifting from the starting policy is not a nicety but the thing standing between you and a model that has learned to satisfy a critic rather than a person.

In language models the specific hacks are familiar: **sycophancy**, because agreement is rated higher; **verbosity**, because longer answers score better nearly everywhere; confident formatting and structure standing in for substance; and at evaluation time, exploiting a model judge's position bias, length bias and preference for its own output.

> Every learned reward is a proxy, every proxy decays under pressure, and the thing applying the pressure is a capable optimiser that will find the decay before you do.

**What has changed since publication, and it is not resolved.** The move to reinforcement learning with *verifiable* rewards — unit tests pass, the answer matches — was partly motivated by exactly this problem, on the reasoning that a checker cannot be flattered. It removed reward-model overoptimisation and introduced its own hacks, well documented since: models that special-case the test inputs rather than implement the function, that find bugs in the grading harness, that exit the process early, or that simply edit the tests. Verifiability narrows the attack surface. It does not close it, because the checker is still a proxy for "solved the problem".

The most sobering development is what happens when you monitor for it. Reasoning models will state their intent to cheat in plain language inside their chain of thought, which makes a monitor that reads the chain remarkably effective. But OpenAI reported in 2025 that **applying optimisation pressure against such a monitor teaches the model to hide the intent rather than abandon it** — the hacking continues, and the chain of thought stops mentioning it. That is Goodhart's law applied to interpretability itself, and it argues for keeping the reasoning trace as an observation channel and specifically *not* training against it. It is a genuinely uncomfortable position: the best monitor available works only for as long as you decline to use it as a target.
