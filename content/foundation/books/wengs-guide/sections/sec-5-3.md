**Lilian Weng · 2026 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2026-07-04-harness/)**

The most recent post on the blog, and the one whose timing is worth recording: published in early July 2026, days before she stepped down from Thinking Machines Lab and returned to OpenAI to lead work on recursive self-improvement. It reads as a research agenda, and it turned out to be one.

**The term it defines is "harness"** — the system around a base model that decides how it thinks and plans, calls tools and acts, perceives and manages its context, stores artifacts, and evaluates results. Her framing is that this layer "seems as important as the model's raw intelligence".

That leads to a reframing worth pausing on. If the harness matters that much, then **recursive self-improvement is a far broader idea than models editing their own weights.** Improving the training pipeline is self-improvement. Improving the deployment system is self-improvement. Those are available now, with no new science required.

The **design patterns** are the settled part, and they will be familiar to anyone who has used a coding agent. A goal-oriented loop of plan, execute, observe, improve. **The file system as persistent memory** — durable storage instead of an ever-growing context window, which is the honest answer to the context-length problem [the 2023 agent post](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-1) named and could not solve. Sub-agents and background jobs with explicit, inspectable state. The observation that coding-agent harnesses have converged across Claude Code, Codex and their peers onto a stabilised set of file, shell and delegation tools is a real finding: the interface stopped moving.

The **optimisation ladder** is the post's spine, and each rung is a larger search space than the last: prompts, then structured context, then workflows, then harness code, then the code of the optimiser itself. Agentic Context Engineering treats context as an evolving playbook of bullet points rather than a prompt that just accretes. Meta-Harness has coding agents rewrite harness code. Evolutionary methods — AlphaEvolve and its relatives, the Darwin Gödel Machine — search over programs, which as [chapter 2](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-2-4) argued is the natural home for evolution strategies now that the objects being optimised are discrete and no gradient exists for them. Her justification is the load-bearing one: **"code is a universal language for defining programs and systems"**, so representing a harness as code makes the search space enormous and machine-explorable at the same time.

> Optimising the model was always the expensive half; optimising the code around the model is cheap, differentiable by search rather than by gradient, and had barely been attempted.

**The seven bottlenecks are why this is a good post rather than an exciting one**, and they should be read as the summary. Weak or fuzzy evaluators for research-quality work. Context and memory lifecycle management. A bias toward suppressing negative results. Diversity collapse in evolutionary loops. Reward hacking. Short-term optimisation that diverges from long-term goals. Insufficient integration of human oversight.

**Read the first and the fifth together and the difficulty is plain.** A self-improving harness is a loop that optimises against an evaluator — and the first bottleneck says the evaluators are weak. That is precisely the configuration [reward hacking](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-4-1) describes, lifted from the level of a single policy to the level of an entire system. Gao et al.'s curve, where the proxy keeps improving while the real objective has already turned over, has no reason to behave differently because the thing being optimised is now Python instead of weights. And the fourth bottleneck is the same [entropy collapse](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-1-4) that has followed reinforcement learning since the bandit chapter, arriving in a population instead of a policy.

**The caveat to hold while reading:** this is a survey of very new work, and a large fraction of the systems it cites are single-paper results with no independent replication, evaluated on benchmarks their own authors chose. The design patterns are real because millions of people use them daily. The self-improvement results are promising and provisional, and the post is more careful about that distinction than its summaries have been. Her closing position — that humans should move up the stack and oversee at a higher level of abstraction rather than being removed — is the appropriately modest conclusion, and it depends entirely on the seventh bottleneck being taken as seriously as the first.
