**Lilian Weng · 2018 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2018-02-19-rl-overview/)**

This is the post that sets the notation for everything after it, and reading the blog out of order without it means re-learning the same symbols four times. It is also the closest thing on Lil'Log to a textbook chapter.

**The setup, in plain terms first.** Reinforcement learning is the study of an agent that acts, gets feedback, and has to work out which of its actions were responsible. An agent in situation `s` takes action `a`, receives a reward `r`, and ends up in a new situation `s'`.

A **policy** is its rule for choosing what to do. A **value function** `V(s)` says how much total reward it expects to collect from a situation onward, counting later rewards for slightly less than immediate ones — that discounting is what keeps an infinite future from summing to infinity. The **action-value** `Q(s,a)` says the same for a situation *and* a chosen action.

The **Bellman equations** are the piece that makes this tractable. They express each value in terms of the values that follow it — the worth of a situation is the reward you get now plus the discounted worth of where you land next. That turns an infinite sum into something you can actually solve, by finding values that are consistent with themselves.

From there, three approaches, distinguished entirely by what they assume you know.

**Dynamic programming** assumes you know the rules of the world in advance — what every action leads to and what it pays. Policy iteration alternates between evaluating the current policy and improving it; value iteration collapses the two into one step. Both are exact, and both require the model of the world, which you almost never have.

**Monte Carlo** assumes nothing and simply waits. Play an episode to the end, see what you actually got, average over many episodes. Correct on average, and unusable when episodes are long or never end.

**Temporal difference** splits the difference, and it is the idea worth carrying. Update after a single step, using your own current estimate of what comes next: nudge `V(s)` toward `r + gamma*V(s')`. This is **bootstrapping** — learning a guess from a guess — and it is what lets you learn as you go, without waiting to see how things turn out.

SARSA does this *on-policy*, updating toward the action actually taken. **Q-learning** does it *off-policy*, updating toward the best action available whether or not the agent took it — which is why it can learn from data generated by any behaviour at all, including someone else's. DQN then makes Q-learning work with a neural network, via two stabilisers the post explains well: **experience replay**, which stores past experiences and samples them at random so consecutive updates are not all about the same moment, and a **frozen target network**, which stops the thing you are aiming at from moving every single step.

> Bootstrapping is what makes reinforcement learning possible online and what makes it unstable: every update is fitted against an estimate that is itself still wrong.

**Where this stops applying, which is the thing to know before reading the rest of the guide.** Almost all of this machinery exists to handle **credit assignment over time** — the reward arrives many steps after the action that earned it, so you need discounting, bootstrapping, value functions and eligibility traces to route it backwards.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback, the subject of [chapter 3](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-1) and most of what RL now means in practice, has almost none of that structure. The model emits one response, a reward model scores it, and the episode is over. There is no state transition, no future to discount, nothing to bootstrap from. **It is a bandit problem** — the subject of [1.3](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-1-3) — dressed in the vocabulary of the full one.

That mismatch explains a great deal of what happened next: why PPO's critic contributes so little in RLHF, why [DPO](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-3) could eliminate the reinforcement learning entirely, and why 2024's simplifications — GRPO removing the value network, REINFORCE-style methods returning — were corrections rather than losses. The post is a faithful account of reinforcement learning as it stood in 2018. Read it for the vocabulary and the Bellman equations, both of which are permanent, and hold the temporal machinery loosely if language models are why you are here. It becomes load-bearing again the moment an agent takes more than one action, which is exactly where [chapter 5](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-1) goes.
