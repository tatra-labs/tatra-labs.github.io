**Lilian Weng · 2018 · [Lil'Log](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2018-02-19-rl-overview/)**

This is the post that sets the notation for everything after it, and reading the blog out of order without it means re-learning symbols four times. It is also the closest thing on Lil'Log to a textbook chapter: the Markov decision process, and the three families of algorithm that solve it.

The setup is standard and stated cleanly. An agent in state `s` takes action `a`, receives reward `r`, and transitions to `s'` according to a transition function. A **policy** maps states to actions. A **value function** `V(s)` gives the expected discounted return from a state; the **action-value** `Q(s,a)` gives it from a state-action pair. The Bellman equations express each in terms of its own successors — `V(s)` is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you land — which is what makes the whole thing computable, because it converts an infinite sum into a fixed point.

From there, three approaches distinguished by what they assume.

**Dynamic programming** assumes you know the transition and reward functions. Policy iteration alternates evaluating the current policy against improving it greedily; value iteration collapses the two. Both are exact and both require the model, which you almost never have.

**Monte Carlo** assumes nothing and waits. Play an episode to the end, observe the actual return, average over episodes. Unbiased, and unusable when episodes are long or unbounded.

**Temporal difference** splits the difference and is the idea worth carrying. Update from a single step using your own current estimate of what comes next: `V(s)` moves toward `r + gamma*V(s')`. This is **bootstrapping** — learning a guess from a guess — and it lets you learn online, without waiting for an outcome. SARSA does this on-policy, updating toward the action actually taken. **Q-learning** does it off-policy, updating toward the best action available whether or not the agent took it, which is why it can learn from data generated by any behaviour. DQN then makes Q-learning work with a neural network via two stabilisers that the post explains well: **experience replay**, which breaks the correlation between consecutive samples, and a **frozen target network**, which stops the regression target moving every step.

> Bootstrapping is what makes reinforcement learning possible online and what makes it unstable: every update is fitted against an estimate that is itself still wrong.

**Where this stops applying, which is the thing to know before reading the rest of the guide.** Almost all of this machinery exists to handle **credit assignment over time** — the reward arrives many steps after the action that earned it, so you need discounting, bootstrapping, value functions and eligibility traces to route it backwards. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, the subject of [chapter 3](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-3-1) and most of what RL now means in practice, has almost none of that structure. The model emits one response, a reward model scores it, and the episode ends. There is no state transition, no future to discount, no bootstrapping. **It is a contextual bandit**, which is the subject of [1.3](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-1-3), dressed in the vocabulary of the full problem.

That mismatch explains a great deal of what happened next: why PPO's critic contributes so little in RLHF, why [DPO](/foundation/book/karpathys-list?section=sec-4-3) could eliminate the reinforcement learning entirely, and why 2024's simplifications — GRPO removing the value network, REINFORCE-style methods returning — were corrections rather than losses. The post is a faithful account of reinforcement learning as it stood in 2018. Read it for the vocabulary and the Bellman equations, both of which are permanent, and hold the temporal machinery loosely if language models are why you are here. It becomes load-bearing again the moment an agent takes more than one action, which is exactly where [chapter 5](/foundation/book/wengs-guide?section=sec-5-1) goes.
